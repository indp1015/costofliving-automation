📊 Local Market Analysis

Garland County’s cost of living sits well below the national average, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 86.8 versus 100 for the United States. Prices for goods—groceries, apparel, and fuel—register at 93.1, still modest but the highest component of the local price basket. Services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are markedly inexpensive, at 56.7, while rental costs for housing are 81.9, indicating that landlords charge roughly 18 % less than the national norm. The median household income of $55,409 trails the national median of $65,047 by 15 %, yet the rent burden remains modest at 20.4 % of income, well under the 30 % threshold that defines affordability. The dominant driver of Garland County’s price structure is the goods sector, which, although still below national levels, lifts the overall cost profile relative to the very low service index. Daily life is therefore inexpensive in terms of utilities, medical care, and restaurant meals, but grocery bills and fuel expenses are comparatively higher, narrowing the gap between local and national household budgets. With a median monthly rent of $942—just 1.5 % above the national median—the housing component does not offset the savings realized in services, leaving a net cost environment that is affordable for most residents, especially those with stable employment. Disposable income can be gauged through the Purchasing Power Index of 63,827.9, which translates to roughly $64 k of purchasing power per household when adjusted for local price levels. Subtracting the median rent of $942 leaves about $3,500 per month for all other expenses; after accounting for the goods index, a typical household retains roughly 70 % of its nominal income for discretionary spending. This residual purchasing power is bolstered by the county’s relatively low service costs, allowing residents to stretch their budgets further than the national average despite lower earnings. Demographically, the county’s median age of 45.3 years and a bachelor's‑degree attainment rate of 16.6 % suggest a mature, less‑educated labor force, which aligns with the 5.9 % unemployment rate—slightly above the national average but not prohibitive. The older age profile reduces demand for child‑related services, reinforcing the low service price index, while limited higher‑education attainment may constrain wage growth, explaining why income has risen 43 % since 2010 but still lags behind national gains. Over the past decade, the RPP fell from 88.6 to 86.8, a 2 % decline, indicating that consumer prices have softened relative to the nation. Median household income surged 43 % from $38,733 to $55,409, outpacing the 32 % rise in median rent, which climbed from $712 to $942. The faster income growth relative to housing costs suggests that wages have kept pace with, and even exceeded, expense inflation, enhancing real purchasing power for residents. Prospective residents should allocate a larger share of their budget to groceries and fuel, anticipate modest utility and healthcare bills, and expect rent to consume roughly one‑fifth of gross earnings. With a median disposable income of about $3,500 per month after rent, a prudent budget would earmark roughly $1,200 for food and transportation, $800 for utilities and medical expenses, and the remainder for savings or discretionary items. This allocation reflects the county’s price composition and ensures that households can maintain a comfortable standard of living without exceeding the 30 % rent‑burden threshold.

What this means for residents of Garland County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 63827.9, a median household income of $55,409 in Garland County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $35,366,401 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
