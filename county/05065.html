📊 Local Market Analysis

Izard County’s cost of living sits well below the national norm, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 86.8 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices—groceries, apparel, and fuel—register an index of 93.1, still under the national average but the highest component locally. Services, encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining, are markedly cheaper at 56.7, while rental costs are modest at 81.9. The median household income of $47,728 trails the national median of $65,047 by 26.6%, yet the gap narrows when housing costs are considered: median rent of $626 is 32.5% lower than the national median of $928, and rent consumes only 15.7% of local income, well under the often‑cited 30% affordability threshold. The expense profile is driven primarily by goods, which lift the overall index despite the pronounced service discount. Daily life therefore feels inexpensive in categories that dominate household budgets—energy, medical care, and restaurant meals—while grocery bills remain relatively higher, reflecting the county’s reliance on transportation‑intensive supply chains. Housing costs, though not negligible, are sufficiently low to leave room for discretionary spending after rent, but the modest goods index tempers that advantage. Purchasing power, measured by the index of 54,979.8, indicates that a median earner retains roughly 55% of the buying capacity of a national counterpart after accounting for local price levels. Subtracting the median rent of $626 from monthly income ($3,977) leaves $3,351 for all other expenses; applying the service index (56.7) suggests that typical utility and healthcare outlays cost about 57% of what they would nationally, further stretching disposable income. However, the higher goods index erodes part of that gain, leaving a net disposable share that is modest but not precarious. Demographically, the county’s median age of 47.5 years and a bachelor's‑degree attainment rate of 12.8% point to a mature, less‑educated labor force. The unemployment rate of 7.0% exceeds the national average, implying limited wage growth pressure and reinforcing the low‑cost environment. A workforce concentrated in lower‑skill occupations tends to prioritize essential spending, which aligns with the county’s cheaper services and housing but also limits capacity to absorb higher goods prices. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped from 88.56 to 86.81, a 2.0% decline, while median household income surged 46.3% from $32,630 to $47,728. Rental rates rose 23.2% in the same period, outpacing the modest increase in the overall price index. Income growth has outstripped both inflation and rent, suggesting that wages have generally kept pace with, and even surpassed, rising expenses. Prospective residents should budget a larger share of income for groceries and fuel, anticipate low utility and healthcare bills, and allocate roughly 15‑16% of earnings to rent. With a median disposable income cushion of about $3,350 per month after housing, households can comfortably cover essential goods and services, but limited local wage premiums and higher goods costs warrant careful monitoring of grocery and transportation expenditures.

What this means for residents of Izard County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 54979.8, a median household income of $47,728 in Izard County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $26,240,759 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
