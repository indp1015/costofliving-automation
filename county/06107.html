📊 Local Market Analysis

Tularemia County’s cost of living sits well above the national baseline, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) indicating an overall index of 112.2 versus 100 for the United States. Goods are modestly pricier at 106.8, but services surge to 157.8, reflecting a 57.8% premium on health care, utilities, and dining relative to the country average. Housing, measured by rental costs, registers a 147.3 index—nearly a 48% surcharge over national levels. The median monthly rent of $1,206 further underscores the housing premium, outpacing the national median rent of $928 by 30%. The service sector dominates the local cost structure. While groceries and apparel remain only slightly above average, the 157.8 service index drives the bulk of the expense burden. Daily life in Tularemia County is therefore expensive, particularly for households that rely heavily on out‑of‑home consumption or incur high utility bills. The rental market compounds this pressure; at a 147.3 index, renters face a cost that is roughly one‑and‑a‑half times the national norm. Consequently, a typical household must allocate a larger share of income to non‑essential expenditures, leaving less room for discretionary spending. Disposable income calculations reveal a constrained purchasing power. The median household income of $69,489 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 6.9%, yet the Purchasing Power Index—$61,935.9—lags behind the national average, indicating that after accounting for the elevated price levels, residents retain roughly 89% of the purchasing power of an average American household. Rent consumes 20.8% of median income, well below the conventional 30% affordability threshold, but the high service costs erode the remaining budget. After deducting the median rent, a household retains $58,283 annually; subtracting an estimated 15% of income for services (≈$10,423) leaves roughly $47,860 for all other expenses, a figure that translates into tighter margins for savings or leisure. Demographically, the county’s labor market intensifies the cost experience. An unemployment rate of 8.8%—significantly higher than the national rate—limits earnings potential and heightens income volatility. Only 10.1% of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting a workforce concentrated in lower‑skill, lower‑wage occupations, which aligns with the modest income growth. The median age of 31.9 years points to a relatively young population, often early in their career trajectories and more likely to rent, thereby exposing them directly to the inflated rental and service costs. Over the past decade, RPP rose from 109.7 in 2010 to 112.2 in 2023, a modest 2.3% increase. In the same period, median household income surged 65.3% from $42,031 to $69,489, while median rent climbed 45.3% from $830 to $1,206. Income growth outpaced both overall price inflation and rental inflation, yet the disproportionate rise in service costs—reflected in the 57.8% premium—means that wage gains have not fully translated into higher real purchasing power. The gap between income and expense growth underscores a lingering affordability strain, especially for households heavily dependent on services. Prospective residents should budget with a clear hierarchy: allocate roughly 21% of gross income to rent, anticipate that services will consume an additional 15% to 20%, and plan for goods expenditures at near‑national levels. With a median disposable income of about $48 k after rent and services, a realistic monthly budget for groceries, transportation, and modest entertainment would hover around $2,500. Savings capacity will be limited unless households can reduce service usage or secure higher‑paying employment. Understanding these cost dynamics is essential for anyone evaluating the affordability of daily life in Tularemia County.

What this means for residents of Tulare County, California
