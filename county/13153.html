📊 Local Market Analysis

Houston County residents navigate a cost environment that sits 3.5 percent below the national benchmark, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 96.5 in 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals distinct pressure points for household budgets. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — represent the highest local cost component at 97.7, nearly converging with the national average and leaving little margin for savings on daily necessities. Conversely, services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining out carry a substantial 11.7 percent discount at 88.3, while rental costs sit 7.7 percent below the national level at 92.3. This structure creates a bifurcated experience: recurring service obligations are markedly affordable, but the unavoidable weekly expenditure on food and fuel tracks closely with metropolitan pricing, limiting the practical relief a sub-100 index might suggest. The median household income of $80,743 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 24.1 percent, translating into a Purchasing Power Index of 83,677 that signals strong real wage retention after local price adjustments. Rent burden stands at a remarkably low 17.0 percent of gross income, with the median monthly rent of $1,143 consuming a far smaller share of earnings than the national median rent of $928 does relative to national income. However, the 5.0 percent unemployment rate sits above typical full-employment thresholds, and an educational attainment rate of just 18.9 percent for bachelor’s degrees or higher suggests a labor market weighted toward sectors where wage growth may not sustain its recent trajectory. The median age of 36.2 years indicates a workforce in its prime earning years, yet the demographic profile implies vulnerability should the local employment base contract. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the RPP drifted upward from 94.6 to 96.5, a modest 2.0 percent increase in the cost level that pales against the 51.6 percent surge in median income from $53,270 to $80,743. Median rent advanced 34.5 percent over the same span, climbing from $850 to $1,143, a pace significantly slower than income growth but faster than the broader inflation index. This decade-long divergence has expanded the real income cushion considerably, though the acceleration in goods pricing — now at 97.7 — hints that the easiest gains in purchasing power may have already been captured. The widening gap between service affordability and goods parity suggests future cost-of-living pressure will likely concentrate in the non-discretionary basket. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that mirror national city averages rather than the deep discounts found in housing and utilities. The 17 percent rent-to-income ratio provides exceptional shelter affordability, freeing cash flow for transportation, childcare, or debt service, but the goods index of 97.7 means a family of four will see limited savings at the checkout counter compared to higher-cost metros. With services running 11.7 percent cheaper, healthcare premiums and utility deposits should underwrite favorably, yet the local unemployment rate of 5.0 percent warrants maintaining a larger liquid reserve than the low rent burden alone might justify. The data supports a household strategy that leverages cheap housing and services to absorb the near-national pricing of everyday consumables.

What this means for residents of Houston County, Georgia

With a Purchasing Power Index of 83676.7, a median household income of $80,743 in Houston County, Georgia has the equivalent buying power of $67,563,078 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 92.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
