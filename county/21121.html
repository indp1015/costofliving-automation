📊 Local Market Analysis

Knox County residents navigate a cost structure that sits 10.1 percent below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 89.9 in 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals sharp asymmetries across spending categories. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel — command an index of 94.5, only marginally cheaper than the U.S. baseline, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 62.9, the lowest component and the primary driver of the county’s affordability profile. Rental costs settle at 80.9, offering meaningful shelter savings relative to the national median rent of $928, with local median rent resting at $631. This bifurcation means daily essentials like food and gasoline absorb a disproportionate share of household budgets compared to service-intensive expenditures, a dynamic that distinguishes the local consumer experience from broader low-cost metros where goods and services typically move in tandem. The median household income of $33,153 translates into a purchasing power index of 36,881.7, effectively granting residents a 11.2 percent boost in real terms once local prices are accounted for, though the nominal gap against the national median income of $65,047 remains substantial. Rent burden sits at a manageable 22.8 percent of gross income, well below the 30 percent stress threshold, leaving theoretical latitude for other outlays. However, an unemployment rate of 7.9 percent — significantly above national norms — paired with a bachelor’s degree attainment rate of just 7.0 percent and a median age of 39.8 years, signals a labor market concentrated in lower-wage, service-oriented sectors. This demographic backdrop suggests the modest rent burden may mask volatility; a single job loss or reduction in hours can quickly erode the buffer provided by low service costs, particularly when goods prices track near national levels. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the RPP drifted downward only 0.6 percent, from 90.4 to 89.9, indicating remarkable price stability in the aggregate. Meanwhile, nominal median income rose 24.6 percent, from $26,599 to $33,153, outpacing the 15.8 percent increase in median rent, which climbed from $545 to $631. This divergence implies a genuine improvement in real housing affordability over the decade, a rare trajectory in many comparable rural economies. Yet the near-flat RPP suggests the purchasing power gains stem almost entirely from income growth rather than deflationary pressure, leaving households exposed should wage growth stall while goods prices — already at 94.5 — accelerate. A prospective resident should budget for a grocery and fuel bill that feels nearly identical to suburban America, offset by deeply discounted healthcare, utility, and restaurant tabs. The $631 median rent anchors a housing budget that consumes roughly $7,572 annually, but the 94.5 goods index implies a family spending $800 monthly on food and transportation nationally would still spend roughly $756 locally — a savings of only $528 per year. Conversely, the 62.9 services index could slash a $400 monthly utility and healthcare bill to roughly $252, yielding nearly $1,800 in annual relief. The critical budgeting variable remains income stability; with unemployment near 8 percent and educational attainment low, the household ledger depends less on the static cost structure and entirely on the reliability of the paycheck funding it.

What this means for residents of Knox County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 36881.7, a median household income of $33,153 in Knox County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $12,227,390 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
