📊 Local Market Analysis

Lee County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 11.2 percentage points below the national baseline, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 88.8 in 2023, yet the composition of that advantage reveals a stark bifurcation between physical goods and local services. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — command an index of 96.6, nearly converging with national pricing, while services including healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 66.0, the lowest component by a wide margin and the primary engine suppressing the aggregate figure. Rental costs, measured at 87.3, track closer to the goods category than to services, meaning the typical household experiences a daily financial reality where filling a prescription or hiring a tradesperson costs dramatically less than in metropolitan centers, but the weekly grocery bill and commute fuel expenses offer scant discount. The median household income of $59,803 trails the national median of $65,047 by 8.1 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 67,369.2 indicates that after adjusting for local price levels, the typical earner commands roughly 3.6 percent more real consumption capacity than the nominal gap suggests. Rent burden stands at a remarkably low 16.3 percent of gross income — the median monthly outlay of $814 compares favorably against the national median of $928 — freeing discretionary cash flow for savings or goods consumption where price parity is highest. Demographic context sharpens this picture: a median age of 43.7 years and a bachelor's attainment rate of just 15.6 percent signal a labor force concentrated in skilled trades, healthcare support, and manufacturing rather than knowledge-economy roles, while a 4.0 percent unemployment rate suggests a tight but not overheated market where wage pressure remains modest. Over the thirteen-year span from 2010 to 2023, the RPP declined 2.6 percent from 91.139 to 88.769, a rare deflationary drift in local price levels that coincided with nominal median income surging 38.1 percent from $43,312 to $59,803. Median rent rose 41.1 percent over the same period, from $577 to $814, slightly outpacing income growth and nudging the rent-to-income ratio upward, though the absolute burden remains well below the 30 percent stress threshold. The divergence — wages growing faster than the overall cost index but slower than the shelter component — means households have gained ground on services and goods affordability while allocating a marginally larger share of each paycheck to housing. A prospective resident should budget for a grocery and fuel bill that mirrors national averages almost dollar-for-dollar, while anticipating healthcare premiums, utility deposits, and restaurant meals that can run 30 to 35 percent cheaper than in a typical U.S. metro. The rental market delivers genuine value: a two-bedroom unit at the median consumes roughly one-sixth of pre-tax income, leaving room to absorb the goods-category parity or to build reserves. However, the low educational attainment and aging workforce imply limited upward wage mobility in the near term, so any household budget should treat the current purchasing power advantage as a structural feature of the local economy rather than a temporary windfall.

What this means for residents of Lee County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 67369.2, a median household income of $59,803 in Lee County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $40,288,803 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
