📊 Local Market Analysis

Pueblo County presents a cost-of-living profile that sits marginally above the national baseline, registering a 2023 Regional Price Parity of 101.9, yet this aggregate figure masks a severe structural distortion in local consumer economics. The overall index is inflated almost entirely by a Services component—encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining—that clocks in at 130.5, a full 30.5 percentage points above the national average and the highest cost driver in the basket. Conversely, daily essentials captured in the Goods index (groceries, apparel, fuel) run slightly below par at 99.2, while Rental Costs offer significant relief at 86.8, roughly 13 percent cheaper than the U.S. norm. This bifurcation creates a distinct "barbell" economy: a household can stock a pantry and fuel a vehicle affordably, but the moment they require medical care, utility hookups, or restaurant meals, the discount vanishes and the premium asserts itself. Purchasing power metrics confirm that local wages have not fully closed the gap created by that services premium. The median household income of $62,250 trails the national median of $65,047 by 4.3 percent, and when adjusted for the local price level, the Purchasing Power Index settles at $61,115.70—effectively a $1,134 annual haircut relative to nominal earnings. Rent burden sits at a manageable 20.4 percent of gross income based on a median monthly rent of $1,059, which compares favorably against the national median rent of $928 only because local incomes are lower; nationally, that $928 rent would consume a smaller share of the higher $65,047 median income. The disposable income cushion is therefore thinner than the raw rent-to-income ratio suggests, leaving less margin for the elevated services costs that define the local budget. Demographic and labor market indicators sharpen the picture of constrained flexibility. An unemployment rate of 5.4 percent exceeds typical full-employment thresholds, signaling slack in the labor market that likely suppresses wage growth, while an educational attainment rate of just 16.2 percent for bachelor’s degrees or higher suggests a workforce concentrated in sectors with limited pricing power. The median age of 40.4 years implies a population with established household formation patterns and likely higher healthcare utilization, directly exposing the typical resident to the 130.5 Services index. This combination of an older, less credentialed workforce facing above-average joblessness creates a feedback loop where the primary cost driver—services—is least amenable to consumer substitution or negotiation. Over the 2010–2023 window, the RPP drifted up a modest 1.3 percent from 100.59 to 101.86, suggesting broad price stability, but nominal income growth of 50.8 percent ($41,286 to $62,250) significantly outpaced the 36.8 percent rise in median rent ($774 to $1,059). While this divergence improved the rent-to-income trajectory on paper, the static nature of the Services RPP near 130 implies that the real purchasing power gains from wage growth have been partially absorbed by non-housing inflation. A prospective resident budgeting for daily life should anchor expectations on cheap shelter and groceries but allocate a disproportionate share of monthly cash flow—potentially 15 to 20 percent above national norms—to health insurance premiums, utility deposits, and outsourced household services, as these line items carry the true weight of the local cost structure.

What this means for residents of Pueblo County, Colorado

With a Purchasing Power Index of 61115.7, a median household income of $62,250 in Pueblo County, Colorado has the equivalent buying power of $38,044,523 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 86.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
