📊 Local Market Analysis

Pima County presents a cost of living profile that sits marginally above the national baseline, registering a 2023 Regional Price Parity of 100.7, yet this aggregate figure masks a pronounced structural divergence between what residents pay for shelter versus what they pay to maintain daily life. Housing costs represent the county’s primary competitive advantage, with rental expenses clocking in at 83.3 on the RPP index—roughly 17 percent below the national average—and a median monthly rent of $1,154 that consumes only 20.4 percent of the median household income of $67,929. Conversely, the services category, encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining, runs significantly hot at 108.6, pushing the overall index into positive territory despite goods pricing at a modest 97.9. This inversion creates a distinctive budgetary environment where the largest fixed expense—shelter—is deeply discounted, but the variable, recurring costs of daily consumption carry a measurable premium. The purchasing power calculus reveals a net gain for the median earner. Adjusting the $67,929 median income by the 100.7 RPP yields a real purchasing power index of approximately $67,429, effectively preserving the nominal income advantage over the national median of $65,047. The rent burden metric of 20.4 percent underscores this dynamic; it sits well below the standard 30 percent affordability threshold and compares favorably against the national median rent of $928, which, while lower in absolute terms, often commands a higher share of income in tighter coastal markets. However, the 108.6 services index implies that discretionary spending on healthcare, childcare, and energy erodes that shelter savings faster than the headline RPP suggests, requiring households to allocate a larger slice of post-rent income to non-negotiable service consumption. Local labor market characteristics amplify this tension. An unemployment rate of 5.7 percent exceeds the national norm, signaling slack in the employment market that may suppress wage growth relative to tighter metros, while an educational attainment rate of 20.4 percent for bachelor’s degrees or higher trails the national average, correlating with the region’s concentration in service-sector and public-sector employment rather than high-wage knowledge industries. The median age of 39.7 years indicates a workforce in its prime earning years, yet the decade-long trend data exposes a critical nuance: median income surged 47.2 percent from $46,162 to $67,929 between 2010 and 2023, outpacing the 41.4 percent rise in median rent from $816 to $1,154. While wages technically won the race, the RPP barely budged, inching from 100.558 to 100.742, suggesting that non-housing inflation—specifically in services—absorbed much of the real income gains. For a prospective resident, the budgeting imperative centers on the services-housing trade-off. The low rental burden—roughly $1,154 monthly—frees immediate cash flow, but the elevated services index demands a larger contingency for healthcare premiums, utility volatility in a desert climate, and dining costs that exceed national norms. A household relocating from a high-cost metro will likely experience an immediate standard-of-living lift via housing savings, yet the persistence of above-average service costs means the "real" raise shrinks once essential non-rent obligations are met. The data suggests the county remains a net positive for purchasing power, but the margin is thinner than the shelter discount alone implies, demanding rigorous line-item budgeting for recurring service expenses rather than fixation on the lease payment.

What this means for residents of Pima County, Arizona

With a Purchasing Power Index of 67428.7, a median household income of $67,929 in Pima County, Arizona has the equivalent buying power of $45,803,642 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 83.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
