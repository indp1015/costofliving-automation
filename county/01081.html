📊 Local Market Analysis

Lee County’s cost‑of‑living index sits at 89.1, meaning consumer prices are roughly 11 % below the national average of 100. Goods prices—groceries, apparel and fuel—register an index of 94.6, only 5 % under the national level, while services such as healthcare, utilities and dining are markedly cheaper at 61.6, a 38 % discount. Rental costs, measured by the BEA’s housing component, are 89.9, essentially on par with the overall index and 10 % lower than the national norm. The composite picture shows a market where everyday necessities are modestly priced, but the most pronounced savings stem from services rather than goods. The dominant driver of Lee County’s price structure is the services sector, whose index of 61.6 pulls the overall cost down far more than the relatively higher goods index. Daily life therefore feels inexpensive for activities tied to health, utilities and eating out, while grocery bills and fuel expenditures remain only slightly below national levels. Median monthly rent of $999 exceeds the national median of $928 by 7.6 %, yet the rent‑burden ratio of 19.6 % of household income indicates that housing remains affordable for most residents; the national benchmark for rent burden is typically cited around 30 %. After deducting the average rent, a median household earning $61,123 retains $53,124 annually, or $4,427 per month, for all other expenses—a disposable income that aligns with the Purchasing Power Index of 68,603, roughly 68 % of the national benchmark. Demographically, the county’s labor force is youthful (median age 33.6) and moderately educated, with 22.6 % holding a bachelor’s degree or higher. Unemployment sits at 3.7 %, well under the national rate, suggesting a stable job market that supports the median income of $61,123—about 6 % below the national median of $65,047. The combination of low unemployment and a younger population tends to keep demand for services high, reinforcing the low service price index as providers compete for a price‑sensitive clientele. Meanwhile, the modest educational attainment may limit access to higher‑paying occupations, tempering income growth. Over the past decade, the Regional Price Parities index slipped from 89.78 to 89.10, a 0.8 % decline, indicating that overall price pressure has eased slightly. Median household income, however, surged 37.1 % from $44,570 to $61,123, outpacing the 26.1 % rise in median rent ($792 to $999). The disparity between income growth and rent inflation suggests that wages have more than kept pace with housing costs, enhancing real purchasing power. Nonetheless, the slower decline in goods prices relative to services implies that future budget pressures will likely hinge on grocery and fuel expenditures rather than on service costs. Prospective residents should allocate a larger share of their budget to goods—particularly food and transportation—while expecting modest utility and healthcare bills. With rent consuming just under one‑fifth of income, the remaining 80 % can be distributed across groceries, fuel, and discretionary spending, leaving an estimated $4,400 per month for non‑housing costs. Budget planners would do well to monitor fuel price volatility, as it represents the primary source of cost variance in an otherwise affordable cost‑of‑living environment.

What this means for residents of Lee County, Alabama

With a Purchasing Power Index of 68602.8, a median household income of $61,123 in Lee County, Alabama has the equivalent buying power of $41,932,089 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 89.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
