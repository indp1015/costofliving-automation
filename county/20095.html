📊 Local Market Analysis

Kingman County residents navigate a cost environment sitting 10.1 percentage points below the national average, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity (RPP) for 2023 registering 89.9 against a baseline of 100. This overall discount masks significant internal divergence: the Goods category—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—runs only 3.5 percent below the national level at 96.5, while Services, covering healthcare, utilities, and dining, plunges to 68.6, a staggering 31.4 percent discount. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 90.8. The data reveals that daily life is structurally inexpensive primarily because service-intensive expenditures, which typically consume a disproportionate share of household budgets in metro areas, are deeply discounted here, whereas the unavoidable, frequent outlays for goods track much closer to national norms. The consumption breakdown indicates that the Goods category effectively drives the local cost structure upward, preventing the overall index from falling further toward the Services floor. A median household income of $59,819 translates into a Purchasing Power Index of 66,533.6, meaning the typical family commands roughly $6,700 more in real goods and services than their nominal earnings suggest. This surplus stems directly from the low Services RPP and a rent burden of just 16.2 percent—median rent of $807 consumes a far smaller slice of income than the national median of $928 against a $65,047 income. However, the 16.8 percent bachelor’s degree attainment rate and a median age of 43.4 years signal a workforce skewed toward established, non-degreed occupations, while the 3.7 percent unemployment rate suggests a tight labor market that may support wage stability but limits upward mobility for those without specialized credentials. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the RPP declined 3.3 percent from 92.991 to 89.908, indicating that local consumer prices fell relative to the national basket even as nominal median income rose 17.7 percent from $50,844 to $59,819. The critical friction point appears in housing: median rent surged 29.1 percent from $625 to $807, outpacing income growth by a significant margin. While the overall purchasing power trajectory remains positive due to the collapsing Services index, the accelerating rental trajectory erodes the affordability advantage for new entrants or renters renewing leases, compressing the discretionary buffer that the low Goods and Services costs otherwise provide. A prospective resident budgeting for daily household expenses should anchor expectations to the reality that grocery and fuel bills will resemble national averages, while utility, healthcare, and dining-out costs will feel substantially cheaper. The primary budget risk sits in the rental market, where costs have risen nearly 30 percent in thirteen years, a pace that, if sustained, will gradually close the gap between local purchasing power and the national benchmark. Current renters benefit from a 16.2 percent rent-to-income ratio that leaves ample room for savings or debt service, but that cushion thins quickly if rental inflation reverts to its recent trend while wage growth moderates in a county where educational attainment limits high-wage sector density.

What this means for residents of Kingman County, Kansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 66533.6, a median household income of $59,819 in Kingman County, Kansas has the equivalent buying power of $39,799,734 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 90.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
