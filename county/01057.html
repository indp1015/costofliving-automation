📊 Local Market Analysis

Fayette County, Alabama, exhibits a cost of living that is modestly below the national benchmark, with an overall price‑index of 89.1 relative to the national average of 100. The local goods component registers at 94.6, indicating consumer prices for groceries, apparel and fuel are slightly higher than the national norm, while services—encompassing healthcare, utilities and dining—stand at 61.6, well under the national average. Housing costs, measured by rental rates, sit at 89.9, essentially matching the national level. Median household income of $50,733 falls short of the national median of $65,047, and median rent of $622 is markedly lower than the national median rent of $928, shaping a distinct affordability profile. The consumption breakdown reveals that goods are the primary driver of the local cost structure, scoring 94.6 versus the national baseline of 100. Services, at 61.6, are the cheapest component, suggesting that everyday expenses such as utilities and dining are comparatively inexpensive. Housing, while near the national index, remains a moderate expense, especially when viewed against the median rent of $622. Consequently, daily life in Fayette County is driven more by the relatively higher cost of goods than by housing or services, making routine purchases the most significant budgetary pressure for households. Purchasing power analysis shows that the median household retains a substantial portion of income after essential costs. Rent consumes 14.7% of the median income, well within the conventional affordability threshold, and the purchasing power index of 56,941.3 reflects the real purchasing capacity of local residents after accounting for price variations. When compared with national benchmarks, the lower rent burden and the sub‑national cost of services enhance disposable income, allowing households to allocate more toward discretionary items despite a lower overall median income. Local demographics further contextualize the cost environment. An unemployment rate of 9.5% exceeds the national average, which can suppress wage growth and increase financial uncertainty for some residents. Educational attainment is low, with only 9.9% of adults holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, potentially limiting higher‑paying employment opportunities. The median age of 42.8 suggests a mature workforce, yet the combination of limited educational credentials and elevated unemployment may constrain income growth and influence the demand for lower‑cost services. Trend analysis over the past decade indicates modest shifts in both costs and incomes. The regional price‑parity index declined from 89.783 in 2010 to 89.097 in 2023, a marginal -0.8% change, while median household income rose sharply from $34,680 to $50,733, a +46.3% increase. Median rent increased from $469 to $622, a +32.6% rise, outpacing the overall price‑index decline. Wages have risen faster than the price‑index, but rent growth has been notable, suggesting that housing affordability, while still favorable, is tightening relative to income gains. For a prospective resident, budgeting should prioritize the higher relative cost of goods, which consistently rank above the national average. While housing remains affordable—rent at $622 representing a modest 14.7% of median income—utilities and dining, being the cheapest components, require minimal allocation. Savings can be directed toward discretionary spending, but households should monitor employment stability given the elevated unemployment rate and limited educational attainment in the area.
