📊 Local Market Analysis

Marion County residents operate within a cost structure that sits marginally below the national baseline, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 98.8 in 2023, yet the composition of that index reveals distinct pressure points for household budgets. Goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—represent the sole category exceeding the national average at 101.6, creating a persistent drag on discretionary spending every time a resident visits a supermarket or fills a tank. Conversely, services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining come in at a substantial discount of 92.4, while rental housing costs sit deeply depressed at 80.4, nearly twenty points below the national norm. This divergence means the typical consumer experiences a bifurcated reality: the recurring, non-negotiable outlays for physical necessities run slightly hot, while the largest fixed expense—shelter—and service-intensive costs offer significant relief relative to the broader U.S. economy. The median household income of $60,839 trails the national median of $65,047 by roughly 6.5 percent, but the local purchasing power index of 61,607.9 indicates that lower prices effectively bridge that nominal gap, granting residents slightly more real buying power than their raw paychecks suggest. Housing affordability is the primary driver of this dynamic; median monthly rent of $771 consumes only 15.2 percent of gross income, a figure that stands in stark contrast to the 30-percent burden threshold common in metropolitan centers and the national median rent of $928. This low shelter cost absorbs the friction of above-average goods pricing, leaving a larger residual for savings or debt service, provided employment remains stable. However, the local labor market introduces vulnerability: an unemployment rate of 6.4 percent sits well above typical national lows, and an educational attainment rate of just 11.8 percent for bachelor’s degrees or higher suggests a workforce concentrated in sectors with limited wage growth trajectories. The median age of 41.3 further implies a population potentially facing rising healthcare utilization, a category where the 92.4 services index provides a buffer but cannot fully offset fixed insurance premiums or out-of-pocket maximums set at national levels. Over the past decade, the economic trajectory has favored the earner, though the pace of improvement has decelerated. The RPP declined from 101.778 in 2010 to 98.752 in 2023, a 3.0 percent drop in relative living costs, while median income surged 44.0 percent from $42,238 to $60,839. Rent appreciation lagged significantly at 23.8 percent, rising from $623 to $771, meaning shelter costs grew at barely half the rate of incomes. This divergence has expanded the margin between earnings and essential outlays, a rare win for real wage growth in the current economic climate. The data suggests the county has become progressively more affordable in real terms, largely because income growth has outstripped the local inflation basket, particularly in the heavily weighted housing component. For a prospective resident constructing a monthly budget, the actionable takeaway centers on the asymmetry between fixed and variable costs. The low rent burden—roughly $771 for a median unit—frees cash flow that would otherwise be allocated to housing in almost any other jurisdiction, allowing for absorption of the 1.6 percent premium on goods without severe lifestyle compression. Budgeters should, however, allocate a higher-than-average share of the variable expense column to groceries and transportation fuel, as the 101.6 goods index compounds across every shopping trip and commute. The services discount of 7.6 percent offers modest relief on utilities and dining, but it does not neutralize the goods penalty. Ultimately, the county presents a low-fixed-cost, high-variable-cost environment; financial resilience here depends less on negotiating rent and more on managing the weekly volatility of the checkout lane and the gas pump.

What this means for residents of Marion County, Illinois

With a Purchasing Power Index of 61607.9, a median household income of $60,839 in Marion County, Illinois has the equivalent buying power of $37,481,630 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.4 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
