📊 Local Market Analysis

Monroe County presents a cost of living profile that sits 13.2 percent below the national benchmark, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 86.8 for 2023, yet this aggregate figure masks a severe structural distortion in local consumer prices. Goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—represent the highest cost component at 93.1, nearly converging with the national average, while Services plummet to 56.7, the lowest component by a wide margin, and Rental Costs settle at 81.9. This divergence indicates that while housing consumes a relatively modest 16.5 percent of the median household income of $43,955—well below the standard 30 percent burden threshold—residents face near-national pricing for tangible daily necessities without the corresponding wage structure to support them. The median monthly rent of $603 compares favorably against the national $928, but the Goods index suggests that the savings on shelter are substantially eroded at the checkout counter and the gas pump. The purchasing power calculus reveals the true constraint on household budgets. Although the Purchasing Power Index adjusts the nominal median income to $50,633.6 to reflect local price levels, this adjusted figure remains 22.2 percent below the national median income of $65,047, signaling that the cost advantage fails to fully close the earnings gap. Local demographics sharpen this picture: an unemployment rate of 9.4 percent, an educational attainment rate of just 12.0 percent for bachelor’s degrees or higher, and a median age of 45.5 years describe a labor market characterized by limited high-wage opportunity and an older workforce with potentially fixed or stagnant earnings. In this context, the low Services index likely reflects a scarcity of healthcare providers, dining options, and utility infrastructure rather than a consumer windfall, forcing residents to travel for specialized care or accept limited local amenities. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the economic trajectory has favored income growth over inflation, though from a deeply depressed base. The overall RPP declined a modest 2.0 percent, moving from 88.561 to 86.81, while median household income surged 59.0 percent from $27,647 to $43,955. Median rent increased only 21.3 percent over the same span, rising from $497 to $603, confirming that wage growth has significantly outpaced the primary fixed expense of shelter. However, the persistence of a Goods index near parity (93.1) suggests that the rising tide of income has not insulated households from national supply-chain pressures on food and energy, categories where local policy holds little sway. For a prospective resident, the budgeting reality centers on a trade-off between cheap shelter and expensive survival goods. The low rent burden—roughly $603 monthly—frees cash flow, but the 93.1 Goods index demands that a disproportionate share of that liberated income be allocated to groceries and transportation at near-national prices. The 56.7 Services index implies limited local access to healthcare and professional services, introducing hidden costs of time and travel for an aging population. With unemployment near double digits and educational attainment low, the local labor market offers limited leverage for wage negotiation, meaning household resilience depends heavily on external income sources or remote work arrangements rather than local economic dynamism.

What this means for residents of Monroe County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 50633.6, a median household income of $43,955 in Monroe County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $22,255,999 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
