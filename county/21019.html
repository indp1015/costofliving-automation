📊 Local Market Analysis

Boyd County residents navigate a cost structure that sits 10.1 percent below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 89.9 in 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals a distinct bifurcation in daily expenses. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — represent the highest local cost component at 94.5, only marginally cheaper than the U.S. baseline, while Services plummet to 62.9, the lowest segment by a wide margin. Rental costs settle at 80.9, roughly 19 percent below the national figure, aligning with a median monthly rent of $839 against a national median of $928. This profile indicates that while housing and healthcare/utilities deliver substantial savings, the unavoidable weekly outlay for consumables remains near parity with higher-cost metros, compressing the practical relief a household feels at the checkout counter. The median household income of $60,659 trails the national median of $65,047 by 6.7 percent, but the Purchasing Power Index of 67,481.4 suggests that after adjusting for the local price level, the typical earner commands roughly 3.7 percent more real purchasing capacity than the nominal income gap implies. Rent burden sits at a remarkably low 16.6 percent of gross income, a figure that underscores the dominance of the housing discount in the local budget calculus. However, with Goods priced at 94.5 of the national average, a disproportionate share of disposable income flows toward non-discretionary items like food and fuel, muting the theoretical advantage conferred by cheap services and rent. The labor market context — a 6.6 percent unemployment rate paired with only 10.4 percent of adults holding a bachelor’s degree and a median age of 42.4 — points to an economy reliant on sectors where wage growth often lags productivity gains, limiting the ability of households to arbitrage the low service costs into higher savings rates. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP drifted downward only marginally from 90.4 to 89.9, a negligible 0.6 percent decline, while nominal median income surged 43.3 percent from $42,319 to $60,659 and median rent rose 34.2 percent from $625 to $839. Wages have demonstrably outpaced both the broad cost index and shelter costs over the decade, expanding real purchasing power significantly. Yet the persistence of Goods pricing near the national ceiling means inflationary pressure on essentials — grocery bills, gasoline, clothing — transmits almost one-to-one from national trends, leaving households exposed to the same price shocks experienced in far more expensive regions without the offsetting wage premiums found in those labor markets. A prospective resident should budget for a daily expense profile where the "cheap" categories are structural and fixed — rent, utilities, and healthcare access — while the "expensive" categories are variable and frequent. The 94.5 Goods index implies a grocery bill for a family of four that might run only 5 to 6 percent below a suburban Ohio or Virginia counterpart, whereas the 62.9 Services index translates to markedly lower out-of-pocket costs for medical visits, electricity, and dining out. With unemployment elevated relative to the national rate and educational attainment low, income volatility remains a tangible risk; the low rent burden provides a cushion, but the high marginal propensity to consume on goods leaves limited slack for unexpected price spikes in fuel or food.

What this means for residents of Boyd County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 67481.4, a median household income of $60,659 in Boyd County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $40,933,542 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
