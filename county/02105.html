📊 Local Market Analysis

The Hoonah‑Angoon Census Area registers a cost of living that sits 3.3 percent above the national baseline, with a Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 103.3. Goods prices are marginally higher than the country average at 103.7, while services are modestly cheaper at 96.7. Housing, measured by rental costs, is the outlier: an index of 113.3 places local rents 13.3 percent above the national level, a gap that widens the overall cost profile despite lower service expenses. Housing drives the local cost structure. Median monthly rent of $986 exceeds the national median of $928 by 6.2 percent, and the rent‑burden metric shows that households allocate 18.9 percent of income to housing—well under the conventional 30 percent affordability threshold but still the dominant expense category. Goods remain near parity, so everyday purchases such as groceries and fuel do not impose a substantial premium. Services, including healthcare and utilities, are 3.3 percent cheaper than the national average, tempering the overall cost pressure. Consequently, daily life is modestly expensive, primarily because housing consumes a larger share of income while other consumption categories remain competitive. Disposable income for the median household is constrained by the interaction of income and housing costs. With a median income of $62,500, the Purchasing Power Index (PPI) of 60,505.1 indicates that residents can afford roughly 60 percent of the national basket of goods and services. After paying $986 in rent, the median household retains $61,514 annually for other expenses, equating to about $5,126 per month. This residual amount must cover utilities, food, transportation, and discretionary spending, leaving limited flexibility compared with the national average where median rent is lower and income slightly higher. Demographic factors amplify the cost experience. The unemployment rate of 16.9 percent far exceeds the national average, curtailing household earnings and reinforcing the rent‑burden despite the relatively low rent‑to‑income ratio. Only 15.8 percent of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting a labor market dominated by lower‑skill, lower‑wage occupations. A median age of 51.4 years points to an older population, likely with fixed or retirement incomes, further limiting discretionary spending capacity. Over the past decade, RPP has risen a mere 0.2 percent, from 103.099 in 2010 to 103.297 in 2023, indicating price stability. Median household income, however, grew 19.2 percent—from $52,419 to $62,500—while median rent surged 36.0 percent, from $725 to $986. Wage growth outpaced overall price changes but lagged behind rent inflation, eroding real purchasing power for renters. Prospective residents should budget aggressively for housing, allocating close to one‑fifth of income to rent, and anticipate that the bulk of remaining funds will cover essential goods and services that are near national cost levels. Careful tracking of utility bills, grocery spend, and transportation costs will be essential to maintain a balanced household budget in this remote Alaskan region.
