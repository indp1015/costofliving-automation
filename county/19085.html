📊 Local Market Analysis

Harrison County presents a cost of living profile that sits 11.2 percentage points below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 88.8 for 2023, a modest decline from 91.1 in 2010. This aggregate figure masks a sharp bifurcation in consumer pricing: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel track close to the national baseline at 96.6, while services—including healthcare, utilities, and dining—plummet to 66.0, the lowest component by a wide margin. Rental costs occupy a middle ground at 87.3, suggesting that while shelter is affordable relative to coastal metros, the daily basket of tangible goods offers residents little discount compared to the broader U.S. consumer. The services category acts as the primary deflationary force in the local budget, suppressing the overall index despite near-parity pricing on goods. A median household income of $77,027 translates into a Purchasing Power Index of 86,772.4, indicating that after adjusting for local price levels, the typical household commands roughly $21,700 more in real purchasing capacity than the national median income of $65,047 would suggest. This surplus is reinforced by a rent burden of just 13.3 percent—median monthly rent of $854 against the national $928—leaving a disproportionately large share of disposable income for savings, debt service, or discretionary consumption. The labor market underscores this stability; a 2.8 percent unemployment rate signals tight conditions that support wage growth, even as educational attainment remains modest at 15.6 percent holding a bachelor’s degree or higher and the median age of 41.9 years points to an established, rather than transient, workforce. Over the past decade, the economic trajectory has favored the earner. Median income surged 43.8 percent from $53,567 to $77,027, outpacing a 33.6 percent rise in median rent from $639 to $854, while the RPP itself drifted downward by 2.6 percent. This divergence implies that wage gains have not merely kept pace with shelter inflation but have accelerated past the broader cost curve, expanding real household budgets. The declining RPP suggests that local service sectors—healthcare, personal care, and utilities—have either improved productivity or faced competitive pressure that national averages have not mirrored, a dynamic that benefits the resident consumer directly. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the goods-services split. Weekly grocery trips and fuel fills will feel familiar in price, demanding national-level outlays, whereas recurring service obligations—medical visits, utility bills, restaurant meals—carry a structural 34 percent discount. Housing costs, while rising, remain anchored well below the national median, preserving cash flow. The demographic profile suggests a community where labor demand is firm but credential requirements are lower, meaning income growth may rely more on tenure and overtime than on degree-driven advancement. Households relocating from higher-cost regions should anticipate a immediate boost in discretionary capacity, provided their consumption patterns do not overweight goods relative to the local service advantage.

What this means for residents of Harrison County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 86772.4, a median household income of $77,027 in Harrison County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $66,838,177 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
