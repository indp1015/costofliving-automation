📊 Local Market Analysis

Monroe County’s cost of living sits well below the national norm, with the BEA Regional Price Parities index at 89.9 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices are the most elevated component at 94.5, still under the national benchmark, while services are markedly cheap at 62.9. Housing, measured by rental costs, registers at 80.9, indicating that a typical apartment costs roughly 19 % less than the average American market. The composite picture is one of a region where everyday expenses—groceries, apparel, fuel, and utilities—are consistently cheaper than elsewhere, even though the price of consumable goods remains the highest sub‑category within the county. The dominant driver of the local cost structure is the service sector, whose index of 62.9 pulls the overall cost down. Daily life therefore feels inexpensive: a resident spending $400 a month on utilities, healthcare, and dining would be paying only about $250 in a comparable national setting. Rental costs reinforce this affordability; the median monthly rent of $629 is 32 % lower than the national median of $928, and the rent‑burden metric shows that only 15.3 % of household income is devoted to rent, far beneath the 30 % threshold that signals affordability stress. Consequently, while goods remain the priciest segment, the combined effect of low service and housing costs makes routine household budgeting relatively relaxed. Disposable income calculations underscore the purchasing advantage. Median household income in Monroe County stands at $49,430, roughly 24 % below the national median of $65,047. After subtracting the median rent of $629 (approximately $7,548 annually), a typical household retains $41,882 for all other expenses. The Purchasing Power Index of 54,989.4 quantifies this residual buying capacity, translating to roughly 84 % of the national purchasing power level. In practical terms, a family can afford a broader basket of goods and services than the index suggests, thanks to the low cost of services and housing that free up income for discretionary spending. Demographic factors shape this affordability landscape. With only 5.8 % of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, the labor market is skewed toward lower‑skill occupations, which traditionally command modest wages. The unemployment rate of 4.2 % aligns closely with the national average, indicating a stable job environment but not one that drives high earnings. A median age of 41.3 years points to a mature workforce, likely entrenched in long‑term employment and less prone to rapid income volatility. These characteristics explain why wages have risen sharply—median income climbed 59.2 % from $31,045 in 2010 to $49,430—yet remain anchored by a cost structure that does not accelerate at the same pace. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped marginally from 90.433 to 89.89, a 0.6 % decline, signaling that consumer prices have softened slightly even as incomes surged. Rent, however, increased 21.0 % from $520 to $629, outpacing the modest deflation in the overall price index but still lagging far behind national rent growth. The disparity between income growth (59.2 %) and rent inflation (21.0 %) suggests that wages have outstripped housing cost pressures, reinforcing the county’s affordability advantage. Prospective residents should budget with the low service and housing costs in mind. Allocating roughly 15 % of income to rent leaves 85 % for utilities, healthcare, food, and transportation, where goods prices hover just under the national average. A monthly grocery bill of $350 and a utility bill near $150 would be typical, leaving ample room for discretionary spending or savings. Understanding that services are the cheapest component helps households prioritize spending on higher‑priced goods while capitalizing on the county’s overall low cost of living.

What this means for residents of Monroe County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 54989.4, a median household income of $49,430 in Monroe County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $27,181,260 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
