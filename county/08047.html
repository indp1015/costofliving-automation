📊 Local Market Analysis

Gilpin County presents a distinctive cost-of-living profile where the overall Regional Price Parity of 101.9 masks a severe bifurcation between housing affordability and service-sector inflation, sitting 1.9 percent above the national baseline. The composite index is suppressed by rental costs registering at 86.8—13.2 percent below the national average—and goods prices at 99.2, nearly at parity. However, the services component, encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining, surges to 130.5, marking a 30.5 percent premium over the U.S. mean and establishing services as the primary driver of local expense. This dynamic creates a paradoxical environment where securing shelter is relatively inexpensive compared to the national median rent of $928, yet the recurring monthly outlays for essential services exert disproportionate pressure on household cash flow. The median household income of $88,654 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 36.3 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 87,038.6 reveals that local price levels erode roughly 1.8 percent of that nominal advantage. Rent burden stands at a manageable 18.1 percent of gross income—well below the 30 percent stress threshold—largely because the median monthly rent of $1,340 remains depressed relative to income growth. Over the 2010–2023 period, median income accelerated 35.0 percent from $65,670, outpacing the 30.2 percent climb in median rent from $1,029. However, the overall RPP drifted only marginally from 100.59 to 101.856, a 1.3 percent increase, suggesting that the bulk of inflationary pressure has been absorbed by the services category rather than shelter, preserving real wage gains for tenants but penalizing consumption of healthcare and utilities. Demographic and labor indicators frame a workforce that is older, less formally educated, and tightly employed. A median age of 48.7 years signals a population skewed toward peak earning years or early retirement, while a 22.5 percent bachelor’s degree attainment rate trails national averages, potentially concentrating employment in sectors vulnerable to service-cost inflation such as hospitality, gaming, or trades. The 3.0 percent unemployment rate indicates a taut labor market that supports wage negotiation but offers little slack for households absorbing the 30.5 percent services premium. This tightness suggests that while nominal incomes have risen robustly, the purchasing power of those wages is structurally capped by the high floor for non-discretionary services. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the divergence between fixed and variable costs. The housing discount—rent consuming less than one-fifth of gross income—frees capital for savings or discretionary spending, but the services surcharge demands explicit allocation. A household budgeting $1,340 for rent should anticipate utility, healthcare, and dining expenses running roughly 30 percent higher than in a median U.S. county. The stability of the RPP over the last decade implies this cost structure is persistent rather than cyclical. Residents benefit from a labor market that has historically delivered income growth exceeding rent growth, yet they must contend with a services tax that effectively reduces the real value of every dollar spent outside of housing.

What this means for residents of Gilpin County, Colorado

With a Purchasing Power Index of 87038.6, a median household income of $88,654 in Gilpin County, Colorado has the equivalent buying power of $77,163,200 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 86.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
