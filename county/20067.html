📊 Local Market Analysis

Grant County, Kansas, presents a cost of living profile significantly below the national baseline, registering an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 89.9 in 2023. This 10.1 percent discount relative to the national average of 100 is structurally driven by a pronounced divergence between goods and services pricing. While goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel — sit at 96.5, merely 3.5 percent below the U.S. mean, services collapse to 68.6, a staggering 31.4 percent discount that ranks as the county’s most deflationary component. Rental costs, indexed at 90.8, occupy the middle ground, roughly 9.2 percent cheaper than the national benchmark. Consequently, daily life is distinctly inexpensive for service-intensive consumption — healthcare, utilities, and dining — while the weekly grocery bill or fuel stop remains largely aligned with broader national pricing pressures. The consumption breakdown reveals that the local cost structure is anchored by the relative affordability of services rather than goods or shelter. With services representing the lowest index value at 68.6, households allocate a smaller share of their budget to recurring monthly obligations like insurance, medical visits, and utility bills than counterparts in almost any other jurisdiction. This dynamic suppresses the overall index despite goods prices hovering near parity. The median monthly rent of $696 consumes only 11.5 percent of the median household income of $72,484, a rent burden roughly half the national standard and vastly lower than the national median rent of $928. This shelter affordability, combined with cheap services, creates a fiscal environment where fixed overhead is minimal, leaving disproportionate income available for discretionary spending or savings. Purchasing power metrics confirm the material advantage this cost structure confers. The Purchasing Power Index of 80,620.2 indicates that the median household’s $72,484 income commands the equivalent of roughly $80,620 in goods and services at national average prices, effectively delivering a 11.2 percent real income premium over the nominal figure. This surplus exceeds the national median income of $65,047 by a wide margin in real terms. The local labor market reinforces this dynamic: an unemployment rate of 2.5 percent signals a tight market with strong wage bargaining power, while a median age of 32.9 years suggests a workforce in its prime earning years. However, the relatively low educational attainment — only 16.0 percent hold a bachelor’s degree or higher — implies the wage floor is supported by industrial or agricultural demand rather than knowledge-economy premiums, a factor that sustains affordability by capping high-end service inflation. Over the past decade, the trajectory has favored the resident. The overall RPP declined from 92.991 in 2010 to 89.908 in 2023, a 3.3 percent drop in relative price levels, while nominal median income surged 30.8 percent from $55,432 to $72,484. Median rent increased only 22.1 percent over the same period, from $570 to $696, lagging income growth by nearly nine percentage points. This divergence indicates that wages have not merely kept pace with expenses but have outstripped them considerably, expanding the real wage buffer. For a prospective resident, the budgeting implication is clear: the primary financial friction point will be goods purchases — groceries and transportation — where national inflation transmits almost directly. Conversely, the service basket and housing costs offer deep, structural discounts that insulate the household budget from the volatility plaguing higher-cost metros, allowing for aggressive savings rates or elevated discretionary consumption without corresponding income stress.

What this means for residents of Grant County, Kansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 80620.2, a median household income of $72,484 in Grant County, Kansas has the equivalent buying power of $58,436,746 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 90.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
