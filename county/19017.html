📊 Local Market Analysis

Bremer County presents a cost of living profile significantly below the national benchmark, with an overall Regional Price Parity of 88.8 indicating that a standard basket of goods and services costs roughly 11.2 percent less than the U.S. average. This aggregate figure masks a sharp bifurcation in consumer expenditure categories: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel carry an index of 96.6, nearly converging with national pricing, while services—encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining—plunge to an exceptionally low 66.0, the primary driver suppressing the overall index. Rental costs sit at 87.3, offering meaningful shelter savings relative to the national median rent of $928, with the local median of $844 consuming only 12.2 percent of median household income, a burden level that signals substantial housing affordability for renters. The purchasing power calculus confirms the advantage. A median household income of $83,343—28.1 percent above the national median of $65,047—combines with the depressed price level to yield a Purchasing Power Index of 93,887.5, meaning the typical resident commands roughly $28,800 more in real purchasing capacity than the national median worker. Local labor dynamics reinforce this strength; an unemployment rate of 3.6 percent signals a tight market supporting wage growth, while a median age of 38.6 and a bachelor’s attainment rate of 24.1 percent suggest a workforce balanced between experienced prime-age earners and a modest professional class, stabilizing the tax base and service demand without the inflationary pressure seen in high-density talent hubs. Over the 2010–2023 period, the county’s cost trajectory has improved relative to the nation, with the RPP declining 2.6 percent from 91.139 to 88.769. Nominal income growth of 34.1 percent, rising from $62,163 to $83,343, has comfortably outpaced the 42.8 percent surge in median rent from $591 to $844, though the latter’s faster clip warrants monitoring. Because the RPP fell while rents rose, the data implies that non-housing categories—particularly services—experienced disinflation or price declines strong enough to drag the composite index down, effectively subsidizing the household budget against shelter cost acceleration. For a prospective resident, the budgeting reality centers on a distinct trade-off: daily variable expenses for goods run near national parity at 96.6, offering little arbitrage on grocery or fuel bills, while fixed service costs—insurance premiums, utility bills, medical copays—represent the primary zone of savings at 34 percent below average. The low rent burden of 12.2 percent frees cash flow for savings or discretionary spending, but the goods index suggests that consumption habits tied to tangible products will not stretch significantly further here than in a typical metro. Households relocating from high-cost corridors should calibrate expectations: the windfall derives almost entirely from service-sector deflation and rental discounts, not cheaper trips to the supermarket.

What this means for residents of Bremer County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 93887.5, a median household income of $83,343 in Bremer County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $78,248,659 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
