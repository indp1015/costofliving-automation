📊 Local Market Analysis

Gilchrist County presents a cost of living profile that sits modestly above the national baseline, registering a 2023 Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 103.6 against the U.S. average of 100, yet this aggregate figure masks a severe structural distortion in local consumer pricing. The overall index is inflated almost entirely by the Services component, which clocks in at 123.2—23.2 percent above the national norm—encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining expenses that weigh heavily on recurring monthly budgets. Conversely, the Goods category, covering groceries, apparel, and fuel, runs below parity at 98.2, while Rental Costs offer significant relief at 93.7, roughly 6.3 percent cheaper than the national average. This divergence creates a bifurcated experience: households benefit from relatively affordable shelter and tangible goods but face persistent pressure from service-intensive necessities that are difficult to substitute or defer. The Services sector acts as the primary driver of the local cost structure, rendering daily life more expensive than the headline RPP suggests for residents whose consumption baskets skew toward healthcare utilization, electricity consumption, or prepared meals. With a Purchasing Power Index of 58,927.4 derived from a median household income of $61,070, the median worker retains considerably less real purchasing power than the nominal income implies, falling roughly $6,100 short of the national median income of $65,047 even before adjusting for the 3.6 percent price premium. However, the low rental burden of 16.8 percent—calculated against a median monthly rent of $855 versus the national $928—provides a critical buffer, absorbing a smaller share of cash flow than in most comparable jurisdictions and partially offsetting the service-sector penalty. Demographic and labor market characteristics sharpen the context for these cost dynamics. An unemployment rate of 3.2 percent signals a tight labor market that theoretically supports wage growth, yet an educational attainment rate of only 8.8 percent for bachelor’s degrees or higher suggests a workforce concentrated in lower-wage service or trade occupations, limiting the household income elasticity required to absorb the 123.2 Services index. The median age of 42.7 years indicates a population skewing older than the national median, a cohort that typically commands higher healthcare consumption, thereby amplifying the impact of the county’s most expensive cost category on the typical household budget. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP climbed from 100.4 to 103.6, a cumulative increase of 3.2 percent, while median income surged 50.3 percent from $40,623 to $61,070 and median rent rose 29.9 percent from $658 to $855. Wages have decisively outpaced both the general price level and shelter costs over the decade, improving the real income trajectory for incumbent residents. A prospective resident budgeting for relocation should weigh the advantage of sub-$900 median rents against the certainty of elevated recurring service bills—particularly electricity and medical outlays—that exceed national norms by over 20 percent, ensuring that the apparent affordability of housing does not obscure the higher fixed cost of daily maintenance in this county.

What this means for residents of Gilchrist County, Florida

With a Purchasing Power Index of 58927.4, a median household income of $61,070 in Gilchrist County, Florida has the equivalent buying power of $35,986,963 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 93.7 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
