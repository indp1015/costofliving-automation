📊 Local Market Analysis

Perry County’s cost of living sits well below the national norm, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 89.1 versus 100 for the United States. Grocery, apparel and fuel prices register at 94.6, still under the national average, while utilities, health care and dining out are markedly cheap at 61.6. Rental costs, measured by the housing component of the RPP, are 89.9, indicating that a typical apartment costs roughly 10 % less than the national benchmark. These figures translate into a median monthly rent of $610, compared with $928 nationwide, and a rent‑burden ratio of 21.3 % of household income—well under the 30 % threshold that defines affordability. The dominant driver of Perry County’s price structure is the goods category, which, at 94.6, exceeds both services and housing. Nevertheless, the absolute level remains modest, so daily expenses for food and transportation are only slightly higher than the already low service costs. The net effect is a household budget that feels inexpensive, especially for discretionary spending on health care and dining, where prices are roughly 38 % below the national average. The low service index reflects reduced provider wages and limited competition, which keeps out‑of‑pocket costs down but may also signal constrained service quality. Disposable income calculations reveal a stark disparity between local earnings and purchasing power. The median household income of $34,368 yields a monthly pre‑tax income of $2,864; after subtracting the median rent of $610, 78.7 % of earnings remain for all other expenses. The Purchasing Power Index of 38,573.7—approximately 38 % of the national figure—captures the combined effect of lower wages and cheaper goods, indicating that even with modest expenditures, residents retain far less real buying power than the average American. The high unemployment rate of 15.8 % and the low share of residents with a bachelor’s degree (10.5 %) limit upward wage mobility, reinforcing the modest disposable income pool. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped from 89.78 to 89.10, a 0.8 % decline, while median household income rose 40.1 % from $24,537 to $34,368. Rent increased 19.1 % in the same period, outpacing the modest rise in the housing price index. Wage growth has outstripped price inflation, yet the gains are muted by persistent unemployment and limited educational attainment, which together suppress labor‑market bargaining power. For a prospective resident, budgeting must prioritize the low‑cost service environment while accounting for a relatively high rent‑to‑income ratio compared with national norms. Allocating roughly one‑fifth of income to housing leaves about 60 % for food, transportation, health care and discretionary items; however, the limited local labor market suggests that supplementing income through remote work or commuting may be necessary to achieve a comfortable standard of living.

What this means for residents of Perry County, Alabama

With a Purchasing Power Index of 38573.7, a median household income of $34,368 in Perry County, Alabama has the equivalent buying power of $13,257,009 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 89.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
