📊 Local Market Analysis

Perry County sits 10.9 percent below the national price level with an overall Regional Price Parity of 89.1, yet the composition of that discount reveals a distorted consumer landscape rather than broad-based affordability. Goods — groceries, apparel, and fuel — run only 5.4 percent cheaper than the national average at 94.6, while services including healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 61.6, a staggering 38.4 percent discount that drags the headline index down. Rental costs at 89.9 track close to the overall index, meaning the county's low-cost reputation rests almost entirely on a services sector priced far below national norms, likely reflecting limited provider density and suppressed wage floors rather than genuine value for households. The median household income of $34,368 yields a purchasing power index of 38,573.7 when adjusted for local prices, effectively granting residents a 12.2 percent boost in real terms against nominal earnings, but that gain evaporates under scrutiny. Rent consumes 21.3 percent of gross income at a median $610 monthly, a manageable burden on paper, yet the 15.8 percent unemployment rate and a bachelor's attainment rate of just 10.5 percent signal a labor market incapable of delivering stable, full-time hours at wages that cover the 94.6-priced goods basket. With a median age of 42.5, the workforce skews older and less educated, concentrating earnings in low-wage service roles that cannot absorb even modest price increases in food or fuel. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP barely moved, slipping from 89.783 to 89.097, a negligible 0.8 percent decline, while nominal median income surged 40.1 percent from $24,537 to $34,368 and median rent climbed just 19.1 percent from $512 to $610. On the surface, wages have outpaced shelter costs by a wide margin, but the goods index at 94.6 implies that the basket households actually buy daily — groceries, gasoline, clothing — has likely tracked closer to national inflation than the headline RPP suggests, eroding much of the nominal gain for families without employer-sponsored benefits or supplemental income. A prospective resident should budget for near-national prices at the grocery store and the pump while accepting that healthcare access, dining options, and utility reliability will reflect the 61.6 services index — sparse, inexpensive, and potentially inadequate. The rent-to-income ratio appears favorable, but the 15.8 percent unemployment rate means income volatility is the dominant budget risk, not fixed expenses. Households moving from higher-wage metros will find their dollars stretch further on rent but not on the weekly shop, and any interruption in earnings exposes the thin margin between the purchasing power index and the actual cost of a goods-heavy consumption bundle.

What this means for residents of Perry County, Alabama

With a Purchasing Power Index of 38573.7, a median household income of $34,368 in Perry County, Alabama has the equivalent buying power of $13,257,009 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 89.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
