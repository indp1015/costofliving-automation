📊 Local Market Analysis

Decatur County residents experience a cost of living roughly 8 percent below the national benchmark, reflected in a 2023 Regional Price Parity of 92.1, yet the composition of that discount reveals a distinct bifurcation in daily expenses. Goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—sit at 95.6, only marginally cheaper than the U.S. average, while Services plummet to an index of 71.3, marking the lowest cost component and a substantial 28.7 percent discount relative to the nation. Rental costs, indexed at 84.7, occupy the middle ground, contributing to an overall environment where the median monthly rent of $828 consumes just 13.4 percent of the $74,228 median household income, a burden significantly lighter than the national median rent of $928 against a lower national median income of $65,047. The consumption breakdown indicates that daily life is structurally inexpensive primarily due to the services sector, where healthcare, utilities, and dining out cost dramatically less than in metropolitan centers, offsetting the near-parity pricing of tangible goods. This dynamic creates a purchasing power advantage quantified by an index of 80,597.6, suggesting the median household commands real resources roughly 24 percent above the nominal national median after adjusting for local prices. The labor market reinforces this stability; a 2.6 percent unemployment rate signals tight demand for labor, while a median age of 40.4 and a bachelor’s attainment rate of 14.6 percent describe a workforce anchored in established, likely blue-collar or technical occupations rather than high-wage knowledge sectors, keeping wage pressure moderate but steady. Over the past decade, the RPP has drifted marginally lower, moving from 93.0 to 92.1, indicating that local consumer price inflation has lagged the national pace by approximately 1 percent cumulatively. In stark contrast, nominal median household income surged 49.1 percent, climbing from $49,799 to $74,228, while median rent increased only 12.0 percent, from $739 to $828. This divergence means real wage growth has significantly outpaced the two largest household budget line items—shelter and general consumption—expanding the margin for discretionary spending or savings accumulation for incumbent residents. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the asymmetry between goods and services. While housing costs offer predictable, low-stress allocation—rent claiming roughly one-eighth of gross income—grocery and fuel budgets will feel familiar to anyone relocating from average-cost metros, offering little arbitrage opportunity. The windfall resides in services: healthcare premiums, utility bills, and restaurant meals likely represent the deepest discounts relative to prior locations. However, the low educational attainment figure and service-sector pricing structure suggest limited high-end consumption amenities, meaning the savings on services may reflect a narrower array of options rather than pure efficiency. Households should model budgets assuming goods costs track national trends while treating the services discount as a structural, persistent feature of the local economy.
