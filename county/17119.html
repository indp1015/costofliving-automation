📊 Local Market Analysis

Madison County’s cost of living sits just below the national norm, with the overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) index at 98.8 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices are modestly higher than average at 101.6, while services are notably cheaper at 92.4, and housing—measured by rental costs—trades at a pronounced discount of 80.4. The median household income of $74,800 outpaces the national median of $65,047 by roughly 15 %, yet the county’s rent burden remains low at 15.5 % of income, reflecting the affordability of housing relative to earnings. The expense structure is driven primarily by the goods component. With an index of 101.6, everyday items such as groceries, apparel, and fuel cost about 1.6 % more than the national average, offsetting the savings generated by cheaper services and housing. Daily life therefore feels mixed: consumers benefit from lower utility bills, healthcare, and dining costs, but must allocate a slightly larger share of their budget to essential consumables. The rental market, at $969 median monthly rent, is 4.4 % above the national median of $928, yet the low rent‑to‑income ratio (15.5 % versus the often‑cited 30 % affordability threshold) keeps overall household expenditures manageable. Purchasing power, captured by the index of 75,745.3, indicates that a median earner in Madison County retains roughly $10,700 more purchasing power than the national median household after accounting for local price levels. Subtracting the median rent of $969 from monthly income (approximately $6,233) leaves $5,264 for all other expenses, a figure that comfortably covers the below‑average service costs and the slightly elevated goods prices. The net effect is a disposable income pool that supports a modestly higher standard of living than the national average. Demographic factors reinforce this affordability. With 19.6 % of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, the labor force is less credentialed than the national average, which can limit access to high‑paying occupations. Nevertheless, the unemployment rate of 4.7 % aligns closely with the national rate, suggesting a stable job market. The median age of 40.2 years points to a mature population, often associated with higher household formation rates and steadier consumption patterns, which can temper price pressures on services and utilities. Over the past decade, the RPP fell from 101.78 in 2010 to 98.75 in 2023, a 3 % decline, while median household income rose from $53,431 to $74,800, a 40 % increase. Rent climbed from $778 to $969, a 24.6 % rise, outpacing the national rent growth but remaining proportionally low relative to income gains. Wage growth has therefore outstripped inflationary pressures, expanding real purchasing power for residents. Prospective movers should budget for a slightly higher grocery and fuel bill than the national average, while expecting savings on healthcare, utilities, and dining out. Allocating roughly 15 % of income to rent leaves about 85 % for all other categories; within that remainder, a 1–2 % premium on goods should be factored in. A realistic monthly budget might allocate $600–$650 to food and transportation, $400–$450 to services, and the remainder to discretionary spending, ensuring that the county’s overall affordability translates into a comfortable household cash flow.

What this means for residents of Madison County, Illinois

With a Purchasing Power Index of 75745.3, a median household income of $74,800 in Madison County, Illinois has the equivalent buying power of $56,657,484 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.4 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
