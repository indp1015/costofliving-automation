📊 Local Market Analysis

Yavapai County’s cost of living sits marginally above the national baseline, with an overall Relative Price Parity (RPP) of 100.7 compared with the U.S. index of 100. Goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel are priced 2.1 % lower than the national average (97.9), while services—including healthcare, utilities, and dining—run 8.6 % higher (108.6). Rental costs are the most affordable component, at 83.3, indicating that landlords charge roughly 17 % less than the national norm. The mixed profile means that everyday necessities are cheap, but recurring service expenses push the aggregate cost slightly above the countrywide figure. The dominant driver of Yavapai’s price structure is the services sector. At 108.6, it outweighs the modest savings in goods and the pronounced advantage in housing. Residents therefore experience a paradox: shelter and basic consumables are inexpensive, yet routine expenditures such as medical visits, electricity, and restaurant meals erode the savings. For a household earning the median income of $66,106, the net effect is a modest cost advantage in housing offset by a service premium that makes daily life only slightly more expensive than elsewhere in the United States. Purchasing power calculations underscore the tight budgetary balance. The median household’s monthly rent of $1,258 consumes 22.8 % of annual earnings, well below the 30 % rent‑burden threshold that signals financial strain. After accounting for the full cost profile, the Purchasing Power Index stands at 65,619.1, roughly 2 % below the national median income of $65,047. In practical terms, a median earner retains enough discretionary income to cover basic goods and services, but the elevated service costs limit the amount available for non‑essential spending. The index reflects that while Yavapai residents enjoy cheaper housing, the higher service prices compress real disposable income relative to the national average. Demographic factors shape this cost environment. With a median age of 55 years, the county’s population skews older, a profile that typically demands higher healthcare utilization, reinforcing the service price premium. Only 17.3 % hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting a labor market concentrated in lower‑skill occupations that may not command premium wages. The unemployment rate of 4.2 % aligns closely with the national rate, indicating a stable but not booming job market. Consequently, the combination of an aging populace and modest educational attainment sustains demand for services while limiting upward pressure on wages. Over the past decade, the RPP index has risen from 100.558 in 2010 to 100.742 in 2023, a negligible 0.2 % increase, signaling price stability. Median household income, however, surged 47.7 % from $44,748 to $66,106, outpacing the 46.8 % rise in median rent (from $857 to $1,258). The near‑parallel growth of income and rent preserves affordability, while the flat RPP suggests that price pressures have been largely contained. Wage growth has therefore kept pace with housing costs and marginally exceeded the modest inflation in services, preserving the county’s overall purchasing power. Prospective residents should structure budgets around the service premium. Allocating roughly 30 % of income to utilities, healthcare, and dining is prudent, given the 108.6 service index. Housing costs, at 22.8 % of earnings, remain manageable, but the higher cost of services means that discretionary spending—entertainment, travel, and non‑essential goods—will be tighter than in regions where services are cheaper. A realistic monthly budget for a median household would therefore prioritize low‑cost housing, monitor healthcare and utility expenses, and plan for a modest cushion to absorb the elevated service costs that define Yavapai County’s everyday financial reality.

What this means for residents of Yavapai County, Arizona
