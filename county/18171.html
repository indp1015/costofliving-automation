📊 Local Market Analysis

Warren County residents navigate a cost environment roughly 8 percent below the national benchmark, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity (RPP) registering 92.1 for 2023. That aggregate figure masks a sharp bifurcation in daily expenditure patterns: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel sit at 95.6, nearly matching the U.S. average, while services — encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining — plummet to 71.3, the lowest component by a wide margin. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 84.7, translating to a median monthly outlay of $815 against a national median of $928. Because goods command a larger share of frequent, non-discretionary spending than the deeply discounted services category, the practical experience of daily life leans closer to parity than the headline index suggests; a household filling a grocery tank and a prescription basket simultaneously feels the national price level on the former and a steep discount on the latter. The purchasing power calculus reveals a striking surplus. Median household income stands at $74,635, comfortably above the $65,047 national median, and when adjusted by the local RPP, the Purchasing Power Index rises to $81,039.6 — effectively a $6,400 real-income premium over nominal earnings. Rent burden consumes only 13.1 percent of gross income, a fraction of the pressure observed in metropolitan corridors. Local demographics sharpen this picture: a median age of 43.4 years and a bachelor’s attainment rate of just 12.6 percent signal a workforce anchored in skilled trades, manufacturing, and public-sector roles rather than knowledge-economy sectors, while a 4.6 percent unemployment rate suggests a labor market tight enough to support wage growth without overheating. The composition of employment likely reinforces the low services RPP, as healthcare and utility infrastructure costs are distributed across a smaller, older population base with lower utilization intensity than urban centers. Over the past decade, the trajectory has favored the earner. The overall RPP drifted downward from 93.022 in 2010 to 92.097 in 2023, a marginal 1.0 percent decline that signals relative price stability. Meanwhile, nominal median income surged 33.2 percent from $56,042 to $74,635, and median rent increased only 8.2 percent from $753 to $815. Wages have not merely kept pace; they have decoupled from shelter and general inflation, expanding real disposable income at a clip rarely seen in the Midwest. The goods component’s persistence near 95.6 indicates that tradable items — fuel, food, vehicles — remain tethered to national supply chains, limiting further downside on the cost side, but the services discount has proven durable, widening the gap between local and national service-price levels. A prospective resident should budget for a grocery and fuel bill that mirrors the national average, while allocating significantly less for healthcare premiums, utility deposits, and restaurant meals than peers in higher-RPP counties. The $815 median rent leaves ample room in a $6,200 monthly gross budget for transportation, insurance, and savings, provided the household does not import high-cost consumption habits from pricier regions. The primary fiscal risk lies not in shelter or services but in the goods

What this means for residents of Warren County, Indiana

With a Purchasing Power Index of 81039.6, a median household income of $74,635 in Warren County, Indiana has the equivalent buying power of $60,483,905 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 84.7 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
