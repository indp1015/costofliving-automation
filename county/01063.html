📊 Local Market Analysis

Greene County, Alabama, exhibits a cost of living that is markedly below the national benchmark, with an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) index of 89.1 compared to the national average of 100. Goods and services reflect divergent pressures: the goods component registers at 94.6, indicating that everyday items such as groceries, apparel, and fuel are modestly more expensive than the national average, while the services component sits at 61.6, underscoring that healthcare, utilities, and dining are substantially cheaper. Housing costs, measured by rental rates, align closely with the national level at 89.9, suggesting that rent burdens are not a primary driver of the county’s affordability. The data reveal that the local cost structure is dominated by the goods category, which is the highest‑priced component, whereas services provide the greatest discount, shaping daily expenses toward a mixed but generally inexpensive experience. The median household income of $31,495 leaves a notable gap relative to the national median of $65,047, yet the purchasing power index of 35,349.1 indicates that residents can stretch their earnings further in this market. With a median monthly rent of $429, rent consumes 16.3 % of income, a proportion that is well within comfortable limits and reflects a labor market where unemployment is low at 3.3 %. The demographic profile—only 7.5 % of adults holding a bachelor’s degree or higher and a median age of 44.4 years—suggests a workforce concentrated in lower‑skill, potentially lower‑wage sectors, which helps explain the modest income levels while also contributing to the affordability of services. The combination of low unemployment and a relatively young‑ish median age (for a rural county) implies steady demand for basic goods but limited upward pressure on housing costs. Over the past decade, consumer costs have remained relatively stable, with the RPP edging down from 89.783 in 2010 to 89.097 in 2023, a marginal -0.8 % shift that signals little inflationary pressure on overall living expenses. In contrast, median household income has risen sharply, climbing from $20,541 to $31,495, a +53.3 % increase that outpaces the rise in rent, which grew from $343 to $429, a +25.1 % gain. This divergence suggests that wage growth has been the primary driver of improved affordability, allowing residents to allocate a larger share of income to discretionary spending despite the higher relative price of goods. The modest decline in the RPP indicates that the county’s cost environment has not become more expensive, even as incomes have risen. Prospective residents should budget with the expectation that everyday essentials, particularly groceries and fuel, will cost slightly more than the national average, while utilities, healthcare, and dining will be markedly cheaper. A typical household earning the median income can allocate roughly $2,500 per month after covering rent and basic utilities, leaving room for savings or discretionary items. The low rent burden of 16.3 % provides a cushion against unexpected expenses, and the stable cost trends suggest that budgeting assumptions based on current levels are unlikely to shift dramatically. Residents should also anticipate that the local labor market, characterized by low unemployment but limited higher‑education opportunities, may constrain income growth, making the current affordability a function of modest earnings rather than abundant high‑wage employment.

What this means for residents of Greene County, Alabama

With a Purchasing Power Index of 35349.1, a median household income of $31,495 in Greene County, Alabama has the equivalent buying power of $11,133,199 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 89.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
