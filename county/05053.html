📊 Local Market Analysis

Grant County, Arkansas presents a cost-of-living profile significantly below the national baseline, registering an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 86.8 against the national 100 in 2023. This 13.2 percent discount is structurally driven by an exceptionally low Services index of 56.7 — nearly 43 percent below the national average — which encompasses healthcare, utilities, and dining expenses. Goods, including groceries, apparel, and fuel, sit closer to parity at 93.1, while Rental Costs index at 81.9, offering an 18.1 percent discount relative to the broader market. The divergence between the Services component and the other categories indicates that daily operational costs for households are heavily subsidized by local service-sector pricing dynamics, making routine expenditure on non-tradables the primary driver of affordability. Purchasing power metrics confirm that the median household translates this price advantage into tangible fiscal capacity. With a median income of $72,512 — exceeding the national median of $65,047 by 11.5 percent — and a median monthly rent of $779 against a national $928, the rent burden stands at a remarkably low 12.9 percent of gross income. The resulting Purchasing Power Index of 83,529.5 suggests that after accounting for local price levels, the median household commands real resources substantially higher than their nominal earnings imply. This surplus is amplified by the historical trajectory: since 2010, median income has surged 57.1 percent from $46,171 while median rent increased only 12.1 percent from $695, and the overall RPP actually declined 2.0 percent from 88.6. Wage growth has not merely kept pace with expenses; it has dramatically outstripped them, expanding real disposable income over the decade. Local labor market characteristics reinforce this dynamic. An unemployment rate of 4.0 percent signals a tight labor market supporting wage pressure, while a median age of 40.2 years reflects a workforce in prime earning years. However, educational attainment remains a constraint, with only 15.8 percent of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting the income gains are likely concentrated in sectors not requiring advanced credentials — potentially manufacturing, logistics, or skilled trades — rather than knowledge-economy roles. This demographic profile implies a cost structure resilient to inflationary spikes in professional services but potentially vulnerable to goods-price volatility, given the Goods RPP of 93.1 leaves less buffer against national fuel or food shocks. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the asymmetry between cheap services and near-average goods. Housing costs consume a minimal share of income, freeing cash flow for transportation, groceries, and healthcare outlays that track closer to national norms. Utility bills and dining expenses should run notably lower than in metropolitan areas, but vehicle fuel and grocery baskets will feel familiar in price. The critical variable remains the Services index at 56.7; any structural shift in local healthcare provision or utility regulation that narrows this gap would erode the purchasing power advantage faster than rental market adjustments, given the current low rent burden leaves little offsetting room in the shelter category.

What this means for residents of Grant County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 83529.5, a median household income of $72,512 in Grant County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $60,568,911 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
