📊 Local Market Analysis

Power County’s cost‑of‑living index sits at 92.2, meaning the average consumer price level is 7.8 % below the national benchmark of 100. Goods prices are the most elevated component at 95.9, still modestly under the national average but noticeably higher than the county’s overall index. Services register the greatest discount, at 86.9, while rental costs are the cheapest segment with an index of 68.1, indicating that housing expenses are roughly one‑third lower than the national norm. The composition of the local price structure is driven primarily by the relatively high goods index. Grocery bills, apparel purchases, and fuel costs are only 4.1 % above the national average, whereas the steep discount on services and especially on rents pulls the overall index down. Daily life for a typical household therefore feels inexpensive in terms of housing and discretionary services, yet modestly pricier for essential consumables. A median renter pays $729 per month, 21.5 % less than the national median rent of $928, which translates into a rent‑burden of 14.6 % of the median household income of $59,760. This burden is well under the conventional affordability threshold of 30 %, leaving a sizable share of earnings for other expenditures. Disposable income can be gauged through the Purchasing Power Index of 64,790.3, which reflects the amount of goods and services a median household can afford after accounting for local price levels. Compared with the national median income of $65,047, Power County’s median earnings are 8.0 % lower, yet the lower cost of housing and services amplifies real purchasing power. After subtracting the average monthly rent, a median household retains roughly $56,300 annually for food, transportation, health care, and leisure, a figure that outpaces many higher‑cost metros where rent consumes a larger income slice. Demographic factors reinforce the affordability narrative. With only 12.1 % of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, the labor market leans toward lower‑skill occupations that typically command modest wages. The unemployment rate of 5.9 % exceeds the national average, suggesting some slack in the job market that could temper wage growth. A median age of 33.9 years points to a relatively young population, often associated with lower household formation costs and a higher propensity to rent rather than own. These characteristics combine to produce a cost environment where housing remains cheap, but limited educational attainment may constrain earning potential, keeping disposable income modest despite low rents. Over the past decade, the Regional Price Parity (RPP) slipped from 93.2 in 2010 to 92.2 in 2023, a 1.0 % decline, indicating that consumer prices have softened relative to the nation. Median household income, however, surged from $44,779 to $59,760, a 33.5 % increase, outpacing the modest rise in median rent of 2.5 % (from $711 to $729). The divergence between income growth and price stability suggests that wages have more than kept pace with expenses, bolstering real purchasing power for residents. Prospective residents should budget with the knowledge that housing consumes a small fraction of income—approximately 14.6 %—leaving ample room for groceries, fuel, and basic services, which are only slightly above national levels. Allocating roughly 30 % of take‑home pay to food and transportation, 15 % to health care and utilities, and the remainder to discretionary spending would align with the county’s price profile. Monitoring local employment trends remains prudent, as the modestly elevated unemployment rate and lower educational attainment could affect job stability and wage trajectories.

Analysis prepared by the Data in DP Research Team. Based on BEA RPP and Census ACS data.

What this means for residents of Power County, Idaho

With a Purchasing Power Index of 64790.3, a median household income of $59,760 in Power County, Idaho has the equivalent buying power of $38,718,683 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 68.1 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
