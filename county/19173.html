📊 Local Market Analysis

Taylor County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 11.2 percent below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 88.8 for 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals a distinct bifurcation in daily expenses. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — command an index of 96.6, nearly par with the national baseline, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining plummet to 66.0, the lowest component by a wide margin. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 87.3, translating to a median monthly outlay of $707 against a national median of $928. This profile indicates that while the sticker price of tangible goods offers little relief, the substantial discount on labor-intensive services drives the county’s overall affordability advantage. The consumption breakdown confirms that services act as the primary deflationary lever in the local budget, suppressing the aggregate index far more than housing or goods. A median household income of $67,279 exceeds the national median of $65,047, and when adjusted for the local price level, purchasing power rises to an effective $75,791 — a 16.4 percent premium over nominal earnings. Rent burden stands at a remarkably low 12.6 percent of gross income, roughly half the standard 30 percent affordability threshold, freeing significant cash flow for discretionary spending or savings. However, the goods index near parity means trips to the grocery store or gas station deliver minimal geographic arbitrage; the "cheap" daily life is concentrated in haircuts, medical visits, and utility bills rather than at the checkout counter. Demographic and labor market dynamics reinforce this economic picture. An unemployment rate of 3.4 percent signals a tight labor market supporting wage growth, yet educational attainment remains modest with only 11.8 percent of adults holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, and a median age of 43.6 years suggests an established, perhaps aging, workforce. Over the 2010–2023 period, nominal median income surged 52.1 percent from $44,246 while median rent climbed just 19.4 percent from $592, vastly outpacing shelter cost inflation. Simultaneously, the RPP declined 2.6 percent from 91.1 to 88.8, meaning local prices actually fell relative to the national average during a period of broad inflation. Real wage growth has been exceptionally strong, expanding the gap between earnings and the cost of maintaining a standard consumption basket. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the divergence between fixed and variable costs. Housing consumes a trivial share of income, and service expenses — from electricity to primary care — carry a 34 percent discount to the national norm, providing a structural buffer against financial stress. Conversely, the goods basket offers no such cushion; food and fuel budgets should be calibrated to near-national averages. The high purchasing power index implies that a household earning the local median effectively commands upper-quintile consumption capacity relative to the country, provided their consumption pattern weights services and shelter heavily over goods. The data suggests a household budget here is resilient against rent shocks but exposed to national commodity cycles.

What this means for residents of Taylor County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 75791.1, a median household income of $67,279 in Taylor County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $50,991,494 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
