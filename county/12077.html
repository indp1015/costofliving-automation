📊 Local Market Analysis

Liberty County’s cost of living sits modestly above the national benchmark, with the BEA Regional Price Parity (RPP) index at 103.6 versus a baseline of 100. Goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel are priced 2.2 % lower than the national average (98.2), while services—including healthcare, utilities, and dining—are 23.2 % higher (123.2). Rental costs are the most affordable component, registering at 93.7, or 6.3 % below the national level. The composite picture is one of a region where everyday necessities are inexpensive, but the price of consuming services pushes the overall index above parity. The service sector dominates the local cost structure. With a services index of 123.2, residents pay substantially more for health care, electricity, water, and restaurant meals than the average American. By contrast, the goods index of 98.2 indicates that grocery bills and fuel purchases are slightly cheaper, and the housing index of 93.7 shows that renters benefit from lower monthly rents. Median rent of $968 is 4.3 % higher than the national median of $928, yet the rent‑burden figure of 21.6 % of household income signals that housing remains affordable relative to earnings. The higher service costs mean that discretionary spending on dining out or personal care quickly erodes disposable income, while basic sustenance and transportation remain relatively cheap. Median household income in Liberty County stands at $53,824, 17.3 % below the national median of $65,047. After accounting for the average rent of $968, a typical household spends roughly $11,616 annually on housing, leaving $42,208 for all other expenses. The Purchasing Power Index of 51,935.6 translates to a real‑term buying capacity that is about 20 % lower than the national average, reflecting the combined effect of lower wages and higher service prices. Unemployment at 6.8 %—well above the national rate—limits earnings potential, while only 8.5 % of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting a labor market concentrated in lower‑skill occupations that typically command modest pay. The median age of 39.1 years indicates a mature workforce, but the limited educational attainment constrains upward mobility and keeps wage growth modest. Over the past decade, the RPP rose from 100.392 in 2010 to 103.636 in 2023, a 3.2 % increase, whereas median household income climbed from $39,406 to $53,824, a 36.6 % gain. Rent, however, surged from $573 to $968, a 68.9 % jump, outpacing both income and overall price growth. The disparity reveals that while wages have improved substantially, they have not kept pace with the rapid escalation in rental costs, and the modest rise in the overall price index masks the pronounced pressure from service and housing expenses. Prospective residents should budget with a clear hierarchy: allocate a larger share of income to services and health‑related costs, anticipate a rent commitment that, despite being below national parity, consumes over one‑fifth of earnings, and leverage the lower goods prices to stretch discretionary funds. A realistic monthly budget might allocate roughly 30 % to rent, 25 % to utilities and health care, 20 % to food and transportation, and the remaining 25 % to savings, debt service, and occasional leisure. Understanding that service costs are the primary driver of household outlays will help newcomers calibrate their spending patterns and avoid unexpected shortfalls in a county where basic goods are cheap but the price of living well is decidedly higher.
