📊 Local Market Analysis

Western Connecticut Planning Region presents a cost-of-living profile that sits modestly above the national baseline at 104.2 on the BEA Regional Price Parity index, yet this aggregate figure masks a severe structural distortion driven almost entirely by shelter expenses. While residents benefit from goods pricing — groceries, apparel, and fuel — that actually undercuts the national average at 98.6, the services complex runs hot at 116.6, reflecting elevated healthcare, utility, and dining-out costs. The dominant outlier remains rental housing, which registers a staggering 153.2, meaning tenants pay a 53.2 percent premium over the national benchmark for shelter. This single category skews the entire consumption basket, rendering daily life expensive not because of milk or gasoline prices, but because the fixed cost of putting a roof overhead consumes a disproportionate share of the household ledger. The consumption breakdown reveals a bifurcated reality where discretionary spending on goods remains relatively affordable, but non-discretionary services and housing create a high floor for monthly outlays. Median monthly rent of $2,060 absorbs 19.8 percent of the $124,553 median household income, a rent-burden ratio that appears manageable on paper but obscures the pressure on lower-earning quartiles. The Purchasing Power Index of 119,531.5 confirms that, in aggregate, the region’s high wages more than offset the elevated price level, granting the median household roughly $19,500 in additional real purchasing power compared to a national average earner. However, this surplus is concentrated among the 29.8 percent of adults holding bachelor’s degrees or higher, whose earnings power anchors the median; those without such credentials face a far tighter margin between the 5.5 percent unemployment rate and the 153.2 rental index. Historical trends indicate a subtle but meaningful improvement in affordability over the last thirteen years. The overall RPP declined 2.6 percent from 107.0 in 2010 to 104.2 in 2023, suggesting local inflation ran cooler than the national rate. Nominal median income grew 4.7 percent over the period, reaching $124,553 from $118,930, while median rent increased 5.5 percent to $2,060 from $1,952. Although rent growth slightly outpaced income growth on a percentage basis, the absolute dollar gap widened in favor of income, and the declining RPP signals that the real cost of the broader consumption basket — particularly goods and services — has become less burdensome relative to the rest of the country. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the housing-to-income negotiation. A household earning the national median of $65,047 would confront a rent burden exceeding 38 percent at the local $2,060 median, a threshold widely considered unsustainable. Conversely, a household earning the local median retains roughly $8,200 monthly after rent, sufficient to absorb the 116.6 services index and still capitalize on the 98.6 goods discount. The demographic profile — a median age of 41.5 and a labor market with notable slack at 5.5 percent unemployment — suggests a settled, professional workforce where dual-income households are likely the norm, buffering the high fixed costs. The critical variable is not the price of eggs or gasoline, but the lease negotiation; securing housing at or below the $2,060 median is the prerequisite for unlocking the region’s positive real wage advantage.

What this means for residents of Western Connecticut Planning Region, Connecticut

With a Purchasing Power Index of 119531.5, a median household income of $124,553 in Western Connecticut Planning Region, Connecticut has the equivalent buying power of $148,880,069 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 153.2 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
