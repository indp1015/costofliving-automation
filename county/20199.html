📊 Local Market Analysis

Wallace County residents experience a cost of living roughly 10 percent below the national benchmark, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity for 2023 registering 89.9 against a U.S. average of 100. The aggregate figure masks sharp divergence across consumption categories: goods — covering groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — sit at 96.5, only marginally cheaper than the national norm, while services including healthcare, utilities, and dining out plummet to 68.6, the lowest component by a wide margin. Rental costs, indexed at 90.8, track closer to goods than to services, leaving housing as a moderate rather than a deep discount. Daily life is therefore inexpensive primarily because labor-intensive services cost dramatically less than in metropolitan areas, whereas the price of tangible goods remains tethered to national supply chains and transportation logistics. The median household income of $60,263 falls below the national median of $65,047, yet the purchasing power index of 67,027.4 indicates that local wages stretch further once the 89.9 price level is applied. Rent burden stands at a remarkably low 11.5 percent of gross income, with median monthly rent of $578 compared to $928 nationally, freeing substantial cash flow for savings, vehicle ownership, or discretionary spending. Demographic context reinforces this dynamic: an unemployment rate of 1.2 percent signals a tight labor market that supports wage stability, while a median age of 47.2 years and a bachelor's degree attainment rate of 18.5 percent suggest an established, experienced workforce with relatively low educational overhead costs. The labor market's strength likely underpins the modest but positive income growth observed over the past decade. Between 2010 and 2023, the overall RPP declined 3.3 percent from 92.991 to 89.908, meaning the county became incrementally cheaper relative to the nation even as nominal incomes rose 27.5 percent from $47,279 to $60,263. Median rent increased 42.4 percent over the same span, from $406 to $578, outpacing income growth by a meaningful margin and nudging the rent-to-income ratio upward despite its current low level. The divergence suggests that while the service-cost advantage has deepened — pulling the overall index down — housing costs have exerted upward pressure on the household budget, a trend that bears monitoring if rental demand intensifies further. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that mirror national averages, but anticipate significantly lower outlays for healthcare visits, utility bills, and restaurant meals. The 68.6 services index translates into tangible monthly savings on insurance premiums, electricity, and routine medical care, effectively subsidizing the higher relative cost of goods. With rent consuming barely one-ninth of gross income, households gain flexibility to absorb goods-price volatility or allocate funds toward retirement and emergency reserves. The critical variable remains housing supply: should rental demand accelerate, the 42 percent decade-long rent escalation could erode the current affordability cushion faster than income growth can offset it.

What this means for residents of Wallace County, Kansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 67027.4, a median household income of $60,263 in Wallace County, Kansas has the equivalent buying power of $40,392,722 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 90.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
