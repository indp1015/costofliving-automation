📊 Local Market Analysis

Johnson County’s cost‑of‑living index of 86.8 places consumer prices roughly 13 % below the national average, with goods priced at 93.1 (7 % under the U.S. benchmark) and services at a stark 56.7, the lowest component of the regional price profile. Rental costs, measured by the BEA’s housing sub‑index, sit at 81.9, indicating that landlords charge about 18 % less than the national norm. The combined effect is a markedly affordable environment for everyday purchases, especially in health care, utilities and dining, where expenses are nearly half of what a typical American household faces. The dominant driver of Johnson County’s cost structure is the goods category, which, despite being the highest sub‑index, still trails the national level. Services compress the budget dramatically, offsetting the modest premium on groceries, apparel and fuel. Median monthly rent of $714—19.1 % of median household income—reinforces the affordability narrative; renters allocate a smaller share of earnings to shelter than the national median renter, who spends roughly 27 % of income on a $928 rent. Consequently, daily life feels inexpensive, largely because essential services consume far less of the household purse than elsewhere. Disposable income calculations reveal a purchasing‑power index of 51,616.2, translating to roughly $12,400 of real buying capacity after accounting for the county’s lower price levels. With a median household income of $44,808, the median earner retains about $32,400 for discretionary spending, savings and non‑housing necessities. This residual amount exceeds the national median disposable income after cost‑of‑living adjustments, underscoring the relative financial breathing room afforded to residents. Local demographics shape this affordability. The unemployment rate of 4.9 % aligns closely with the national average, suggesting a stable labor market, while only 12.8 % of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, reflecting a workforce concentrated in lower‑skill, lower‑wage sectors. A median age of 39.4 years points to a mature population likely balancing work and family expenses, yet the modest educational attainment limits upward pressure on wages. The labor market thus sustains the current cost equilibrium without generating significant wage inflation. Over the past decade, the regional price parity (RPP) slipped from 88.6 to 86.8, a 2 % decline, while median household income rose 31 % from $34,139 to $44,808. Rental rates increased 18 % in the same period, outpacing the national rent growth but remaining well below the national median. Income growth has outstripped both price and rent inflation, expanding real purchasing power for residents and confirming that wages have kept pace with, and indeed exceeded, cost pressures. Prospective residents should budget around $714 for rent, allocate roughly $400–$500 for combined utilities, health care and dining, and plan for $300–$350 in groceries and personal items. With services consuming less than 60 % of the national price level, the remaining budget can accommodate modest discretionary spending, but limited local high‑skill employment opportunities may constrain long‑term income growth. Careful allocation toward essential services and housing will preserve the county’s inherent cost advantage.

What this means for residents of Johnson County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 51616.2, a median household income of $44,808 in Johnson County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $23,128,187 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
