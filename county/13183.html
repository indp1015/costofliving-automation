📊 Local Market Analysis

Long County residents navigate a cost environment that sits modestly below the national baseline, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 96.5 in 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals distinct pressure points for household budgets. While the aggregate index suggests a 3.5 percent savings relative to the U.S. average, the Goods component—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—runs at 97.7, nearly parity with national levels and standing as the highest cost driver in the local consumption basket. Conversely, Services, including healthcare, utilities, and dining, offer meaningful relief at 88.3, a full 11.7 points below the national benchmark, while Rental Costs sit at 92.3, providing a 7.7 percent discount on shelter compared to the broader market. This structural split creates a bifurcated experience for the median household earning $64,747 annually. The Purchasing Power Index of 67,099.5 indicates that after adjusting for local prices, the typical worker commands roughly $2,050 more in real goods and services than their nominal income suggests, a buffer derived almost entirely from depressed service and housing costs. Rent burden remains low at 17.3 percent of gross income, with the median monthly outlay of $931 tracking almost identically to the national median of $928 despite the lower local income ceiling. However, because Goods consume a disproportionate share of discretionary spending for lower-to-middle income cohorts, the near-parity pricing on fuel and groceries erodes the theoretical advantage granted by cheap services, leaving families with less elastic flexibility than the headline purchasing power figure implies. Demographic undercurrents sharpen this dynamic. An unemployment rate of 5.4 percent exceeds the national norm, signaling slack in the labor market that likely suppresses wage growth, while an educational attainment rate of just 8.0 percent for bachelor’s degrees or higher constrains access to higher-paying remote or professional roles that could arbitrage the low service costs. The median age of 33.6 years suggests a population in prime household formation years, yet the income trajectory—rising 32.5 percent from $48,863 in 2010 to $64,747 in 2023—has barely outpaced the 28.6 percent surge in median rent over the same period. Meanwhile, the overall RPP crept up only 2.0 percent across the decade, moving from 94.6 to 96.5, indicating that local inflation has been relatively contained but income gains have been largely absorbed by shelter costs rather than translating into expanded real consumption. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the trade-off between predictable, low fixed costs and volatile, high variable costs. The 11.7 percent discount on services translates into tangible monthly savings on utilities and healthcare, and the sub-18 percent rent-to-income ratio preserves cash flow for savings or debt service. However, the 97.7 index on goods means a weekly grocery trip or a commute reliant on personal vehicles delivers almost no geographic arbitrage; a household spending $800 monthly on food and fuel realizes roughly $20 less in savings than the national average counterpart. Long-term residents have seen real incomes improve marginally, but the next decade’s affordability will hinge on whether wage growth can decouple from the rental trajectory that has historically tracked income gains at a near 1:1 ratio.

What this means for residents of Long County, Georgia

With a Purchasing Power Index of 67099.5, a median household income of $64,747 in Long County, Georgia has the equivalent buying power of $43,444,913 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 92.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
