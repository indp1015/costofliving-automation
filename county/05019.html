📊 Local Market Analysis

Clark County residents navigate a cost environment where overall consumer prices sit 13.2 percent below the national benchmark, registering a Regional Price Parity of 86.8 in 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals distinct pressure points for household budgets. Goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—represent the highest relative cost component at 93.1, merely 6.9 percent below the national average, while services including healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 56.7, a staggering 43.3 percent discount that drags the aggregate index down. Rental costs occupy a middle ground at 81.9, translating to a median monthly outlay of $734 against a national median of $928. This structure creates a bifurcated reality: discretionary service consumption is remarkably cheap, but the unavoidable weekly trip for groceries and fuel delivers far less relief than the headline index suggests. The median household income of $50,985 lags the national figure of $65,047 by 21.6 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 58,731.7 indicates that local wages stretch further than nominal dollars imply, effectively granting residents roughly $7,747 in additional real purchasing capacity relative to their nominal earnings. Rent burden sits at a manageable 17.3 percent of gross income, well below the 30 percent stress threshold, freeing cash flow for the comparatively expensive goods basket. However, the local labor market context—marked by a 4.4 percent unemployment rate, a youthful median age of 31.9, and a bachelor’s degree attainment rate of only 16.4 percent—suggests an economy tilted toward lower-wage service or production roles rather than high-skill knowledge work, capping the upside for household income growth even as living costs remain suppressed. Over the thirteen-year span from 2010 to 2023, the RPP drifted downward from 88.6 to 86.8, a modest 2.0 percent decline in relative price levels, while median income surged 45.5 percent from $35,031 to $50,985. Rent appreciation tracked at 26.6 percent over the same period, rising from $580 to $734, significantly lagging income growth and improving the rent-to-income ratio substantially. This divergence signals a decade of genuine real wage gains for the median household, a rarity in many comparable non-metro counties, though the low educational attainment ceiling raises questions about the sustainability of that trajectory without structural shifts in the local employment base. A prospective resident should budget for a grocery and fuel bill that feels nearly national in magnitude while enjoying deep discounts on healthcare visits, utility bills, and restaurant meals. The low service costs effectively subsidize the higher goods burden, but the narrow industrial base implied by the education and age demographics means job mobility and wage negotiation leverage remain limited. Households relocating from higher-cost metros will experience immediate relief on fixed shelter and service contracts, yet the marginal dollar saved on rent may be partially offset by the necessity of reliable personal transportation in a rural setting, keeping the 93.1 goods index a persistent factor in the monthly ledger.

What this means for residents of Clark County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 58731.7, a median household income of $50,985 in Clark County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $29,944,357 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
