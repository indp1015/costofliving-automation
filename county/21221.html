📊 Local Market Analysis

Trigg County’s cost of living sits well below the national norm, with the overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) index at 89.9 versus a benchmark of 100. Goods prices are the most elevated component, scoring 94.5, while services are markedly cheaper at 62.9. Housing, measured by rental costs, registers at 80.9, indicating that a typical lease in the county is roughly 19 % less expensive than the average American rental. These figures illustrate a price environment where everyday necessities such as groceries, apparel, and fuel are only modestly higher than the national average, but health care, utilities, and dining out are substantially cheaper, and shelter costs are the most affordable of the three major categories. The consumption structure is driven primarily by the goods segment, which accounts for the highest relative price pressure. Despite this, the low service index and sub‑national housing costs combine to make daily life comparatively inexpensive. A median household earns $54,630 annually, yet the median monthly rent of $802 consumes just 17.6 % of that income—well under the conventional 30 % rent‑burden threshold. After covering rent, a household retains roughly $45,000 of pre‑tax earnings for other expenses. The Purchasing Power Index of 60,774.3 quantifies this advantage: residents can purchase nearly 39 % more with their income than the average American, reflecting the combined effect of lower housing and service costs offsetting the slightly higher goods prices. Demographic characteristics reinforce the modest cost profile. The unemployment rate of 4.1 % aligns closely with the national rate, suggesting a stable labor market, while only 11.3 % of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, indicating a workforce concentrated in lower‑skill, lower‑wage occupations. The median age of 46.6 years points to a mature population, often associated with higher homeownership rates and lower consumption of youth‑oriented services, which further depresses service‑sector spending. The limited educational attainment may constrain wage growth, yet the prevailing employment conditions keep income streams steady enough to sustain the current cost structure. Over the past decade, RPP slipped marginally from 90.433 to 89.89, a 0.6 % decline, while median household income rose 23.9 % from $44,083 to $54,630. In contrast, median rent surged 40.9 % from $569 to $802. Although rent growth outpaced income gains, the absolute rent level remains low relative to the national median of $928, preserving affordability. The slight easing of overall price pressure, combined with robust income growth, indicates that wages have generally kept pace with, and even outstripped, the modest rise in consumer costs. Prospective residents should allocate a larger share of their budget to goods—particularly transportation fuel and grocery bills—than to services or housing. With rent representing less than one‑fifth of median income, a prudent budget might earmark 10–12 % of earnings for utilities and health care, leveraging the 62.9 service index. The remaining discretionary income can accommodate higher‑priced goods without jeopardizing financial stability. Understanding that the primary expense driver is goods pricing, while housing and services remain inexpensive, enables households to plan for a cost structure that is overall favorable compared with national averages.

What this means for residents of Trigg County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 60774.3, a median household income of $54,630 in Trigg County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $33,201,000 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
