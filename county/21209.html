📊 Local Market Analysis

Scott County, Kentucky presents a cost-of-living profile that sits 10.1% below the national benchmark, registering an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 89.9 for 2023, yet the internal architecture of that discount reveals a sharp bifurcation between what residents pay for tangible goods versus intangible services. The Goods index, covering groceries, apparel, and fuel, runs at 94.5—only a modest 5.5% discount to the national average—making everyday consumables the primary driver of the local cost structure. Conversely, the Services index, encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining, collapses to 62.9, a staggering 37.1% below the national level, while Rental Costs settle at 80.9. This configuration indicates that while housing and service-intensive expenditures are deeply discounted, the unavoidable weekly outlay for food and transportation retains a much tighter tether to national pricing, limiting the total relief a household feels at the checkout counter. Purchasing power metrics confirm that median earners capture significant real income gains despite the sticky goods component. The median household income of $83,660 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 28.6%, and when adjusted by the local RPP, the Purchasing Power Index rises to $93,069.3, effectively granting residents a 43.1% real income premium over the national baseline. Rent burden remains exceptionally low at 16.9% of gross income—median rent of $1,181 against the national $928—signaling that shelter costs, while rising, have not yet eroded the disposable income cushion. The labor market supports this stability with a 4.0% unemployment rate and a median age of 36.4, suggesting a workforce in prime earning years, though the 21.2% bachelor’s attainment rate trails national averages, potentially capping wage growth in knowledge-intensive sectors. Historical trends expose a widening fissure between income growth and shelter inflation over the last decade. Since 2010, the overall RPP has barely budged, slipping a marginal 0.6% from 90.4 to 89.9, yet median rent has surged 55.4% from $760 to $1,181, significantly outpacing the 32.7% rise in median income from $63,027. This divergence implies that the county’s affordability advantage is being quietly arbitraged away on the housing front, even as the broader price level stays flat. The services deflation—likely driven by localized healthcare and utility pricing structures—has acted as a counterweight, absorbing some of the rental pressure, but the trajectory suggests shelter is the variable to watch for future budget strain. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on a favorable fixed-cost baseline offset by near-national variable costs. The 19.1% discount on rent and the profound 37.1% discount on services—particularly utilities and healthcare—create a structural surplus in the monthly ledger that many peer counties cannot offer. However, the grocery and fuel bill will look familiar to anyone relocating from a major metro; the 94.5 Goods index means a household cannot "arbitrage" their food budget downward. A family moving from a high-cost state should model their budget assuming housing and insurance savings fund lifestyle upgrades or accelerated savings, not a reduction in total outlays, because the marginal dollar spent at the pump or the supermarket buys roughly the same quantity as it does nationally.

What this means for residents of Scott County, Kentucky
