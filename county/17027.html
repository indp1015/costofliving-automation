📊 Local Market Analysis

Clinton County’s cost of living sits just below the national benchmark, with an overall Relative Purchasing Power (RPP) index of 98.8 against a U.S. average of 100. Prices for goods—groceries, apparel, and fuel—run 1.6 % higher than the nation (101.6), while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are 7.6 % cheaper (92.4). Housing, measured by rental costs, is the most pronounced deviation, registering at 80.4, or roughly 20 % lower than the national level. This mix produces a modestly inexpensive living environment overall, driven primarily by the affordability of rent. The dominant force shaping the county’s price structure is the low housing cost. Median monthly rent stands at $934, only marginally above the national median of $928, yet the rent‑burden metric reveals that renters allocate just 13.6 % of household income to housing, far below the national concern threshold of 30 %. By contrast, the goods index exceeds the national figure, indicating that everyday purchases—food, clothing, gasoline—cost slightly more. Consequently, daily life feels inexpensive in the housing dimension but modestly pricier at the grocery and fuel counter. The net effect is a budget where shelter consumes a small slice of earnings, allowing discretionary spending on higher‑priced goods without overwhelming the household. Disposable income calculations underscore the county’s purchasing strength. Median household income reaches $82,314, outpacing the national median of $65,047 by 26.4 %. After accounting for the local cost structure, the Purchasing Power Index of 83,354.3 suggests that a typical Clinton County household can purchase roughly $18,300 more in goods and services than a median U.S. household with the same nominal income. Subtracting the median rent of $934 leaves $73,380 of annual earnings for all other expenses, a figure that comfortably exceeds the national average after adjusting for the higher goods price index. The low rent burden thus translates into a sizable residual income pool for savings, transportation, or leisure. Demographic and labor‑market characteristics reinforce the affordability narrative. Unemployment rests at 3.3 %, well under the national rate, indicating a tight labor market and stable earnings potential. However, only 17.0 % of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting a workforce concentrated in lower‑skill, potentially lower‑wage occupations. The median age of 41.4 years points to a mature population, likely with established earning power and fewer dependents, which can mitigate the impact of higher goods prices. Together, these factors create a scenario where steady employment and modest household sizes combine with low housing costs to preserve purchasing power despite a slight premium on everyday goods. Over the past decade, the county’s RPP slipped from 101.78 in 2010 to 98.75 in 2023, a 3.0 % decline, indicating that consumer prices have softened relative to the national average. Median household income surged 30.2 % in the same period, rising from $63,236 to $82,314, while median rent climbed 19.9 % from $779 to $934. Income growth outpaced both overall price inflation and housing cost increases, expanding real disposable income and reinforcing the county’s affordability edge. The divergence between faster wage growth and slower price escalation suggests that residents have experienced a genuine improvement in living standards over the last ten years. Prospective residents should anchor budgeting around the low rent figure while allowing for a modest premium on groceries and fuel. With rent consuming roughly one‑eighth of median earnings, allocating 10‑12 % of income to housing provides a comfortable cushion. The remaining budget should prioritize goods, where a 1.6 % price uplift over national averages may require a slightly higher allocation for food and transportation. Given the strong employment backdrop and low unemployment, households can expect stable income streams to support these expenditures. A disciplined approach—capping housing at under 15 % of take‑home pay and monitoring goods spending against the 101.6 index—will enable newcomers to maintain the county’s historically favorable cost‑of‑living advantage.

What this means for residents of Clinton County, Illinois
