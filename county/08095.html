📊 Local Market Analysis

Phillips County registers a composite Regional Price Parity of 101.9, placing overall consumer prices marginally above the national baseline, yet this headline figure masks a profound structural bifurcation in the local cost architecture. Services—encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining—command an index of 130.5, marking the single most expensive component and running 30.5 percent above the U.S. average, while goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel sit at 99.2, essentially at parity. Rental housing provides the primary counterweight at 86.8, a 13.2 percent discount that significantly depresses the aggregate index; consequently, daily life is characterized by cheap shelter and affordable staples but penalizes residents heavily for essential service consumption, a dynamic that distorts the typical household budget toward unavoidable fixed costs like medical care and electricity. The median household income of $60,372 translates to a Purchasing Power Index of 59,271.9, indicating that local prices erode roughly 1.8 percent of nominal earnings, a modest implicit tax compared to high-cost metros but meaningful against a national median income of $65,047. Rent burden stands at a low 18.8 percent, with the median monthly outlay of $947 tracking just above the national median of $928, confirming that housing affordability remains a relative bright spot. The labor market reflects extreme tightness at 0.8 percent unemployment, yet educational attainment is limited—only 18.8 percent of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher—suggesting an economy anchored in sectors that do not require advanced credentials, while a median age of 40.3 years implies a stable, older workforce with potentially fixed income streams sensitive to the elevated services index. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the RPP drifted upward a mere 1.3 percent, from 100.59 to 101.856, signaling remarkable price stability in the broad consumption basket. Nominal income growth of 38.3 percent, rising from $43,639, comfortably outpaced this general inflation, yet median rent surged 60.8 percent from $589, nearly doubling the pace of wage appreciation. This divergence indicates that while the overall cost curve has been flat, the specific trajectory of shelter costs has compressed the housing affordability advantage that once existed, even as the current burden ratio remains manageable by national standards. A prospective resident should calibrate expectations around a budget where the savings on rent—roughly $100 below what the national average would imply for this income level—are largely redirected toward service categories running 30 percent above norm. Healthcare premiums, utility bills, and restaurant meals will absorb a disproportionate share of disposable income, demanding careful scrutiny of insurance networks and energy efficiency. The tight labor market supports wage stability, but the low educational attainment ceiling suggests limited upward mobility for professional-track earners, making the current purchasing power calculus—where real income sits slightly below nominal—likely persistent for the near term.

What this means for residents of Phillips County, Colorado

With a Purchasing Power Index of 59271.9, a median household income of $60,372 in Phillips County, Colorado has the equivalent buying power of $35,783,631 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 86.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
