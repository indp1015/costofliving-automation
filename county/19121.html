📊 Local Market Analysis

Madison County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 11.2 percentage points below the national baseline, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity for 2023 registering 88.8 against a U.S. average of 100. This discount is not distributed evenly across the consumption basket. Goods — covering groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — carry an index of 96.6, nearly converging with national pricing, while services including healthcare, utilities, and dining out plunge to 66.0, the lowest component and the primary driver of the county's affordability advantage. Rental costs, indexed at 87.3, reinforce the discount but sit closer to the goods category than to services, creating a bifurcated environment where discretionary service consumption is remarkably cheap but essential goods demand near-parity spending. The purchasing power calculus confirms the asymmetry. Median household income stands at $89,542, exceeding the national median of $65,047 by 37.7 percent, while the purchasing power index of 100,870.8 signals that the median dollar stretches roughly 13 percent further locally than it would nationally. Rent burden illustrates the practical impact: at $938 monthly against a national median of $928, housing consumes only 12.6 percent of gross income, a threshold that leaves substantial discretionary capacity for savings, debt service, or the goods categories where price relief is minimal. A household earning the local median effectively commands a real income equivalent to roughly $101,000 in national-average purchasing terms. Demographic and labor market fundamentals underpin this dynamic. An unemployment rate of 2.7 percent indicates a tight labor market that supports wage growth, while a median age of 41.0 years suggests a workforce in peak earning years rather than early-career or retirement cohorts. Educational attainment, with 19.3 percent holding a bachelor's degree or higher, trails the national rate but aligns with an economy anchored in sectors that reward tenure and specialized trades over credential density. The labor market's tightness, combined with low service costs, creates a feedback loop where employers face less pressure to compensate for high local service inflation — childcare, eldercare, dining — because those inputs are structurally inexpensive. Over the 2010–2023 window, the RPP declined from 91.139 to 88.769, a 2.6 percent drop in relative price levels, while median income surged 49.1 percent from $60,060 to $89,542. Median rent increased only 14.7 percent, from $818 to $938, lagging income growth by a factor of more than three. This divergence means the median household has absorbed a decade of nominal wage gains with minimal erosion from housing or broad price pressures. For a prospective resident, the budgeting reality is clear: allocate near-national budgets for groceries and fuel, expect deep discounts on healthcare, utilities, and restaurant meals, and anticipate that housing costs will claim a low-single-digit share of income relative to coastal or metropolitan benchmarks. The primary financial friction point remains the goods basket, where the 3.4 percent discount from the national average offers limited relief for unavoidable weekly expenditures.

What this means for residents of Madison County, Iowa
