📊 Local Market Analysis

Henry County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 10.1 percentage points below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 89.9 in 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals a distinct bifurcation in daily expenses. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — run only marginally cheaper than the U.S. baseline at 94.5, meaning a trip to the supermarket or a fill-up at the pump delivers limited savings relative to metropolitan areas. By contrast, the services category, which captures healthcare, utilities, and dining, plunges to 62.9, the lowest component in the local basket and a primary driver of the county’s affordability advantage. Rental costs, indexed at 80.9, reinforce this profile; the median monthly rent of $871 consumes just 17.2 percent of the median household income of $60,736, a burden significantly lighter than the national median rent of $928 against a national median income of $65,047. This consumption breakdown indicates that daily life is structurally inexpensive for service-intensive households but offers less relief for goods-heavy budgets. A retiree or remote worker leveraging local healthcare and utility networks captures the full weight of the 37.1-point services discount, whereas a family allocating a larger share of expenditures to groceries and transportation experiences a cost environment much closer to parity. The resulting Purchasing Power Index of 67,567 suggests the median dollar stretches roughly 11 percent further than nominal income implies, effectively granting residents the consumption capacity of a $67,500 earner in an average-cost jurisdiction. However, the local labor market context tempers this advantage: an unemployment rate of 3.9 percent signals a tight market, yet educational attainment remains low, with only 10.0 percent of adults holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, likely capping wage growth in higher-skilled service sectors. Over the past decade, the affordability gap has widened modestly in favor of residents. The overall RPP drifted downward from 90.4 in 2010 to 89.9 in 2023, a marginal 0.6 percent decline in relative price levels, while median household income surged 30.6 percent from $46,495 to $60,736. Median rent increased a comparatively modest 24.4 percent over the same period, rising from $700 to $871. This divergence indicates that wage growth has outpaced both inflation and shelter costs, improving real purchasing power for incumbent households. The median age of 41.6 years suggests a stable, established population rather than a transient workforce, which may contribute to the contained rental inflation relative to national trends. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the disparity between goods and services. Household budgets should anticipate near-national outlays for food, clothing, and vehicle operation, with limited scope for geographic arbitrage on those line items. Conversely, healthcare premiums, utility bills, and restaurant meals present meaningful savings opportunities. The low rent-to-income ratio of 17.2 percent provides a buffer against goods-price volatility, but the low educational attainment figure implies that significant income advancement may require commuting or remote arrangements tied to external labor markets. The data portrays a locale where the cost of *living* — services and shelter — is distinctly low, while the cost of *consuming* — tangible goods — remains stubbornly aligned with national averages.

What this means for residents of Henry County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 67567.0, a median household income of $60,736 in Henry County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $41,037,493 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
