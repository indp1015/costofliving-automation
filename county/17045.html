📊 Local Market Analysis

Edgar County presents a cost-of-living profile that sits marginally below the national baseline, registering an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 98.8 in 2023, yet this aggregate figure masks a distinct bifurcation in household expenditure patterns. The most pronounced deviation occurs in the rental housing market, where costs sit at just 80.4 on the national index—nearly 20 percent cheaper than the U.S. average—and stand as the primary driver of the county’s affordability advantage. Conversely, the goods category, encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel, runs hot at 101.6, exceeding the national norm and representing the highest cost component in the local basket. Services, including healthcare, utilities, and dining, track significantly lower at 92.4, creating a dynamic where fixed shelter costs are exceptionally low but variable daily necessities carry a slight premium. This consumption breakdown dictates that daily life is structurally inexpensive for households that have secured housing, but the margin for error narrows at the checkout counter. The median monthly rent of $757 consumes only 16.0 percent of the median household income of $56,909, a rent-burden ratio that is remarkably low compared to the national median rent of $928 against a national median income of $65,047. This dynamic translates into a Purchasing Power Index of 57,628.2, indicating that the median worker retains significant disposable income after accounting for local price levels, effectively earning a real wage that stretches further than the nominal dollar figure suggests. However, the 101.6 goods index implies that every trip to the grocery store or gas station erodes that advantage incrementally, requiring careful budgeting for non-discretionary consumables. Local demographics and labor conditions reinforce this affordability narrative while highlighting structural constraints. An unemployment rate of 4.4 percent suggests a reasonably tight labor market, yet the relatively low educational attainment—only 15.1 percent of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher—correlates with the income ceiling observed in the county. The median age of 46.4 years signals an older, likely settled population, which suppresses rental demand pressure and helps maintain the depressed housing cost index of 80.4. The labor market appears stable but lacks the high-wage knowledge sectors that typically drive income growth above the national median, anchoring the county in a low-cost, moderate-income equilibrium. Over the past decade, the trajectory has favored the resident, albeit narrowly. The overall RPP declined from 101.778 in 2010 to 98.752 in 2023, a 3.0 percent improvement in relative affordability, while nominal median income rose 24.6 percent from $45,691 to $56,909. Median rent increased 25.3 percent over the same period, from $604 to $757, nearly matching income growth but starting from a much lower base. This near-parity in growth rates means the exceptional shelter affordability observed today is a preserved condition rather than a new development. For a prospective resident, the budgeting imperative is clear: housing costs will likely remain a minor line item, but the goods premium demands allocation of roughly 2 to 3 percent more of the monthly budget for groceries and fuel than the national average household spends, a friction that compounds over a year of daily consumption.

What this means for residents of Edgar County, Illinois

With a Purchasing Power Index of 57628.2, a median household income of $56,909 in Edgar County, Illinois has the equivalent buying power of $32,795,632 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.4 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
