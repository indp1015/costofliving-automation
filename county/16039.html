📊 Local Market Analysis

Elmore County’s cost of living sits comfortably below the national norm, with the BEA Regional Price Parities index at 92.2 versus the United States benchmark of 100. Goods prices—groceries, apparel, and fuel—register at 95.9, still under the national average but the highest component within the county’s basket. Services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are the cheapest relative to the country, at 86.9, while housing, measured by rental costs, is markedly low at 68.1. This housing index translates into a median monthly rent of $1,111, a figure that exceeds the national median rent of $928 by 19.6 % yet remains far cheaper than many urban markets because the underlying index is well under 100. The expense structure is dominated by the goods sector, which contributes the most to the overall index. Despite the goods index being close to parity with the nation, the pronounced discount in housing pulls the composite cost of living down, making day‑to‑day living relatively affordable. Residents spend a smaller share of income on shelter—rent burden stands at 22.6 % of median household earnings—while the bulk of discretionary spending is directed toward groceries and transportation. The low services index further cushions household budgets, indicating that utilities and medical care cost less than the national average, which benefits families and retirees alike. Disposable income calculations reveal a modest purchasing cushion. The median household income in Elmore County is $58,976, 9.4 % below the national median of $65,047. After deducting the median rent of $1,111 per month ($13,332 annually), the median family retains roughly $45,644 for all other expenses, taxes, and savings. The Purchasing Power Index of 63,940.3—well under the national reference of 100,000—confirms that each dollar stretches less far than in higher‑cost regions, but the lower rent burden partially offsets this shortfall. In practical terms, a household earning the median income can cover essential goods and services with a modest margin, yet discretionary spending remains constrained relative to wealthier metros. Demographic factors shape this economic picture. With only 15.8 % of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, the labor pool leans toward lower‑skill occupations that typically command lower wages, reinforcing the modest median income. The median age of 32.5 years points to a relatively young population, often associated with higher mobility and a greater propensity to rent rather than own. Unemployment at 6.6 % exceeds the national rate, suggesting that job security is a concern and that income volatility may further compress disposable resources. Together, these variables create a cost environment where affordable housing is a key stabilizer, but limited educational attainment and higher unemployment temper overall purchasing power. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped from 93.2 in 2010 to 92.2 in 2023, a 1.0 % decline, indicating that consumer prices have softened slightly relative to the nation. Median household income, however, surged 34.5 % from $43,848 to $58,976, outpacing the 46.8 % rise in median rent ($757 to $1,111). While wages have grown faster than rents, the increase in goods prices—still near national levels—means that the net gain in disposable income is modest. The gap between income growth and overall price parity suggests that residents have benefited from cheaper housing but have not fully offset higher everyday expenses. Prospective residents should anchor their budgets around the $1,111 monthly rent figure and allocate roughly 23 % of income to housing. The remaining 77 % must cover groceries, fuel, utilities, healthcare, and modest entertainment, all of which are priced near or below national averages. A realistic monthly budget might allocate $500–$600 to groceries and fuel, $200 to utilities and healthcare, and $150–$200 to discretionary dining and personal services. With a median income of $58,976, a household can meet these categories without exceeding the typical rent burden, but any deviation—such as higher education costs or a job loss—could quickly erode the thin discretionary margin. Careful tracking of goods expenditures and maintaining the low housing cost advantage are essential for preserving financial stability in Elmore County.

What this means for residents of Elmore County, Idaho

With a Purchasing Power Index of 63940.3, a median household income of $58,976 in Elmore County, Idaho has the equivalent buying power of $37,709,431 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 68.1 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
