📊 Local Market Analysis

Jefferson County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 10.1 percentage points below the national average, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity index registering 89.9 for 2023. The composition of that discount reveals a distinctive pattern: goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel — run only 3.5 percent below the national benchmark at 96.5, while services including healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 68.6, the lowest component by a wide margin. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 90.8, meaning housing consumes a larger share of the local budget than services but remains modest relative to the broader goods basket. Daily life leans inexpensive primarily because service-sector pricing — often the stickiest and largest expenditure category for households — carries a 31.4 percent discount to the national mean, offsetting the near-parity pricing on tangible goods. That pricing environment translates into tangible purchasing power for the median household earning $77,393, a figure that already exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 19 percent. After adjusting for local prices, the purchasing power index rises to 86,080.2, effectively granting residents an 11.2 percent real-income premium over their nominal earnings. Rent burden underscores the advantage: at $844 monthly against a national median of $928, housing claims just 13.1 percent of gross income, a threshold well below the 30 percent stress benchmark. The labor market reinforces this stability — unemployment sits at 2.4 percent, signaling tight demand for labor — though educational attainment remains modest at 15.4 percent holding a bachelor's degree or higher, and a median age of 43.8 suggests an established, possibly aging workforce less likely to churn through high-cost career transitions. Over the past decade, the trajectory has favored earners. The RPP declined from 92.991 in 2010 to 89.908 in 2023, a 3.3 percent drop in relative living costs, while median income surged 35.2 percent from $57,236 to $77,393. Median rent increased only 15.5 percent over the same span, from $731 to $844, meaning wage growth outpaced shelter costs by a factor of more than two. The divergence suggests that local employers have raised compensation faster than landlords or service providers have raised prices, a dynamic that has progressively widened the real-income gap in favor of resident households. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that feel nearly identical to national averages, while expecting significantly lower outlays for healthcare visits, utility bills, and restaurant meals. The rent-to-income ratio leaves substantial monthly surplus for savings, debt reduction, or discretionary spending, but the low educational attainment rate may correlate with limited high-wage job mobility within the county itself. Commuting to nearby metropolitan centers could unlock higher nominal wages, though that introduces transportation costs not captured in the local goods index. The data indicates a household budget where fixed costs are unusually contained, but income growth depends heavily on external labor markets or sector-specific advancement.

What this means for residents of Jefferson County, Kansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 86080.2, a median household income of $77,393 in Jefferson County, Kansas has the equivalent buying power of $66,620,049 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 90.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
