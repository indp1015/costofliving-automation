📊 Local Market Analysis

Marshall County’s cost‑of‑living index of 89.1 places consumer prices roughly 11 % below the national average, signaling a relatively inexpensive environment for everyday spending. Goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—register an index of 94.6, still modestly under the national benchmark but the highest component locally. Services, which include healthcare, utilities, and dining, sit at 61.6, indicating that routine non‑essential expenditures are dramatically cheaper than elsewhere. Rental costs, measured by the BEA’s housing sub‑index, are 89.9, aligning closely with the overall county figure and confirming that housing remains affordable relative to the nation’s median rent of $928; the median monthly rent of $765 translates to just 15.1 % of median household income. The consumption profile is dominated by the goods sector, which lifts the overall index despite the pronounced discount on services. Daily life in Marshall County can be described as inexpensive in terms of discretionary spending—dining out, utility bills, and medical costs are all markedly lower—while essential purchases such as food and fuel remain only slightly below national levels. Consequently, a typical household enjoys a cost structure that leaves more room for non‑essential consumption than many higher‑priced regions, though the modestly higher goods index tempers that advantage. Median household income of $60,946, when juxtaposed with the Purchasing Power Index of 68,404.1, reveals that residents retain roughly 68 % of the purchasing power of an average American household. After accounting for the median rent of $765, a median earner still has approximately $56,000 of annual discretionary income, or about $4,660 per month, to allocate toward goods, services, savings, and other expenses. This residual income is bolstered by the county’s low rent burden of 15.1 %—well under the 30 % threshold often cited as a stress point—allowing households to allocate a larger share of earnings to consumption and savings. Demographically, the county’s labor market is characterized by a 3.7 % unemployment rate and a relatively low educational attainment, with only 14.5 % holding a bachelor’s degree or higher. The median age of 38.6 suggests a mature workforce, likely anchored in sectors that do not demand advanced credentials, which helps keep wage pressures modest. The combination of low unemployment and limited high‑skill labor supply contributes to the modest income growth observed, while also sustaining the low cost of services that depend on locally sourced labor. Over the past decade, the Regional Price Parity slipped from 89.8 to 89.1, a 0.8 % decline, indicating that price growth has been outpaced by national inflation trends. In contrast, median household income surged 56.3 % from $38,983 to $60,946, and median rent rose 28.8 % from $594 to $765. Income gains have therefore outstripped both price and rent increases, expanding real purchasing power for residents. Prospective movers should budget around $765 for monthly rent, allocate roughly $300–$350 for utilities and healthcare, and expect grocery and fuel costs to be about 5 % higher than the national average for comparable items. With a rent burden well below the national norm and a strong surplus after housing costs, households can comfortably cover essential expenses while retaining meaningful discretionary income for savings or lifestyle choices.

Analysis prepared by the Data in DP Research Team. Based on BEA RPP and Census ACS data.

What this means for residents of Marshall County, Alabama

With a Purchasing Power Index of 68404.1, a median household income of $60,946 in Marshall County, Alabama has the equivalent buying power of $41,689,563 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 89.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
