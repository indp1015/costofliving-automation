📊 Local Market Analysis

Barren County residents navigate a cost structure that sits 10.1 percentage points below the national average, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity (RPP) registering 89.9 for 2023. This overall discount masks significant divergence across consumption categories: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel run only 5.5 percent below the national baseline at 94.5, while services—encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining—plummet to 62.9, a staggering 37.1 percent discount that serves as the primary engine of local affordability. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 80.9, roughly 19 percent cheaper than the U.S. average, but the services component is the definitive driver of the county’s low composite index, effectively subsidizing daily life for households that consume heavily in healthcare and utility categories. The median household income of $49,171 trails the national median of $65,047 by 24.4 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 54,701.3 reveals that the local price level more than compensates for the nominal wage gap, granting the median earner roughly 11.2 percent more real purchasing power than the raw income figure suggests. Rent burden stands at a manageable 19.1 percent of gross income, with the median monthly rent of $784 comparing favorably against the national $928, leaving substantial disposable income for the goods basket that remains relatively expensive at 94.5. However, the local labor market context—marked by a 4.8 percent unemployment rate and an educational attainment rate of just 11.1 percent for bachelor’s degrees or higher—suggests that wage growth is constrained by a skills mismatch, limiting the ability of households to leverage that purchasing power into wealth accumulation or absorb shocks in the higher-priced goods category. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the RPP drifted marginally lower from 90.4 to 89.9, a negligible 0.6 percent decline indicating stable relative price levels, while nominal median income surged 28.1 percent from $38,370 to $49,171. That income growth, however, lagged behind the 32.0 percent increase in median rent, which climbed from $594 to $784, signaling a slow but persistent erosion of housing affordability within the broader low-cost environment. The divergence implies that while the services discount remains a durable structural advantage, the rental component is absorbing a growing share of household budgets, a trend that bears monitoring for renters renewing leases in a market where wage gains are not fully translating into shelter cost coverage. A prospective resident should budget for a daily expense profile where the checkout lane at the grocery store and the gas pump feel nearly identical to national prices, but the monthly utility bill, doctor’s visit, and restaurant meal carry deep discounts. The 19.1 percent rent-to-income ratio provides breathing room, yet the 94.5 goods index means that inflation in food and fuel—categories where substitution is difficult—hits Barren County wallets with nearly full force. With a median age of 40.1, households here are likely balancing peak earning years against rising healthcare consumption, making the 62.9 services index a critical, though often invisible, buffer against the higher cost of tangible goods.

What this means for residents of Barren County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 54701.3, a median household income of $49,171 in Barren County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $26,897,176 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
