📊 Local Market Analysis

Wrangell City and Borough presents a cost of living profile that sits modestly above the national baseline, with a 2023 Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 103.3, yet the aggregate figure masks a sharp bifurcation in household expenditure categories. Rental costs constitute the primary inflationary pressure, registering an index of 113.3—over thirteen points above the national average—while goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel run only slightly hot at 103.7. Conversely, the services basket, encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining, offers a distinct discount at 96.7, making Wrangell one of the rare Alaskan municipalities where service-level consumption falls below the U.S. mean. This structure renders daily life expensive for households allocating a large share of income to shelter, but relatively manageable for those whose budgets weight heavily toward utilities and medical care. The median household income of $64,545 tracks nearly identically with the national median of $65,047, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 62,484.9 reveals that local prices erode roughly $2,060 of real annual earning capacity. Rent burden stands at 19.5 percent, a figure that appears moderate only because the denominator includes homeowners without mortgage payments; for the renter cohort facing a median monthly outlay of $1,047 against a national median of $928, the squeeze is tighter. Demographic undercurrents amplify this dynamic: a median age of 44.3 years and a bachelor’s degree attainment rate of just 14.4 percent suggest a workforce concentrated in trades, fishing, and public administration rather than high-wage knowledge sectors. The 3.5 percent unemployment rate signals a tight labor market, but the occupational mix likely caps wage growth potential for the median earner. Over the 2010–2023 period, the overall RPP barely budged, inching from 103.099 to 103.297, a nominal 0.2 percent rise that implies broad price stability. Beneath that stability, however, nominal median income surged 32.8 percent from $48,603 while median rent accelerated 43.0 percent from $732, outpacing income growth by a full ten percentage points. This divergence indicates that while the general consumption basket has held steady, the shelter component has absorbed a disproportionate share of household wage gains, effectively neutralizing a significant portion of the decade’s nominal income improvement for renters. A prospective resident should budget for a rental market that commands a roughly twelve percent premium over the national median, offset partially by utility and healthcare costs that run three to four percent below average. Grocery and fuel bills will track slightly above Lower 48 benchmarks, reflecting supply-chain friction common to Southeast Alaska. The critical budgeting variable remains lease negotiation: with rent consuming nearly one-fifth of gross median income and historical rent appreciation outstripping wage growth, housing security requires either income significantly above the local median or a willingness to accept higher shelter-cost ratios than the aggregate data suggests.

What this means for residents of Wrangell City and Borough, Alaska

With a Purchasing Power Index of 62484.9, a median household income of $64,545 in Wrangell City and Borough, Alaska has the equivalent buying power of $40,330,879 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 113.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
