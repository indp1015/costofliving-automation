📊 Local Market Analysis

Fremont County presents a cost of living profile that sits 11.2 percent below the national benchmark, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 88.8 for 2023. This aggregate figure masks significant divergence across consumption categories: goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel — run at 96.6, nearly parity with the U.S. average, while services including healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 66.0, the lowest component by a wide margin. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 87.3. Consequently, daily life is inexpensive relative to the nation, but the discount is driven almost entirely by service-sector pricing rather than the tangible goods households purchase weekly. A resident filling a prescription or hiring a contractor captures substantial savings, yet the grocery bill and fuel tank reflect mainstream national pricing pressures. Purchasing power dynamics reinforce this advantage. The median household income of $73,750 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 13.4 percent, while median rent of $753 falls 18.9 percent below the $928 national figure, yielding a rent burden of just 12.3 percent of gross income. The Purchasing Power Index of 83,080.8 quantifies the real value of local earnings after adjusting for the 88.8 RPP, indicating that the median dollar stretches considerably further here than in higher-cost metros. Local labor market conditions amplify this effect: a 1.0 percent unemployment rate signals extreme labor scarcity, which typically exerts upward pressure on wages, yet the educational attainment profile — only 17.2 percent of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher — suggests the wage premium may be concentrated in specific sectors rather than broadly distributed across knowledge-economy roles. The median age of 46.4 years further implies a workforce skewed toward established, peak-earning households rather than early-career entrants. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the county’s cost trajectory has decoupled favorably from income growth. The RPP declined 2.6 percent from 91.139 to 88.769, meaning the area became marginally cheaper in real terms even as national inflation accumulated. Simultaneously, median household income surged 38.3 percent from $53,324 to $73,750, outpacing the 23.8 percent rise in median rent from $608 to $753. Wages have not merely kept pace with shelter costs; they have widened the gap substantially. This divergence suggests that the primary inflationary risk for residents lies not in housing — which remains deeply affordable — but in the goods basket that tracks national indices closely at 96.6. For a prospective resident budgeting daily expenses, the calculus centers on category-specific exposure. Housing consumes a negligible share of income, freeing cash flow for transportation, food, and healthcare. However, the goods index near parity means savings on rent are partially offset by grocery and fuel bills that mirror coastal prices. The services discount at 66.0 provides a structural buffer for healthcare utilization and utility costs, a critical factor given the older demographic profile. Budgeters should anticipate that discretionary spending power is high, but the marginal cost of consumption goods offers no geographic arbitrage.

What this means for residents of Fremont County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 83080.8, a median household income of $73,750 in Fremont County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $61,272,090 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
