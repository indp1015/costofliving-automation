📊 Local Market Analysis

Chautauqua County sits 10.1 percentage points below the national price level with an overall Regional Price Parity of 89.9, though that aggregate masks sharp divergence across consumption categories. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — run only 3.5 percent below the U.S. average at 96.5, making everyday retail purchases nearly as expensive as in metropolitan areas. Services tell a different story: healthcare, utilities, and dining out carry a 68.6 index, a full 31.4 points below the national baseline and the single largest driver of the county's affordability advantage. Rental costs settle at 90.8, modestly discounted but not dramatically so, meaning shelter consumes a larger share of the local budget than the service discount alone would suggest. The median household income of $54,592 trails the national median of $65,047 by 16.1 percent, yet the purchasing power index of 60,719.8 indicates that after adjusting for local prices, the typical resident commands roughly $6,128 more in real goods and services than the nominal income implies. Rent of $713 claims just 15.7 percent of gross income, well under the 30 percent burden threshold and substantially lighter than the national median rent of $928. That cushion, however, rests on a labor market showing strain: a 6.3 percent unemployment rate exceeds typical full-employment levels, while only 13.5 percent of adults hold a bachelor's degree or higher, limiting access to higher-wage service sectors. The median age of 45.3 years signals an older workforce with potentially fixed incomes, amplifying the importance of low service costs — particularly healthcare and utilities — for household stability. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP fell from 92.991 to 89.908, a 3.3 percent decline in relative living costs, while median income surged 43.7 percent from $37,993 to $54,592. Rent rose only 17.3 percent from $608 to $713, meaning real housing affordability improved markedly even as nominal shelter costs climbed. Wage growth has comfortably outpaced both inflation and the modest appreciation in local price levels, expanding the real consumption frontier for incumbent households. The divergence between goods inflation (sticky near national levels) and service deflation (deep and persistent) suggests the county's affordability rests on structural factors — lower labor costs in healthcare, limited dining-out infrastructure, regulated utility rates — rather than cyclical conditions. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that mirror national averages, with limited ability to substitute cheaper alternatives given the 96.5 goods index. The 68.6 services index translates to tangible savings on electricity, water, medical visits, and restaurant meals, but also signals thinner provider networks and longer travel times for specialized care. Rent at $713 leaves roughly $3,836 per month before taxes for a median earner, though the 6.3 percent unemployment rate warrants maintaining a larger emergency reserve than in tighter labor markets. Households relocating from higher-cost regions will experience an immediate boost in discretionary capacity, provided their income is portable or sourced remotely; those dependent on local wages should calibrate expectations to an economy where educational attainment and job density constrain upward mobility.
