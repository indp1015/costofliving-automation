📊 Local Market Analysis

Franklin County’s cost‑of‑living index of 92.2 places consumer prices 7.8 percent below the national benchmark of 100, indicating a generally affordable environment for everyday spending. The goods component registers at 95.9, still under the national average but the highest of the three local categories, while services sit at 86.9 and rental housing is the most inexpensive at 68.1. In concrete terms, the median monthly rent of $831 is 10.5 percent lower than the national median of $928, reinforcing the view that shelter costs are the primary driver of the county’s affordability. The composition of expenses reveals that housing exerts the strongest downward pressure on the overall cost structure. With rents at 68.1 percent of the national level, a household allocating $831 to rent consumes only 15.1 percent of its median income of $65,991, well beneath the 30 percent rent‑burden threshold commonly cited as a stress point. Goods, at 95.9, are the only segment approaching parity with national prices, suggesting that groceries, apparel and fuel are only modestly cheaper. Services, at 86.9, are markedly lower, meaning healthcare, utilities and dining out cost roughly 13 percent less than elsewhere in the United States. The net effect is a daily life that feels inexpensive, especially in housing, while basic consumables remain close to national norms. Purchasing power in Franklin County is quantified by an index of 71,545.8, reflecting the amount of goods and services a median household can acquire after covering essential costs. Subtracting the $831 rent from the $65,991 median income leaves $65,160 in annual discretionary earnings, or roughly $5,430 per month. Compared with the national median income of $65,047, the county’s residents enjoy a marginally higher earnings base while paying less for shelter, resulting in a net disposable income advantage of about $5 percent. This surplus is amplified by the low unemployment rate of 1.8 percent, which signals a tight labor market and steady wage pressure, albeit tempered by a modest 15.2 percent share of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher. The relatively young median age of 32.5 suggests a workforce in early to mid‑career stages, likely prioritizing rental housing and cost‑conscious consumption. Over the past decade, the regional price‑performance (RPP) index slipped from 93.2 to 92.2, a 1.0 percent decline, while median household income surged from $48,133 to $65,991, a 37.1 percent increase. Rental costs rose from $691 to $831, a 20.3 percent jump, yet remained well below national rent growth rates. The disparity between income growth and price growth indicates that wages have outpaced consumer expenses, expanding real purchasing power and reinforcing the county’s affordability narrative. Prospective residents should structure budgets around the low housing cost, allocating roughly 15 percent of income to rent and directing the remaining 85 percent toward goods, services and savings. With goods priced near national levels, a typical grocery bill will mirror broader U.S. averages, while utilities and healthcare will be noticeably cheaper. Given the modest rent burden, households can comfortably absorb modest increases in other categories without jeopardizing overall financial stability. Planning for a monthly discretionary pool of $4,500–$5,000 after rent will accommodate typical consumption patterns and preserve a cushion for savings or occasional discretionary spending.
