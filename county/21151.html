📊 Local Market Analysis

Madison County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 10.1 percentage points below the national average, reflected in an overall Regional Price Parity of 89.9, yet the composition of that discount reveals distinct pressure points for household budgets. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel — represent the highest local cost component at 94.5, merely 5.5 points below the national baseline, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining plunge to 62.9, a staggering 37.1-point discount that drags the aggregate index down. Rental costs sit at 80.9, roughly 19 points cheaper than the national average, but the relatively elevated goods index means daily consumables erode the savings generated by cheap services and housing, creating a bifurcated experience where filling a prescription or dining out feels inexpensive, but the weekly grocery run and commute costs remain stubbornly close to national levels. The median household income of $62,407 trails the national median of $65,047 by 4.1 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 69,426 indicates that local wages stretch further than nominal figures suggest once the 89.9 RPP is applied. Rent burden stands at a manageable 16.4 percent of income — the median monthly rent of $851 compares favorably to the $928 national figure — leaving substantial disposable income for other categories. However, the local labor market context complicates this picture: a 5.3 percent unemployment rate exceeds typical full-employment thresholds, and only 20.3 percent of adults hold a bachelor's degree or higher, suggesting a workforce concentrated in sectors where wage growth may lag inflation in goods. The median age of 34.7 signals a population in prime earning and family-formation years, amplifying demand for the very goods category where local prices sit nearest to parity. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the RPP declined marginally from 90.4 to 89.9, a 0.6 percent drop that signals relative price stability, while median income surged 47.2 percent from $42,390 to $62,407, outpacing the 31.5 percent climb in median rent from $647 to $851. This divergence indicates that wage growth has comfortably exceeded both shelter and broad consumer cost inflation over the decade, a rare dynamic that has improved real living standards for incumbent households. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the goods-services split: allocate roughly 95 cents on the dollar for groceries, fuel, and household supplies compared to urban centers, but expect healthcare, utilities, and restaurant meals to cost roughly 63 cents on the dollar. The low rent burden — well below the 30 percent stress threshold — provides a buffer, yet the elevated unemployment rate and modest educational attainment imply that income volatility remains a greater risk to household solvency than cost-of-living spikes.

What this means for residents of Madison County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 69426.0, a median household income of $62,407 in Madison County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $43,326,684 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
