📊 Local Market Analysis

Harrison County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 7.9 percent below the national average, reflected in an overall Regional Price Parity of 92.1, yet the composition of that discount reveals sharp asymmetries across spending categories. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — run only 4.4 percent cheaper than the U.S. baseline at 95.6, making everyday consumables the most expensive relative component of the local budget. Rental housing costs follow at 84.7, a 15.3 percent discount that translates to a median monthly rent of $861 versus the national $928. The dominant deflationary force, however, is services, which clock in at 71.3 — a staggering 28.7 percent below average — driven by below-market healthcare, utility, and dining-out prices. This configuration means daily life is structurally inexpensive for service-intensive households but offers limited relief on the tangible goods that dominate weekly shopping trips. The median household income of $72,468 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 11.4 percent, and when adjusted for the local price level, the purchasing power index rises to $78,687, effectively granting the typical earner roughly $13,600 in additional real buying capacity compared to a national benchmark. Rent burden sits at a remarkably low 14.3 percent of gross income, well under the 30 percent stress threshold, freeing cash flow for savings, transportation, or discretionary spending. The labor market reinforces this stability: a 3.8 percent unemployment rate signals tight conditions, while a median age of 43.0 and a bachelor's attainment rate of just 14.9 percent suggest an economy anchored in skilled trades, manufacturing, and service roles rather than high-wage knowledge sectors. That demographic profile likely suppresses wage growth ceilings but also dampens cost-push inflation in housing and premium services. Over the 2010–2023 window, the RPP drifted marginally lower from 93.0 to 92.1, a 1.0 percent decline indicating that local consumer prices have not merely lagged national inflation but have slightly decoupled in the household's favor. Meanwhile, nominal median income surged 39.0 percent from $52,128 to $72,468, outpacing the 28.7 percent climb in median rent from $669 to $861. Real income growth, therefore, has been robust, with wages expanding roughly 10 percentage points faster than the largest recurring household expense. The goods category's resistance to disinflation — holding near 96 — implies that supply-chain-sensitive items have not shared in the broader cost advantage, a dynamic that could pressure budgets if energy or food commodity cycles turn upward. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that feel nearly identical to metropolitan areas, while capturing meaningful savings on rent, healthcare copays, and utility bills. The 14.3 percent rent-to-income ratio allows unusual flexibility: a household earning the local median could absorb a $200 monthly rent increase and remain below 20 percent burden. However, the low educational attainment rate and older median age correlate with limited high-income trajectory, so long-term financial planning should not assume rapid earnings escalation. The services discount is a structural feature, not a temporary anomaly, making Harrison County a rare environment where outsourcing household labor — childcare, repairs, dining — remains genuinely affordable relative to earned income.

What this means for residents of Harrison County, Indiana

With a Purchasing Power Index of 78686.6, a median household income of $72,468 in Harrison County, Indiana has the equivalent buying power of $57,022,605 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 84.7 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
