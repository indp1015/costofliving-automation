📊 Local Market Analysis

Worth County’s cost of living sits at 96.5 on the BEA RPP scale, meaning consumer prices are roughly 3.5 % below the national benchmark of 100. The goods component leads the local price structure at 97.7, while services are markedly cheaper at 88.3 and rental costs sit near parity at 92.3. Because everyday items such as groceries, apparel, and fuel command a higher index than the national average, a typical household experiences relatively elevated expenses for routine consumption, offsetting the savings found in healthcare, utilities, and dining out. The modest premium in goods suggests that while housing is not a burden, the day‑to‑day purchasing power of residents is eroded by the cost of essentials. Median household income of $57,201 falls short of the national median of $65,047, yet the rent burden remains manageable at 17.9 % of earnings, with a median rent of $855 compared with $928 nationally. The Purchasing Power Index of 59,279.3 reflects the adjusted income after local price adjustments, indicating that a resident’s real spending capacity is about 59 % of the national baseline. This figure underscores that despite lower overall prices, the combination of sub‑median earnings and a relatively high goods index reduces disposable income, leaving households with limited flexibility for discretionary spending. The local demographic profile reinforces the cost dynamics. An unemployment rate of 6.4 % exceeds the national norm, while only 7.9 % of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, a constraint that likely limits higher‑paying employment opportunities. The median age of 43 years suggests a population skewed toward established households, which may prioritize stable, lower‑rent housing and essential consumption over lifestyle upgrades. These factors collectively shape a labor market where wage growth is modest relative to the rising cost of living. Over the past decade, the RPP has risen by just 2.0 % (from 94.574 to 96.494), whereas median income has surged 50.6 % and median rent has climbed 36.4 %. The disparity shows that earnings have outpaced the overall cost‑of‑living index, but rent increases have been more pronounced than income gains, narrowing the margin for housing affordability. While the broader cost structure remains relatively flat, the upward pressure on rental prices suggests that future budget planning should allocate a larger share of income to housing. Prospective residents should budget for a goods‑heavy expense profile, allocating roughly 20‑25 % of a $57,201 household income to groceries, clothing, and fuel, while recognizing that services such as healthcare and utilities are comparatively inexpensive. A monthly rent of $855 should fit comfortably within the 18 % rent‑burden threshold, leaving room for savings only if discretionary spending is controlled. Understanding these nuances enables households to anticipate that daily life, though not prohibitively expensive, demands careful management of essential consumption to preserve disposable income.
