📊 Local Market Analysis

Bell County’s cost of living sits well below the national norm, with the overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) index at 89.9 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices are the most elevated component, registering 94.5, while services are markedly cheaper at 62.9. Housing costs, measured by median monthly rent of $617, translate to a housing index of 80.9, again under the national average rent of $928. The combined effect is a consumer price environment that is roughly 10 % cheaper than the countrywide benchmark, driven chiefly by low service and housing expenses. The consumption structure is dominated by goods, which account for the highest relative price level in the county. Although groceries, apparel, and fuel cost nearly 5 % above the national average, the steep discount in services—healthcare, utilities, and dining—offsets this pressure, keeping everyday life affordable. Rent consumes 22.8 % of median household income, a figure that aligns with the national rent‑burden threshold of 30 % and underscores the relative ease of covering housing. Consequently, residents experience a mixed cost profile: essential consumables are modestly pricier, but the bulk of daily expenditures—utility bills, medical visits, and restaurant meals—are substantially cheaper, yielding a net cost advantage for most households. Disposable income calculations reveal a constrained purchasing environment despite the low price levels. The median household income of $32,403 is less than half the national median of $65,047. After allocating $617 to rent, the remaining $31,786 annually (or $2,648 monthly) must cover all other expenses. The Purchasing Power Index of 36,047.4 quantifies this shortfall: residents can purchase roughly 36 % of the goods and services that an average American can with the same nominal income. In real terms, the median earner retains only about $2,031 per month after rent, which must stretch across groceries, transportation, and the very low‑priced services that define the local economy. Demographic factors amplify the modest purchasing power. An unemployment rate of 10.5 %—well above the national average—restricts labor‑market earnings, while only 8.9 % of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, limiting access to higher‑paying occupations. The median age of 41.2 years suggests a mature workforce, yet the combination of limited educational attainment and elevated joblessness depresses wage growth. These conditions reinforce the reliance on low‑cost services and housing to maintain household solvency. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped from 90.433 to 89.89, a marginal 0.6 % decline, indicating a stable price environment. Median income, however, surged 44.4 % from $22,443 to $32,403, outpacing the 29.1 % rise in median rent ($478 to $617). While wages have grown faster than housing costs, they still lag far behind national earnings, leaving residents with limited discretionary spending power despite the modest price landscape. Prospective residents should budget tightly around the $617 monthly rent, allocate roughly $300–$350 for utilities and healthcare (reflecting the low services index), and expect grocery bills to hover near national averages due to the 94.5 goods index. With a median disposable income of just over $2,000 after rent, a realistic monthly budget would reserve $600–$700 for food, transportation, and personal expenses, leaving little room for savings or non‑essential spending. Understanding this tight margin is essential for anyone considering relocation to Bell County.

What this means for residents of Bell County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 36047.4, a median household income of $32,403 in Bell County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $11,680,439 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
