📊 Local Market Analysis

Moffat County presents a cost-of-living profile that sits marginally above the national baseline, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 101.9 in 2023, yet this aggregate figure masks a profound structural divergence in household expenditure patterns. The composite index is pulled upward almost exclusively by the services sector, which clocks in at 130.5—thirty percent above the national average—reflecting steep premiums for healthcare, utilities, and dining that residents cannot easily substitute. Conversely, the goods basket, encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel, runs slightly below parity at 99.2, while rental housing costs represent a significant discount at 86.8. This configuration creates a distinctive "barbell" economy where the fixed costs of daily survival—keeping the lights on and accessing medical care—are expensive, but the variable costs of consumption and shelter remain relatively affordable. The services component acts as the primary driver of the local cost structure, effectively neutralizing the savings generated by cheaper rents and goods. For the median household earning $70,975 annually, the purchasing power index of $69,681.7 confirms that the typical worker retains roughly 98 cents of real value for every nominal dollar earned, a modest erosion compared to the national median income of $65,047. Housing affordability stands out as a genuine bright spot; median monthly rent of $1,051 consumes only 17.8 percent of gross income, a figure well below the standard 30 percent burden threshold and significantly lighter than the national median rent of $928 would imply for a lower income base. This low shelter-cost ratio frees up cash flow for the inflated service categories, meaning a household’s budget elasticity depends heavily on health status and energy consumption rather than lease terms. Demographic and labor market fundamentals reinforce this dynamic. An unemployment rate of 4.0 percent suggests a reasonably tight labor market, yet the low educational attainment rate—only 15.6 percent of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher—implies a workforce concentrated in sectors that may not command wages indexed to the high service-cost environment. The median age of 37.9 years indicates a population in prime working years, likely supporting dependents, which amplifies the impact of elevated healthcare and childcare costs embedded in the services index. The labor market appears sufficient to maintain occupancy and income levels, but the wage distribution likely clusters around the median, leaving limited room for households to absorb the 30.5 percent service premium without trade-offs in discretionary spending. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the overall RPP drifted up a mere 1.3 percent, signaling broad price stability, yet the underlying components shifted aggressively. Median household income rose 38.1 percent, from $51,387 to $70,975, while median rent accelerated 45.8 percent, from $721 to $1,051. Although rent growth outpaced income growth, the starting base was low enough that the rent-to-income ratio remains manageable. The critical risk for prospective residents lies not in the headline inflation rate but in the opacity of service pricing; unlike rent or gasoline, healthcare and utility costs are often non-negotiable and opaque. A household budgeting for relocation should anchor projections on the 130.5 services index—allocating roughly 30 percent more than the national average for insurance premiums, electricity, and professional services—while treating the 86.8 housing index as a genuine offset that lowers the breakeven threshold for monthly cash flow.
