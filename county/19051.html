📊 Local Market Analysis

Davis County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 11.2 percentage points below the national baseline, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 88.8 for 2023. This discount is not uniform across the consumption basket; goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—run only 3.4 percent below the national average at 96.6, while services including healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 66.0, a staggering 34 percent discount. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 87.3. The data reveals that daily life is structurally inexpensive primarily because the service economy—labor-intensive sectors like personal care, medical administration, and hospitality—is priced dramatically lower than the national norm, whereas the tangible goods residents buy at retail retain near-parity pricing with larger metros. Purchasing power metrics confirm that the median household captures significant real income gains from this disparity. With a median income of $79,505—22.2 percent above the national median of $65,047—and a Purchasing Power Index of 89,563.9, the typical household commands roughly $24,500 more in real purchasing capacity than the national average suggests. Housing costs consume a mere 13.3 percent of gross income at a median rent of $878, well below the national median of $928 and a fraction of the 30 percent burden threshold. This low shelter burden, combined with cheap services, frees substantial discretionary cash flow for savings, debt reduction, or goods consumption where prices are stickier. The demographic and labor backdrop reinforces this affordability profile. An unemployment rate of 3.5 percent signals a tight labor market supporting wage growth, yet educational attainment remains modest at 12.5 percent holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting the local wage premium is driven by labor scarcity in blue-collar and service sectors rather than a high-skill knowledge economy. The median age of 36.2 indicates a workforce in prime earning years, likely supporting household formation without the extreme rental competition seen in university towns or tech hubs. This composition suggests the cost advantage is structural, rooted in lower service-sector wage floors and land costs, rather than a temporary cyclical dip. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP drifted downward from 91.1 to 88.8, a 2.6 percent decline in relative price levels, while nominal median income surged 62.6 percent from $48,888 to $79,505. Median rent increased 50.9 percent over the same span, from $582 to $878, lagging income growth by a significant margin. This divergence indicates that wage appreciation has substantially outpaced both the general cost of living and shelter costs specifically. For a prospective resident, the budgeting implication is clear: the monthly nut for housing and services—utilities, healthcare, local amenities—will feel remarkably light relative to income, but the grocery bill and fuel costs will feel familiar, tracking close to national averages. The household budget here benefits from a rare alignment where the largest fixed expense (shelter) and the most frequent variable expenses (services) are both deeply discounted.

What this means for residents of Davis County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 89563.9, a median household income of $79,505 in Davis County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $71,207,779 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
