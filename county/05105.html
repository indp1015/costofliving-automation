📊 Local Market Analysis

Perry County residents navigate a cost environment registering 13.2 percent below the national average, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity for 2023 settling at 86.8. This aggregate figure masks significant internal divergence: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel carry a parity of 93.1, nearly converging with national pricing, while services—including healthcare, utilities, and dining—plummet to 56.7, representing the county’s most profound discount. Rental costs sit at 81.9, offering meaningful shelter savings relative to the broader U.S. market where median rent reaches $928, compared to the local $734. The data indicates that daily life is structurally inexpensive primarily because the service economy is deeply discounted, whereas the unavoidable weekly basket of goods remains stubbornly close to national levels. The median household income of $60,078 trails the national median of $65,047 by roughly 7.6 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 69,206.3 reveals that the lower price level more than compensates for the income gap, granting the median earner approximately $4,159 in additional real purchasing capacity. Rent burden stands at a remarkably low 14.7 percent of gross income, a figure that frees substantial cash flow for transportation, food, and healthcare relative to metropolitan peers. However, the goods parity of 93.1 implies that every trip to the supermarket or gas station erodes this advantage; a household spending $800 monthly on taxable goods effectively pays a "proximity premium" of roughly $55 compared to a theoretical national baseline, money that cannot be recaptured through cheaper haircuts or utility bills. Demographic and labor dynamics reinforce this bifurcated affordability. An unemployment rate of 4.5 percent suggests a reasonably tight labor market, yet educational attainment—only 12.2 percent of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher—signals a workforce concentrated in sectors where wage growth often lags productivity gains. The median age of 44.1 years indicates an established, likely aging population with fixed healthcare needs, making the 56.7 services parity a critical fiscal shield; however, should healthcare utilization rise with age, the absolute dollar savings on services may struggle to offset the inflexible 93.1 goods index on a shrinking fixed income. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the RPP drifted downward from 88.6 to 86.8, a modest 2.0 percent decline in relative price levels, while nominal median income surged 41.9 percent from $42,346 to $60,078 against an 18.2 percent rise in median rent. This trajectory confirms that wage growth has decisively outpaced shelter inflation, expanding real disposable income. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the immovability of goods costs: vehicle maintenance, grocery bills, and fuel will feel nearly identical to higher-wage regions, while the savings on rent and services act as a static buffer rather than a growing dividend. The household ledger benefits today from a wide gap between income growth and rent growth, but the durability of that gap depends entirely on whether local wages can maintain their recent momentum against a goods floor that refuses to drop.

What this means for residents of Perry County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 69206.3, a median household income of $60,078 in Perry County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $41,577,761 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
