📊 Local Market Analysis

Marshall County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 11.2 percentage points below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 88.8 in 2023. This discount is heavily weighted toward the services category, which clocks in at a striking 66.0 — 34 points below the national benchmark — encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining expenses that typically strain household budgets in metropolitan areas. Goods, by contrast, represent the highest local cost component at 96.6, nearly aligning with national pricing for groceries, apparel, and fuel. Rental costs sit at 87.3, offering meaningful shelter savings relative to the broader market. The divergence between services and goods creates a distinctive consumption environment: daily essentials like food and transportation carry near-national price tags, while the recurring service obligations that dominate monthly cash outflows — medical visits, utility bills, restaurant meals — come at a substantial discount. The median household income of $72,785 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 11.9 percent, and when adjusted for local price levels, the purchasing power index rises to $81,993.7 — a figure that illustrates the compounding advantage of above-average earnings in a below-average cost environment. Rent burden stands at just 14.2 percent of income, with median monthly rent of $860 tracking well below the national $928, leaving considerable disposable income for savings, debt reduction, or discretionary spending. The local labor market reflects modest tightness at 4.0 percent unemployment, while educational attainment — 14.6 percent holding a bachelor's degree or higher — trails national averages, suggesting an economy anchored in sectors that do not require advanced credentials. The median age of 38.7 indicates a workforce in its prime earning years, supporting the income premium observed in the data. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the RPP declined from 91.139 to 88.769, a 2.6 percent drop in relative price levels even as median income surged 36.4 percent from $53,351 to $72,785. Median rent increased 32.3 percent from $650 to $860, nearly matching income growth but starting from a far lower base. This trajectory indicates that wage gains have not merely kept pace with shelter costs but have significantly outstripped the broader consumer price environment, particularly in services where the index remains deeply depressed relative to the nation. The widening gap between local purchasing power and nominal income suggests the county's affordability advantage has strengthened over the decade. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel expenses that mirror national averages, with a typical household allocating roughly $800 to $1,000 monthly for food and transportation depending on household size and commute distance. The service-side savings are concrete: healthcare out-of-pocket costs, utility bills, and dining out will run 25 to 35 percent below what the same household would pay in a median-cost metro area. Rent for a standard apartment consumes roughly one-seventh of gross income, freeing cash flow for retirement contributions or emergency reserves that would be constrained in higher-cost jurisdictions. The trade-off appears in limited local amenities and a labor market where wage growth may depend on regional industrial cycles rather than knowledge-economy dynamics, a factor that shapes long-term earning trajectory more than monthly budget arithmetic.

What this means for residents of Marshall County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 81993.7, a median household income of $72,785 in Marshall County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $59,679,115 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
