📊 Local Market Analysis

Drew County residents navigate a cost structure that sits 13.2 percent below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 86.8 in 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals distinct pressure points for household budgets. While rental costs track 18.1 percent below the national benchmark at an index of 81.9 and services plunge to a remarkably low 56.7—43.3 percent cheaper than the U.S. average—the goods category tells a different story. At 93.1, the price of groceries, apparel, and fuel sits only 6.9 percent below the national level, making tangible daily necessities the primary driver of local expenditure. This divergence means a household saving significantly on housing and utilities still confronts near-national pricing at the checkout counter, a dynamic that compresses disposable income more aggressively than the headline RPP suggests. The median household income of $41,071 translates into a Purchasing Power Index of 47,311.4, effectively granting residents a 15.3 percent boost in real terms relative to their nominal earnings, though that adjusted figure remains 27.3 percent shy of the national median income of $65,047. Rent burden stands at a manageable 20.8 percent, with the median monthly outlay of $712 consuming roughly one-fifth of gross pay, a stark contrast to coastal metros where that ratio frequently exceeds 30 percent. However, the local labor market context tempers this affordability: a 5.1 percent unemployment rate paired with a bachelor’s degree attainment rate of just 15.7 percent signals an economy reliant on lower-wage sectors, limiting upward mobility for the median worker aged 37.7 years. The demographic profile suggests a workforce vulnerable to goods-price inflation, as limited high-skill employment caps wage growth while the cost of fuel and food remains tethered to national supply chains. Over the past decade, the county has experienced a modest deflationary trend in its overall price level, with the RPP declining 2.0 percent from 88.6 in 2010 to 86.8 in 2023. During that same window, nominal median income surged 25.1 percent from $32,819 to $41,071, outpacing the 19.3 percent rise in median rent from $597 to $712. This trajectory indicates that, historically, local wages have expanded faster than the two largest budget line items—shelter and the broader consumption basket—improving the real standard of living for incumbent residents. The services component, anchored by healthcare and utilities at nearly half the national price, has acted as a structural anchor keeping the overall index suppressed, offsetting the stickiness observed in goods pricing. A prospective resident should budget for a daily reality where housing dollars stretch considerably further than the national norm, but the weekly grocery bill and fuel costs offer scant relief. The 93.1 goods index implies that a household spending $800 monthly on food, transportation, and household supplies realizes only roughly $55 in savings compared to an average American city, whereas the rental discount on a comparable unit could exceed $200. Utility and healthcare budgets benefit from the 56.7 services index, potentially freeing cash flow for debt service or savings, provided employment stability holds. The critical variable remains income trajectory: with educational attainment low and unemployment above the national rate, the purchasing power advantage rests on a fragile labor foundation that offers limited insulation against a potential spike in the goods component.

What this means for residents of Drew County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 47311.4, a median household income of $41,071 in Drew County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $19,431,265 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
