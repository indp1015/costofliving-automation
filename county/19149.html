📊 Local Market Analysis

Plymouth County residents navigate a cost structure sitting 11.2 percent below the national benchmark, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 88.8 in 2023 that masks significant divergence across consumption categories. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — represent the highest local cost component at 96.6, nearly matching the national average, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining plunge to 66.0, the lowest index value and a 34 percent discount to the U.S. mean. Rental costs settle at 87.3, contributing to a daily expense profile where the weekly grocery run and commute feel familiar to urban transplants, but the monthly outlay for childcare, medical visits, and restaurant meals delivers substantial relief. This pricing dynamic translates into a purchasing power index of $91,924, effectively granting the median household earning $81,600 a real income advantage of roughly $10,300 over nominal wages. Housing affordability underscores this surplus: median rent of $865 claims only 12.7 percent of gross income, a fraction of the national median rent of $928 against a lower national median income of $65,047. The local labor market reinforces this stability; an unemployment rate of 1.9 percent signals extreme labor scarcity that supports wage floors, while a median age of 41.1 and a bachelor’s attainment rate of 18.4 percent suggest a settled, experienced workforce concentrated in skilled trades and healthcare rather than high-volatility knowledge sectors. Over the past decade, the county has experienced a rare deflationary trend in its broad price level, with the RPP declining 2.6 percent from 91.139 in 2010 to 88.769 in 2023. Nominal income growth of 42.8 percent — rising from $57,130 to $81,600 — has comfortably outpaced this modest price retreat, yet rental inflation has accelerated faster than earnings, climbing 47.9 percent from $585 to $865. This divergence indicates that while the overall basket of goods and services has become relatively cheaper, the shelter component is absorbing a growing slice of the household budget, narrowing the gap between local rent burdens and national averages. A prospective resident should budget for a "split personality" expense ledger. The weekly trip for fuel and groceries demands near-national pricing discipline, as the 96.6 goods index offers little arbitrage opportunity. Conversely, the 66.0 services index creates deep discretionary capacity for healthcare premiums, utility bills, and dining entertainment. The critical pressure point remains shelter velocity; although the current 12.7 percent rent-to-income ratio appears benign, the historical trajectory of rent rising 5.1 percentage points faster than income over thirteen years suggests that lease renewals will progressively test that margin, requiring households to anchor long-term housing costs early to preserve the county’s inherent purchasing power advantage.

What this means for residents of Plymouth County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 91924.0, a median household income of $81,600 in Plymouth County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $75,009,984 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
