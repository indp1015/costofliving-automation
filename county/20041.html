📊 Local Market Analysis

Dickinson County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 10.1 percent below the national average, reflected in an overall Regional Price Parity of 89.9, yet the composition of that discount reveals distinct pressures on household budgets. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel — represent the highest relative expense at 96.5, nearly converging with national pricing, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining carry a steep 31.4 percent discount at 68.6. Rental costs occupy a middle ground at 90.8, translating to a median monthly outlay of $809 against a national median of $928. This bifurcated profile means daily essentials like food and transportation offer limited arbitrage compared to metropolitan areas, whereas the deep service-sector discount provides significant relief on recurring professional and utility bills. The median household income of $67,982 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 4.5 percent, and when adjusted for the local price level, purchasing power rises to an effective $75,613 — a 16.2 percent premium over nominal earnings. Rent burden stands at a remarkably low 14.3 percent of gross income, roughly half the 30 percent threshold commonly associated with housing stress, freeing substantial cash flow for savings or discretionary spending. However, the goods-heavy consumption basket typical of rural and micropolitan households — higher fuel consumption for longer commutes, greater reliance on vehicle ownership, and less substitution away from physical retail — blunts the theoretical advantage of the low overall RPP. A family spending disproportionately on the 96.5-indexed goods category experiences a cost of living much closer to the national baseline than the headline figure suggests. Demographic and labor market fundamentals reinforce this dynamic. An unemployment rate of 2.4 percent signals a tight labor market that has supported a 38.5 percent surge in median income since 2010, outpacing the 29.2 percent climb in median rent over the same period. The median age of 42.1 and a bachelor’s degree attainment rate of 15.3 percent — well below the national average — suggest an economy anchored in skilled trades, agriculture, and services rather than high-wage knowledge sectors. This composition helps explain why wage growth has been robust enough to outstrip shelter inflation yet remains tethered to sectors where goods expenditure is structurally high and remote-work flexibility is limited, keeping transportation and fuel costs central to the monthly budget. Over the last thirteen years, the RPP has drifted downward from 92.99 to 89.91, a 3.3 percent decline indicating that local consumer prices have risen more slowly than the national basket. Income growth has decisively outpaced both rental and overall inflation, expanding real purchasing power considerably. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the asymmetry between categories: housing consumes a minor share of income, but the goods intensity of daily life — filling a tank, stocking a pantry, maintaining vehicles — operates at near-national price levels. The service discount is real but largely captured through lower healthcare and utility bills rather than lifestyle amenities. Households relocating from higher-cost metros will find shelter savings immediate and substantial, though the erosion of that advantage through goods spending requires explicit planning, particularly for multi-vehicle households facing long commutes across the county’s 850 square miles.

What this means for residents of Dickinson County, Kansas
