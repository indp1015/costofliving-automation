📊 Local Market Analysis

Poinsett County registers an overall Regional Price Parity of 86.8, placing local consumer prices 13.2 percent below the national benchmark and establishing the area as a distinctly low-cost environment within the broader U.S. economy. The consumption breakdown reveals a sharp bifurcation: goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel — sit at 93.1, only marginally discounted from the national average, while services collapse to 56.7, the lowest component by a wide margin and the primary driver of the county’s affordability advantage. Rental costs, indexed at 81.9, occupy the middle ground, contributing to a cost structure where daily essentials like food and transportation carry near-national price tags, but labor-intensive services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining remain deeply discounted, making the overall basket cheap relative to the country but not uniformly so across categories. Purchasing power metrics confirm that the median household income of $47,188 stretches considerably further here than the raw nominal figure suggests. The Purchasing Power Index of 54,357.8 implies that after adjusting for the 86.8 RPP, the typical household commands the real equivalent of roughly $54,358 in national-average dollars, a meaningful buffer against the national median income of $65,047. Housing absorbency remains low; median monthly rent of $647 consumes only 16.5 percent of gross income, a stark contrast to the 30-percent burden threshold often cited as the affordability limit, and well below the national median rent of $928. This dynamic leaves a larger share of disposable income available for the goods category where prices are stickier, effectively insulating residents from the full force of national inflation on food and energy. Local demographics and labor conditions frame the context for these cost dynamics. An unemployment rate of 5.3 percent exceeds the national norm, signaling slack in the labor market that likely suppresses wage growth and keeps service-sector pricing depressed. Educational attainment is notably low, with only 8.5 percent of adults holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, correlating with the modest median income and a workforce concentrated in sectors that do not command premium compensation. The median age of 39.8 years suggests a stable, working-age population, but the combination of limited high-skill employment and low service costs creates a feedback loop: low wages sustain cheap services, while cheap services reduce the nominal income required to maintain a basic standard of living. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the RPP drifted downward from 88.6 to 86.8, a modest 2.0 percent decline in relative price levels, while nominal median income surged 42.7 percent from $33,070 to $47,188. Median rent increased only 25.4 percent over the same span, from $516 to $647, lagging income growth by a substantial margin. This divergence indicates that real purchasing power has improved markedly for incumbent residents, as wages have outpaced both the general cost level and the specific cost of shelter. For a prospective resident, the budgeting implication is clear: fixed housing costs are exceptionally manageable, but variable expenses for groceries and fuel will feel much closer to national averages, requiring a budget that accounts for near-parity pricing on tangible goods despite the county’s overall low-cost reputation.

What this means for residents of Poinsett County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 54357.8, a median household income of $47,188 in Poinsett County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $25,650,359 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
