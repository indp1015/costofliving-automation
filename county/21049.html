📊 Local Market Analysis

Clark County residents navigate a cost environment running 10.1 percent below the national benchmark, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity for 2023 registering 89.9 against a U.S. average of 100. This aggregate discount masks significant divergence across consumption categories: goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — sit at 94.5, only marginally cheaper than the national norm, while services including healthcare, utilities, and dining plunge to 62.9, the lowest component and the primary driver of the county’s affordability advantage. Rental costs, indexed at 80.9, reinforce the discount but sit closer to the goods tier than the deep service-sector trough, suggesting that while shelter is accessible, the daily basket of tangible necessities exerts the strongest upward pressure on local budgets. The consumption breakdown reveals that daily life is structurally inexpensive relative to the country, yet the "cheapness" is concentrated in labor-intensive services rather than the goods households purchase most frequently. A median household income of $67,824 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 4.3 percent, and when adjusted by the local RPP, purchasing power rises to an effective $75,452, granting residents roughly $10,400 in additional real buying capacity compared to the average American earner. Rent burden stands at a remarkably low 15.6 percent of gross income — median monthly rent of $883 versus the national $928 — leaving substantial discretionary latitude for savings, debt service, or the higher-priced goods basket that dominates weekly expenditure. Demographic and labor indicators frame the sustainability of this advantage. An unemployment rate of 4.8 percent signals a moderately tight labor market, while a bachelor’s attainment rate of just 13.9 percent — well below typical metro averages — suggests the wage premium over the national median is driven by occupational mix or tenure rather than high-skill density. The median age of 40.8 years indicates a workforce in prime earning years, supporting current income levels, though the relatively low educational ceiling may constrain future wage growth if the local economy fails to attract higher-value industries. These factors combine to create a stable but potentially fragile equilibrium where purchasing power relies on maintaining current employment density rather than productivity gains. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP slipped only 0.6 percent from 90.4 to 89.9, indicating remarkable price stability, while nominal median income surged 41.4 percent from $47,959 to $67,824 and median rent climbed a more modest 26.5 percent from $698 to $883. Wages have not merely kept pace with shelter costs; they have significantly outstripped them, expanding real housing affordability across the decade. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the goods–services split: expect near-national prices at the grocery store and gas pump, but anticipate meaningful discounts on healthcare visits, utility bills, and restaurant meals. The low rent burden — roughly $200 below the 30-percent stress threshold — provides a buffer against goods-price volatility, though any erosion of the service-sector discount or stagnation in the 41-percent income growth trajectory would quickly tighten household margins.

What this means for residents of Clark County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 75452.2, a median household income of $67,824 in Clark County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $51,174,700 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
