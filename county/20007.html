📊 Local Market Analysis

Barber County residents experience a cost of living roughly 10 percent below the national benchmark, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity index registering 89.9 for 2023 against a U.S. average of 100. The discount is heavily concentrated in services, where healthcare, utilities, and dining sit at just 68.6 — more than 30 points below the national level — while goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel run only marginally cheaper at 96.5. Rental costs occupy a middle ground at 90.8, meaning housing consumes a smaller share of the budget than in most metros but does not offer the deep discount seen in services. Daily life is therefore inexpensive relative to the country, driven primarily by a service sector that costs roughly two-thirds of the national average, though the near-parity pricing on goods tempers the overall advantage. The median household income of $57,615 trails the national median of $65,047 by about 11 percent, yet the purchasing power index of 64,082.2 indicates that local wages stretch considerably further once the lower price level is factored in. Rent burden stands at a remarkably low 15.5 percent of income, with the median monthly rent of $743 comparing favorably to the $928 national figure. Labor market tightness reinforces this dynamic: a 2.3 percent unemployment rate signals strong demand for workers, while a median age of 42.6 and a bachelor's degree attainment rate of 21.8 percent suggest a stable, experienced workforce that likely benefits from the low service-cost environment, particularly in healthcare and utilities. Over the past decade, the RPP has declined from 92.991 to 89.908, a 3.3 percent drop that reflects either falling local prices or faster national inflation. Median income rose 11.2 percent from $51,811 to $57,615 during the same period, but median rent accelerated 22 percent from $609 to $743, outpacing wage growth by a factor of two. This divergence means housing affordability, while still strong by national standards, has eroded locally; the rent-to-income ratio has climbed even as the broader cost-of-living index has improved. Goods inflation has likely tracked closer to national trends given the 96.5 index, while the persistent services discount has acted as a buffer against broader price pressures. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that feel nearly identical to urban centers, offset by substantially lower outlays for medical care, electricity, and restaurant meals. The rent advantage — roughly $185 below the national median per month — translates to over $2,200 in annual savings, but the faster trajectory of rental increases warrants attention in lease negotiations. With services priced at a deep discount, households allocating a larger share of spending to healthcare or utilities will experience a disproportionately higher standard of living than the raw income figure suggests, while those with heavy goods consumption see less relative benefit.

What this means for residents of Barber County, Kansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 64082.2, a median household income of $57,615 in Barber County, Kansas has the equivalent buying power of $36,920,960 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 90.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
