📊 Local Market Analysis

Hot Spring County’s cost of living sits well below the national norm, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 86.8 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices are the most elevated component at 93.1, still 6.9 points under the national average, while services are markedly cheaper at 56.7, a 43.3‑point discount. Rental costs, measured by the housing sub‑index, register at 81.9, indicating that rents are roughly 18 % lower than the countrywide level. The composite picture is one of modest affordability, driven primarily by inexpensive services and housing, even though everyday consumer goods are only slightly cheaper than elsewhere. The consumption structure is dominated by goods, which account for the highest index value and therefore the largest share of household outlays. At 93.1, grocery and apparel prices are close to parity with national levels, meaning residents do not enjoy deep discounts on staples. By contrast, services—healthcare, utilities, and dining—cost just over half of what they do nationally, providing a tangible cushion for discretionary spending. Rental costs, while not the cheapest segment, remain well under the national median rent of $928; the local median of $763 translates to a rent‑burden of 17.4 % of household income, comfortably below the 30 % threshold that defines affordability. Consequently, day‑to‑day life in Hot Spring County is inexpensive in terms of housing and services, but residents must allocate a larger proportion of their budget to goods than they would in a lower‑cost‑goods environment. Median household income in the county stands at $52,644, 19.5 % below the national median of $65,047. After subtracting the median rent of $763, a typical household retains roughly $49,800 in annual earnings for all other expenses. The Purchasing Power Index of 60,642.8 quantifies this residual buying capacity, indicating that a dollar earned in Hot Spring County purchases about 60 % of what a dollar does nationally after accounting for local price levels. This figure underscores that, despite lower incomes, the reduced cost of services and housing preserves a respectable amount of discretionary cash for residents, especially when compared to higher‑cost metros where rent alone can consume 30 % or more of earnings. Demographically, the county’s labor market features a 3.6 % unemployment rate, well under the national average, suggesting stable employment prospects for the median worker. However, only 10 % of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, reflecting a workforce concentrated in lower‑skill, lower‑wage occupations, which aligns with the modest income figures. The median age of 43 years points to a mature population, likely with established household budgets and less reliance on high‑cost consumer goods. These characteristics reinforce a cost‑of‑living environment where essential services and housing are affordable, but limited educational attainment may constrain upward income mobility. Over the past decade, the RPP fell from 88.56 in 2010 to 86.81 in 2023, a 2.0 % decline, indicating that consumer prices have softened relative to the national basket. Median household income rose from $40,000 to $52,644, a 31.6 % increase, outpacing the 16.1 % rise in median rent (from $657 to $763). The divergence between wage growth and rent inflation suggests that earnings have kept pace with, and indeed exceeded, housing cost pressures, further enhancing affordability for renters. Goods price stability, combined with a modest drop in the overall RPP, means that the purchasing power of residents has improved markedly over the ten‑year span. Prospective residents should anchor their budgets around the relatively low rent burden of 17.4 % and the inexpensive service sector. Allocating roughly 30 % of income to goods—reflecting the near‑national price level—will cover groceries, clothing, and fuel without straining finances. With a median disposable income of about $49,800 after rent, a household can comfortably absorb the higher cost of goods while still preserving funds for savings or leisure, provided it avoids excessive discretionary spending on premium services. A prudent budgeting framework would therefore emphasize housing and utility savings, while recognizing that grocery bills will align closely with national averages.

What this means for residents of Hot Spring County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 60642.8, a median household income of $52,644 in Hot Spring County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $31,924,796 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
