📊 Local Market Analysis

Quitman County presents a cost of living profile that sits modestly below the national baseline, with an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 96.5, indicating that a standard basket of goods and services costs roughly 3.5 percent less than the U.S. average. This aggregate figure masks significant divergence across categories: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel carry an index of 97.7, nearly tracking the national norm, while services — encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining — register a substantially lower 88.3. Rental costs, indexed at 92.3, offer a discount of nearly 8 percent versus the national benchmark, a factor that directly suppresses the headline number. The highest cost component remains goods, suggesting that daily consumables provide the least relative relief for local households, whereas the services sector delivers the most pronounced affordability advantage. The consumption breakdown reveals that the local cost structure is not uniformly cheap; rather, it is bifurcated. While the 11.7 percent discount on services provides meaningful relief on recurring obligations like electricity and medical visits, the near-parity pricing on goods (97.7) means residents pay almost full freight for weekly necessities such as food and gasoline. This dynamic compresses the practical benefit of the lower overall index. The median monthly rent of $734 consumes 23.9 percent of the median household income of $36,838, a rent-burden ratio that appears manageable on the surface but leaves limited margin given the income floor. The Purchasing Power Index of 38,176.5 quantifies this reality: after adjusting for local prices, the median household commands real purchasing power roughly 41 percent below the nominal national median income of $65,047, underscoring that lower prices only partially offset significantly lower earnings. Demographic and labor market fundamentals sharpen the affordability picture. An unemployment rate of 31.1 percent — extraordinarily high by any modern standard — signals a labor market with minimal wage pressure and scarce full-time opportunity, while an educational attainment rate of just 5.1 percent for bachelor’s degrees or higher limits access to higher-paying remote or professional roles. The median age of 59.4 years suggests a population heavily weighted toward fixed-income retirees or near-retirees, for whom the low services index (88.3) on healthcare and utilities is a critical buffer, yet the high goods index (97.7) erodes the value of Social Security or pension checks at the grocery store and pump. This demographic skew implies that the cost of living "advantage" is largely consumed by the necessity of stretching fixed, low nominal incomes across a basket where essential goods offer no discount. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP drifted upward from 94.574 to 96.494, a 2.0 percent increase in relative price levels, while median income rose 17.0 percent from $31,487 to $36,838 and median rent increased only 7.3 percent from $684 to $734. Nominal wage growth has outpaced both the local inflation proxy and housing cost acceleration, a rare positive trajectory for real incomes. However, the starting base remains so low that the absolute gain — roughly $5,350 over thirteen years — barely moves the needle on discretionary capacity. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that mirror national averages, anticipate significantly cheaper healthcare and utility outlays, and recognize that housing costs, while low in absolute terms, demand a meaningful share of a paycheck that ranks among the lowest in the country. The arithmetic of daily life here hinges less on the 3.5 percent overall discount and more on the ability to secure income well above the local median.

What this means for residents of Quitman County, Georgia

With a Purchasing Power Index of 38176.5, a median household income of $36,838 in Quitman County, Georgia has the equivalent buying power of $14,063,459 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 92.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
