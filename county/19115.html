📊 Local Market Analysis

Louisa County presents a distinct cost-of-living profile where the overall Regional Price Parity of 88.8 sits 11.2 percentage points below the national baseline, yet the composition of that discount reveals sharp asymmetries across consumption categories. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel — run at 96.6, nearly tracking the national average and standing as the highest-cost component in the local basket. By contrast, services including healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 66.0, a staggering 34-point discount that single-handedly drags the aggregate index down. Rental costs settle at 87.3, offering moderate relief compared to the national median rent of $928; the local median of $797 consumes just 11.9 percent of the $80,223 median household income, a rent-burden ratio roughly half the standard affordability threshold. That service-sector discount fundamentally reshapes daily household budgeting. A resident purchasing a market basket of groceries and gasoline experiences pricing nearly indistinguishable from a suburban coastal metro, but the moment consumption shifts to haircuts, medical visits, electricity, or restaurant meals, the outlay drops precipitously. This dynamic suggests the low overall index is not a broad-based affordability story but a structural artifact of a sparsely populated, service-light economy where provider density is low and non-tradable service prices are suppressed. The purchasing power index of 90,372.8 confirms the net benefit: the median household commands real resources roughly 23 percent above the national median income of $65,047 after adjusting for local prices, a surplus driven almost entirely by the service discount rather than wage exceptionalism. Demographic and labor-market indicators contextualize this surplus. An unemployment rate of 3.8 percent signals a tight labor market, yet only 13.9 percent of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, and the median age of 41.8 skews older than the national figure. The workforce appears anchored in occupations that do not require advanced credentials but benefit from proximity to regional logistics and agricultural supply chains. Wage growth has been robust on paper — median income surged 56.9 percent from $51,144 in 2010 to $80,223 in 2023 — yet the RPP drifted only modestly from 91.1 to 88.8, a 2.6 percent decline. Rent rose 29 percent over the same span, significantly lagging income growth, which implies the purchasing-power gains are genuine and not merely nominal. For a prospective resident, the budgeting takeaway is specific: allocate near-national budgets for the weekly grocery run and the commute, but expect materially lower line items for childcare, insurance premiums, utility bills, and dining out. The 11.9 percent rent-to-income ratio leaves unusual latitude for savings or discretionary spending, provided the household does not require specialized medical services or high-frequency professional services that the local market cannot supply at the discounted rate. The data suggests a household budget that stretches further on the margin for shelter and services, but offers no arbitrage on the tangible goods that fill the pantry and the gas tank.

What this means for residents of Louisa County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 90372.8, a median household income of $80,223 in Louisa County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $72,499,771 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
