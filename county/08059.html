📊 Local Market Analysis

Jefferson County presents a distinctive cost-of-living profile where the overall Regional Price Parity of 101.9 masks a severe bifurcation between service costs and housing expenses. Services command an index of 130.5, the highest component by a wide margin, rendering healthcare, utilities, and dining significantly more expensive than the national baseline, while goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel sit slightly below average at 99.2. The most striking deviation appears in rental costs, which register at 86.8—thirteen points below the national average—creating a rare environment where shelter costs substantially dampen the headline inflation figure despite intense pressure on daily service consumption. This structural anomaly translates into robust purchasing power for the median household earning $107,800, a figure 65.7 percent above the national median of $65,047. The Purchasing Power Index of 105,835.7 confirms that after adjusting for local prices, real income remains exceptionally high. Rent burden stands at a manageable 19.9 percent of gross income against a median monthly rent of $1,792, which, while nearly double the national median of $928, consumes a far smaller share of the local paycheck. The labor market underpins this dynamic: a 3.9 percent unemployment rate paired with a 32.1 percent bachelor’s degree attainment rate and a median age of 40.4 suggests a mature, educated workforce concentrated in high-wage sectors that insulate household budgets from the elevated service index. Over the past decade, the inflation trajectory has been modest on paper but divergent in practice. The overall RPP crept up only 1.3 percent from 100.59 in 2010 to 101.856 in 2023, yet nominal median income surged 53.6 percent from $70,164 while median rent accelerated 70.3 percent from $1,052. Wage growth has outpaced the broad cost index comfortably, but rent inflation has nearly matched income gains, gradually eroding the shelter affordability advantage that currently defines the county. The services component, stubbornly fixed at 30.5 percent above national levels, implies that discretionary spending on healthcare, childcare, and dining out absorbs a disproportionate share of the income gains that might otherwise flow to savings or goods consumption. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the services-housing trade-off. The below-average rental index provides immediate monthly relief relative to peer metros, but the 130.5 services index acts as a persistent tax on daily life—every medical visit, utility bill, and restaurant meal carries a steep premium. Goods purchases offer a slight discount at 99.2, providing marginal offset for grocery and fuel budgets. Households should model cash flows assuming shelter consumes roughly one-fifth of gross income while allocating a significantly larger contingency for service-sector inflation than national averages would suggest, particularly given the historical velocity of rent increases relative to wage growth.

What this means for residents of Jefferson County, Colorado

With a Purchasing Power Index of 105835.7, a median household income of $107,800 in Jefferson County, Colorado has the equivalent buying power of $114,090,885 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 86.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
