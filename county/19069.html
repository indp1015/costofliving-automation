📊 Local Market Analysis

Franklin County, Iowa presents a cost of living profile that sits 11.2 percentage points below the national benchmark, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 88.8 for 2023. This aggregate figure masks significant divergence across consumption categories: goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—track closely with the national average at 96.6, while rental costs come in moderately lower at 87.3. The primary driver of the county’s affordability advantage is the services sector, which clocks in at a striking 66.0, roughly one-third cheaper than the U.S. baseline. This deep discount on healthcare, utilities, and dining out effectively subsidizes daily life for residents, offsetting the near-parity pricing on tangible goods and creating a consumption environment where discretionary service spending carries significantly less weight than in metropolitan areas. The purchasing power dynamics reinforce this structural advantage. With a median household income of $63,648—just 2.1% below the national median of $65,047—and median monthly rent of only $649 compared to $928 nationally, the rent burden stands at a mere 12.2% of gross income. This low shelter cost, combined with the depressed services index, translates into a Purchasing Power Index of 71,700.7, indicating that the median dollar stretches substantially further here than the nominal income gap suggests. Residents effectively retain a larger share of disposable income after covering core obligations, a dynamic reflected in the 30.9% nominal income growth over the last decade outpacing the 13.1% rise in median rent, even as the overall RPP drifted downward from 91.1 to 88.8. Local demographics and labor conditions provide the context for this equilibrium. An unemployment rate of 3.8% signals a tight labor market supporting wage growth, yet an educational attainment rate of only 13.5% for bachelor’s degrees or higher suggests the economy relies on sectors that do not typically command premium metropolitan wages. The median age of 41.9 years indicates a settled, established workforce rather than a transient student or early-career population, which stabilizes demand for existing housing stock and keeps rental inflation contained. This demographic profile supports the observed trend where income growth has comfortably outpaced shelter costs, preserving the county’s affordability margin without the volatility seen in high-growth corridors. For a prospective resident budgeting daily household expenses, the calculus centers on the asymmetry between goods and services. Grocery bills and fuel costs will feel familiar, aligning with national norms at 96.6, but the 34% discount on services—particularly healthcare outlays, utility bills, and restaurant meals—creates recurring monthly savings that accumulate significantly over a year. Housing costs require roughly $650 monthly for median rent, a figure that demands a fraction of the income allocation required in urban centers. The critical budgeting consideration is not an absolute low cost for every line item, but rather the structural relief on the service side of the ledger, which insulates the household budget from the inflationary pressures that typically erode real wages in higher-cost service economies.

What this means for residents of Franklin County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 71700.7, a median household income of $63,648 in Franklin County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $45,636,062 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
