📊 Local Market Analysis

Madison County, Idaho, lives on a cost‑of‑living index of 92.2, meaning consumer prices are roughly eight percent below the national average of 100. The goods component sits at 95.9, only four percent under the nation, while services are markedly cheaper at 86.9, a thirteen‑percent discount. Housing, measured by rental costs, is the most affordable pillar at 68.1, translating to a thirty‑two percent gap versus the national benchmark. These figures place everyday expenses in the county firmly on the cheaper side of the national spectrum, especially for shelter and services such as healthcare and utilities. The dominant driver of the local cost structure is housing, not goods or services. Rental prices average $965 per month, which is $37 above the national median rent of $928, yet the rental‑cost index remains the lowest of the three categories because the index reflects relative affordability rather than absolute dollars. Goods, at 95.9, are the only category approaching parity with the nation, indicating that groceries, apparel, and fuel cost nearly the same as elsewhere. Services are substantially lower, suggesting that dining out, medical care, and utility bills stretch a resident’s budget further. Consequently, daily life feels inexpensive for most households, with the primary savings derived from low housing costs and modest service expenses, while grocery and fuel bills remain comparable to national levels. Disposable income for the median household can be gauged by the Purchasing Power Index of 63,163, which represents the amount of goods and services a median income of $58,259 can purchase locally. After allocating the median rent of $965, rent consumes 19.9 % of household income, leaving roughly $48,000 for other expenditures. Compared with the national median income of $65,047, Madison County earners retain about 89 % of the purchasing power of an average American, but the lower cost of services and housing offsets the income gap, resulting in a net purchasing power that is competitive despite the lower nominal earnings. Demographic factors shape this affordability profile. With 28.4 % of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, the county enjoys a modestly educated labor force, yet the unemployment rate of 8.8 % exceeds the national average, indicating a weaker labor market that can suppress wage growth. The median age of 21.4 years points to a youthful population, likely composed of students and early‑career workers whose income expectations are lower and who benefit disproportionately from cheap housing. This demographic mix explains why rent burden remains under 20 % of income—a level generally considered affordable—while the high unemployment rate suggests that many households may rely on part‑time or lower‑wage employment, limiting discretionary spending. Over the past decade, the Regional Price Parity (RPP) index slipped from 93.2 to 92.2, a modest one‑percent decline, indicating that consumer prices have softened relative to the nation. Median household income, however, surged by 80.7 % from $32,233 to $58,259, outpacing the 50.5 % rise in median rent (from $641 to $965). The disparity between income growth and rent inflation suggests that wages have kept pace, and even exceeded, the rise in housing costs, reinforcing the county’s affordability for renters. Goods prices have remained relatively stable, as reflected by the near‑national goods index, while service costs have continued to lag behind national trends. Prospective residents should structure their budgets around the low housing expense but anticipate grocery and fuel bills that mirror national averages. Allocating roughly 20 % of income to rent leaves about 80 % for utilities, transportation, food, healthcare, and discretionary spending. Given the youthful demographic and higher unemployment, a cushion for variable income is prudent. Monitoring service‑related costs—particularly healthcare and utilities—can yield additional savings, as these categories are substantially cheaper than the national norm. A realistic monthly budget for a median‑income household would therefore prioritize rent ($965), allocate approximately $400–$500 for groceries and fuel, $200–$250 for utilities and healthcare, and reserve the remainder for transportation, insurance, and personal expenses, ensuring a comfortable standard of living within Madison County’s comparatively low cost environment.

Analysis prepared by the Data in DP Research Team. Based on BEA RPP and Census ACS data.

What this means for residents of Madison County, Idaho

With a Purchasing Power Index of 63163.0, a median household income of $58,259 in Madison County, Idaho has the equivalent buying power of $36,798,132 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 68.1 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
