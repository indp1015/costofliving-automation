📊 Local Market Analysis

Franklin County presents a cost-of-living profile that sits marginally below the national baseline, with a 2023 Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 98.8, yet the aggregate figure masks a distinct bifurcation in household expenditure patterns. The overall index is depressed primarily by rental housing costs, which register at 80.4—nearly 20 percentage points under the national average—while the goods component, encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel, runs hot at 101.6, exceeding the national norm. Services, including healthcare, utilities, and dining, sit at 92.4. This structure creates a daily financial reality where securing shelter is comparatively inexpensive, but the recurring transactional costs of daily maintenance—filling a tank, stocking a pantry, clothing a family—carry a slight premium over the typical U.S. metro area. The consumption breakdown reveals that goods act as the primary friction point for local budgets. With the median household income at $53,471—roughly 18 percent below the national median of $65,047—the 1.6 percent premium on goods consumes a disproportionate share of disposable income relative to higher-earning regions. However, the housing discount is substantial enough to flip the overall affordability calculus: median monthly rent of $771 claims only 17.3 percent of gross income, a stark contrast to the national median rent of $928. This dynamic yields a Purchasing Power Index of 54,146.8, indicating that after adjusting for local price levels, the median household commands real resources slightly above their nominal income, a rare advantage driven almost entirely by the rental shelter discount. Local labor market characteristics sharpen the context for these figures. An unemployment rate of 6.5 percent signals meaningful slack compared to national lows, while educational attainment remains modest at 11.7 percent holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, and the median age of 43.4 years skews older than the national average. This demographic profile suggests a workforce concentrated in sectors where wage growth has historically lagged, yet the low rent burden—well under the standard 30 percent stress threshold—provides a critical buffer. The labor market does not appear to generate strong income mobility, but the cost structure prevents that stagnation from translating into acute housing insecurity for the median renter. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP declined 3.0 percent from 101.778 to 98.752, signaling that local consumer prices have grown slower than the national basket. Nominal median income surged 35.3 percent from $39,512 to $53,471, outpacing the 30.7 percent rise in median rent from $590 to $771. Wages have not merely kept pace; they have expanded the margin between earnings and shelter costs. For a prospective resident, the budgeting implication is clear: allocate capital for above-average grocery and fuel bills, but expect significant relief on the lease. The primary financial risk lies not in rent escalation, but in the vulnerability of a goods-heavy consumption basket to supply-chain volatility and energy price shocks, which hit harder when the income floor is $12,000 below the national median.

What this means for residents of Franklin County, Illinois

With a Purchasing Power Index of 54146.8, a median household income of $53,471 in Franklin County, Illinois has the equivalent buying power of $28,952,835 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.4 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
