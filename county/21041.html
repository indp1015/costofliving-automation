📊 Local Market Analysis

Carroll County residents navigate a cost structure that sits 10.1 percent below the national average, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity for 2023 registering 89.9 against a baseline of 100. This aggregate figure masks a sharp bifurcation in daily expenses: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel run at 94.5, only marginally cheaper than the U.S. norm, while services—encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining—plummet to 62.9, the lowest component by a wide margin. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 80.9, translating to a median monthly outlay of $853 that consumes 17.7 percent of the median household income of $57,753, a burden significantly lighter than the national rent-to-income dynamic where the median rent of $928 presses harder against a higher national median income of $65,047. The consumption breakdown reveals that daily life is inexpensive primarily because the service economy is deeply discounted, not because goods are cheap. A household filling a prescription, paying an electric bill, or eating at a local restaurant experiences costs roughly 37 percent below the national average, a dynamic that disproportionately benefits fixed-income retirees and families with high healthcare utilization. Conversely, the goods index at 94.5 means trips to the grocery store or gas station deliver only modest savings, limiting the stretch of discretionary dollars for non-service consumption. This structure creates a peculiar purchasing power environment: the Purchasing Power Index of 64,248.5 exceeds the nominal median income by over $6,400, indicating that the median worker effectively commands the consumption capacity of a $64,000 earner in an average-cost jurisdiction, a surplus driven almost entirely by the service-side discount. Local demographics sharpen the picture of who captures this surplus. An unemployment rate of 6.9 percent sits well above national norms, signaling slack in the labor market that likely suppresses wage growth despite a 39.4 percent nominal income surge since 2010, which outpaced the 32.2 percent rise in median rent over the same period. Educational attainment remains a structural drag; only 5.6 percent of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, constraining the region’s ability to attract higher-wage service sectors that could push the services RPP upward. The median age of 38.5 suggests a workforce in its prime earning years, yet the low educational ceiling caps the ceiling on household earnings, leaving the population reliant on the existing low-cost service paradigm to maintain real living standards. Over the last decade, the overall RPP has barely budged, slipping a marginal 0.6 percent from 90.4 to 89.9, confirming that the county’s affordability is a persistent structural feature rather than a cyclical anomaly. Wages have grown faster than rents, improving the rent-burden metric, but the goods index near parity with the nation means inflation in food and energy transmits almost one-to-one to local budgets. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that feel nearly identical to suburban Ohio or Indiana, while allocating significantly less for childcare, medical copays, and utility deposits. The critical budgeting variable is not housing—where the $853 median rent is manageable—but the fragility of income in a high-unemployment, low-credential labor market where a job loss eliminates the purchasing power advantage instantly.

What this means for residents of Carroll County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 64248.5, a median household income of $57,753 in Carroll County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $37,105,436 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
