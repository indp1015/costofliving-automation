📊 Local Market Analysis

Valley County’s cost of living sits well below the national norm, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 92.2 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices are the most elevated component at 95.9, still under the national benchmark, while services register at 86.9 and rental costs plunge to 68.1, indicating that housing is markedly cheaper than elsewhere. The median household income of $76,125 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 17.1%, reinforcing the county’s relative affordability despite modest price levels. The consumption profile is dominated by the goods sector, which accounts for the highest index reading. However, the starkly low housing index (68.1) offsets the modestly higher goods cost, producing a net environment that is inexpensive for everyday living. Monthly rent of $1,011 translates to a rent‑burden of 15.9% of household income, far below the conventional affordability threshold of 30%. By contrast, the services index suggests that healthcare, utilities, and dining out are cheaper than average, further reducing discretionary spending pressure. Consequently, residents experience a cheap daily life, with the primary expense—housing—absorbing a small slice of earnings while other necessities remain modestly priced. Purchasing power in Valley County is quantified by an index of 82,532.9, reflecting the ratio of median income to the cost of living. After allocating the median rent of $1,011, a household retains roughly $71,114 of annual income for all other expenditures, equating to $5,926 per month. This residual amount comfortably covers the lower‑priced services and goods, leaving discretionary funds that surpass the national average disposable income when adjusted for cost differentials. The low rent burden and favorable purchasing power index together indicate that the median worker can sustain a higher standard of living than peers in higher‑cost regions. Demographically, the county’s unemployment rate of 2.9% signals a tight labor market, while only 25.4% of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting a workforce concentrated in sectors that may not demand advanced credentials. The median age of 49 years points to a mature population, often associated with higher savings rates and stable consumption patterns. These factors combine to produce a cost of living experience where stable employment and modest educational attainment keep housing demand subdued, reinforcing the low rental index. The older age profile also implies that many households may prioritize healthcare and utilities, yet the services index remains comfortably low, cushioning potential cost pressures. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped from 93.2 to 92.2, a 1.0% decline, while median household income surged from $48,384 to $76,125, a 57.3% increase. Rent climbed from $762 to $1,011, a 32.7% rise, still far below national rent levels. Income growth outpaced both overall price inflation and housing cost escalation, expanding real purchasing power and confirming that wages have kept pace with, and indeed exceeded, expense growth. Prospective residents should budget around $1,011 for monthly rent, allocate roughly $400–$500 for utilities and healthcare, and plan for $300–$400 in grocery and apparel spending, reflecting the 95.9 goods index. With rent consuming less than one‑sixth of income, the remaining $5,500–$6,000 per month can comfortably cover transportation, entertainment, and savings. Emphasizing the low housing cost and strong purchasing power, a household can expect to allocate a smaller proportion of earnings to fixed expenses than the national average, freeing resources for discretionary spending or debt reduction.

What this means for residents of Valley County, Idaho
