📊 Local Market Analysis

Jefferson County residents navigate a cost structure that sits 11.2 percentage points below the national average, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity registering 88.8 for 2023. This discount is not uniform across the consumption basket. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — carry a near-parity index of 96.6, meaning households pay virtually the same shelf prices as the typical American. Services, however, collapse to 66.0, a 34-point discount driven by below-average healthcare, utility, and dining-out costs. Rental housing, indexed at 87.3, splits the difference. The overall affordability profile is therefore anchored by cheap services rather than cheap essentials; a household filling a grocery cart or fueling a vehicle experiences minimal relief, while the monthly outlay for insurance, electricity, and medical visits delivers the bulk of the savings. Purchasing power metrics confirm that the median household retains significant real income after local prices are accounted for. The Purchasing Power Index of 64,013.3 exceeds the nominal median income of $56,824 by roughly 12.7 percent, effectively granting residents a $7,200 annual buffer relative to national price levels. Rent consumes only 18.6 percent of gross income — well under the 30 percent burden threshold — compared with a national median rent of $928 against a national median income of $65,047. The local labor market, however, presents friction: a 5.3 percent unemployment rate sits above the national norm, and only 23.3 percent of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting a workforce concentrated in moderate-wage sectors. The median age of 43.5 years indicates a stable, older population with likely lower geographic mobility, which can suppress wage pressure but also limit dynamism in service provision. Over the 2010–2023 window, the RPP declined 2.6 percent from 91.139 to 88.769, signaling that local consumer prices grew more slowly than the national basket. Nominal median income rose 32.5 percent, from $42,899 to $56,824, outpacing the 39.0 percent surge in median rent from $634 to $881. While income growth nearly matched shelter cost acceleration, the faster rent trajectory eroded some of the real gains, particularly for newer renters. The divergence between goods inflation (sticky near national levels) and services deflation (widening discount) implies that long-term residents who own homes or locked in leases early captured the full benefit of the services discount, while newcomers face goods prices that offer little concession. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that mirror coastal or urban prices, offset by utility and healthcare outlays that can run 20–30 percent below national averages. The rent-to-income ratio remains favorable on paper, but the 39 percent decade rent increase signals tightening rental inventory; securing a lease below $881 may require compromise on unit quality or commute distance. With unemployment elevated and educational attainment modest, wage negotiation leverage is limited, making the services discount the most reliable pillar of household solvency. Households should model scenarios where goods inflation persists while services costs stabilize, ensuring that the apparent 11 percent overall discount does not mask volatility in the non-discretionary categories that dominate weekly cash flow.

What this means for residents of Jefferson County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 64013.3, a median household income of $56,824 in Jefferson County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $36,374,918 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
