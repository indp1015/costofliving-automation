📊 Local Market Analysis

Grant County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 10.1 percent below the national average, reflected in an overall Regional Price Parity of 89.9, yet the composition of that discount reveals distinct friction points for household budgets. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel — represent the highest local cost component at 94.5, only marginally cheaper than the U.S. baseline, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining plummet to 62.9, the lowest segment by a wide margin. Rental costs sit at 80.9, offering meaningful shelter savings relative to the national median rent of $928, with the local median of $855 consuming just 15.1 percent of the median household income of $67,940, a rent burden significantly lighter than the standard 30 percent affordability threshold. The consumption breakdown indicates that daily life is inexpensive primarily because service-intensive categories — medical care, personal care, and restaurant meals — are deeply discounted, not because the weekly grocery run or the commute is cheap. A household allocating a standard share of expenditures to goods experiences near-national pricing at the checkout counter, meaning the "cheap" label applies selectively. The Purchasing Power Index of 75,581.3 quantifies this dynamic: the median earner commands roughly $7,600 more in real purchasing power than their nominal income suggests, effectively earning a premium over the national median income of $65,047 once local price levels are stripped out. This surplus is driven almost entirely by the service and housing discounts, as the goods basket erodes only 5.5 percent of the national price advantage. Local demographics frame the labor market constraints that anchor this purchasing power reality. With only 9.4 percent of adults holding a bachelor’s degree or higher and an unemployment rate of 4.7 percent — above the national norm — the workforce skews toward sectors where wage growth historically lags professional-services hubs. The median age of 36.4 suggests a population in prime earning years, yet the educational attainment ceiling likely caps median income trajectory, making the current low cost of services a critical stabilizer for household solvency rather than a transient windfall. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP barely budged, slipping a negligible 0.6 percent from 90.4 to 89.9, while median income surged 51.6 percent against a 19.6 percent rise in median rent. Wages have not merely kept pace; they have dramatically outstripped the primary recurring expense categories. For a prospective resident, the budgeting takeaway is specific: expect grocery and fuel bills to mirror national receipts, but anticipate materially lower outlays for healthcare visits, utility bills, and dining. The rent-to-income ratio leaves roughly 85 percent of gross income for all other expenses, a cushion that absorbs the near-parity goods pricing, provided no major disruption hits the heavily discounted service sector.

What this means for residents of Grant County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 75581.3, a median household income of $67,940 in Grant County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $51,349,935 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
