📊 Local Market Analysis

Phillips County registers an overall Regional Price Parity of 86.8, placing local consumer prices 13.2 percent below the national baseline, yet the aggregate figure masks severe internal distortion. Goods — groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — carry an index of 93.1, only marginally discounted from the U.S. average, while services including healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 56.7, the lowest component by a wide margin. Rental costs sit at 81.9, modestly cheaper than the national norm but elevated relative to the service environment. The goods category effectively drives the day-to-day cost structure; a household purchasing food, clothing, and gasoline experiences prices nearly on par with metropolitan areas, whereas the deep service discount reflects limited provider density and suppressed wage floors in healthcare and personal care rather than genuine affordability for consumers facing access gaps. Median household income of $38,874 translates to a purchasing power index of 44,780.6, implying that after adjusting for the 86.8 RPP, the typical resident commands roughly 69 percent of the national median's real buying capacity. Rent consumes 23.3 percent of gross income at a median $756 monthly outlay, a burden that appears manageable until measured against the 15.8 percent unemployment rate and a labor force where only 9.7 percent hold a bachelor's degree. The median age of 39.9 years signals a workforce in prime earning years constrained by structural underemployment; low educational attainment caps wage growth, while elevated joblessness depresses bargaining power, forcing many households to allocate disproportionate shares of nominal income to the goods basket that refuses to discount. Over the 2010–2023 window, the RPP drifted downward from 88.6 to 86.8, a modest 2.0 percent decline in relative price levels, while nominal median income surged 44.8 percent from $26,844 to $38,874 and median rent advanced 28.4 percent from $589 to $756. Wage growth has outpaced both inflation and shelter costs on a cumulative basis, yet the trajectory is uneven: the goods index remains sticky near 93, meaning the categories households purchase most frequently — food at home, gasoline, clothing — have not shared in the relative price decline. Real income gains are therefore concentrated in service categories that many residents cannot fully access, creating a wedge between statistical purchasing power improvement and lived budgetary relief. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that mirror national averages despite a local income base 40 percent below the U.S. median of $65,047. Healthcare and utility outlays will run significantly lower in nominal terms, but provider scarcity may impose hidden travel and time costs. Rent at $756 demands roughly $9,072 annually, leaving approximately $29,800 for all other expenses before taxes; at the current goods price level, a standard USDA moderate food plan for a family of four consumes roughly $1,100 monthly, or 34 percent of post-rent income. Transportation fuel, priced near the national index, adds further pressure for commuters in a county with limited public transit. The arithmetic leaves minimal margin for savings, debt service, or unexpected repairs, making liquidity management the central household challenge.

What this means for residents of Phillips County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 44780.6, a median household income of $38,874 in Phillips County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $17,408,010 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
