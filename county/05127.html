📊 Local Market Analysis

Scott County’s cost‑of‑living index sits at 86.8, meaning consumer prices are roughly 13 % below the national average of 100. Grocery, apparel and fuel prices are the most elevated component at 93.1, still modestly under the national benchmark, while services—including healthcare, utilities and dining—are markedly cheap at 56.7. Rental costs register at 81.9, translating to a median monthly rent of $736, which is 20.7 % lower than the national median of $928. The expense profile is driven primarily by the goods sector; its index is 6.4 points higher than the housing component and 36.4 points above services. Daily life therefore feels inexpensive in categories that dominate household budgets—energy, medical care and restaurant meals—while grocery bills remain only slightly above the national norm. The low service cost offsets the relatively higher goods price, producing a net affordability advantage for most households. Median household income in the county is $45,955, 29.4 % below the national median of $65,047. After deducting the median rent, a typical household retains $39,219 in annual discretionary income, or about $3,268 per month. The Purchasing Power Index of 52,937.4 reflects this gap: residents can purchase roughly half the basket of goods and services that an average American can with the same nominal income. Rent burden sits at 19.2 % of income, well under the 30 % threshold that defines affordability, leaving additional room for non‑housing expenditures. Demographically, the labor force is older (median age 42.4) and less educated, with only 8.7 % holding a bachelor’s degree or higher. Unemployment stands at 6.2 %, above the national rate, suggesting a modestly constrained job market that may limit wage growth. The combination of lower educational attainment and higher unemployment reinforces the modest income level and contributes to the county’s lower service‑price environment, as demand for higher‑priced amenities remains muted. Over the past decade the Regional Price Parity slipped from 88.56 to 86.81, a 2 % decline, while median income rose 23.1 % from $37,321 to $45,955. Rental costs, however, surged 43.8 % from $512 to $736, outpacing income growth and compressing disposable income for renters. The divergence indicates that wages have not kept pace with housing cost inflation, even as overall consumer prices have softened. Prospective residents should budget for a grocery and fuel bill that is near national levels, while expecting substantially lower utility, healthcare and dining expenses. Housing remains the largest variable, with rent consuming roughly one‑fifth of earnings; budgeting for a $736 monthly rent leaves about $3,300 for all other household needs. Given the modest median income and limited high‑skill job pool, households should prioritize cost‑controlled spending on goods and allocate a buffer for the relatively rapid rent increases observed over the last decade.

What this means for residents of Scott County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 52937.4, a median household income of $45,955 in Scott County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $24,327,382 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
