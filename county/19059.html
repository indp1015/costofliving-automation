📊 Local Market Analysis

Dickinson County’s cost of living sits well below the national norm, with the overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) index at 88.8 versus the United States benchmark of 100. Goods prices—groceries, apparel, and fuel—register an index of 96.6, only modestly higher than the national average, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are markedly cheaper at 66.0. Housing costs, measured by median monthly rent of $930, align closely with the national median rent of $928, yet the county’s rental‑cost index of 87.3 confirms that landlords charge roughly 13 % less than the average American market. The combined effect is a price environment that is 11.2 % cheaper than the countrywide average, driven primarily by low service costs and modestly priced housing. The composition of expenses reveals that goods are the dominant cost driver in Dickinson County. At 96.6, the goods index is just 3.4 points shy of parity, whereas services sit 34 points below and rentals 12.7 points below. Consequently, daily life feels inexpensive in categories that dominate household budgets—energy, medical care, and restaurant meals—while the price gap in groceries and fuel remains narrow. A family spending $600 per month on groceries would encounter a price differential of roughly 3‑4 % compared with a comparable basket in a higher‑cost region, but the same family would save substantially on utility bills and health services, where local rates are two‑thirds of national levels. The net result is a budget profile where essential consumables are only slightly above average, but discretionary and recurring service expenses are significantly lower, creating an overall affordable environment. Median household income in Dickinson County stands at $74,570, outpacing the national median of $65,047 by 14.6 %. After accounting for the median rent of $930, which consumes 15 % of household earnings, the remaining disposable income translates into a Purchasing Power Index of 84,004.6. This figure indicates that residents can purchase roughly 84 % of the goods and services that a typical American household can buy with the same nominal income, reflecting the advantage of lower service costs. The county’s unemployment rate of 2.4 %—well under the national average—suggests a tight labor market, while 24 % of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, providing a skilled workforce that supports higher wages. The median age of 48.1 years points to a mature population, often associated with stable consumption patterns and lower propensity for high‑cost lifestyle choices. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped from 91.139 in 2010 to 88.769 in 2023, a 2.6 % decline, indicating that relative prices have softened even as the local economy grew. Median household income surged 30.2 % from $57,265 to $74,570, outpacing the 44.6 % rise in median rent (from $643 to $930). Although rent growth exceeded income growth, the broader price environment—particularly services—remained subdued, preserving overall affordability. The divergence between faster‑rising housing costs and slower‑moving goods and services suggests that while renters may feel pressure on their housing budget, the lower cost of everyday necessities cushions overall spending power. Prospective residents should anchor their budgeting around three pillars: housing, goods, and services. With rent representing 15 % of median earnings, a household earning the county’s median can comfortably allocate $1,200–$1,500 to utilities, healthcare, and food, given the 66.0 services index and 96.6 goods index. A prudent budget would earmark roughly $300–$350 for groceries, $150 for fuel, and $200–$250 for healthcare and utilities, leaving a discretionary margin of $400–$500 for entertainment, savings, or debt service. The low service index means that even modest spending on dining out or medical care will stretch further than in higher‑cost metros. Understanding that rent consumes a larger share of income than the national average, but that the remainder of the budget benefits from cheaper services, enables newcomers to plan a balanced household ledger that leverages Dickinson County’s relative affordability while accounting for the upward pressure on housing costs.

What this means for residents of Dickinson County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 84004.6, a median household income of $74,570 in Dickinson County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $62,642,230 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
