📊 Local Market Analysis

Ellis County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 10.1 percentage points below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 89.9 in 2023, a notable decline from 92.991 in 2010. This aggregate affordability masks a distinct bifurcation in daily expenses: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel run at 96.5, nearly parity with the U.S. baseline, while services—encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining—plummet to 68.6, the lowest component and the primary driver of the county’s cost advantage. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 90.8, slightly below the national median rent of $928, with local median rent settling at $899. Consequently, daily life is inexpensive relative to the country, but the savings are concentrated almost entirely in service consumption rather than the tangible goods households purchase weekly. The median household income of $59,889 trails the national figure of $65,047 by roughly 7.9 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 66,611.4 reveals that local wages stretch significantly further once the depressed price level is applied. Rent burden stands at a remarkably low 18.0 percent of gross income, a metric that underscores the housing affordability advantage relative to coastal metros where 30 percent thresholds are routinely breached. However, the 31.4 percent income growth since 2010 has lagged behind the 41.8 percent surge in median rent over the same period, signaling a gradual erosion of that shelter advantage. The labor market supports this dynamic with a tight 3.2 percent unemployment rate, though the 24.9 percent bachelor’s degree attainment rate and a young median age of 32.5 suggest an economy tilted toward early-career service and trade roles rather than high-wage knowledge sectors. Over the last decade, the RPP’s 3.3 percent decline indicates that local consumer prices have effectively fallen in real terms against the national basket, a rare deflationary trend in a generally inflationary environment. This drop has amplified the real value of the 31.4 percent nominal income gain, boosting purchasing power more than the raw wage figure suggests. Yet the divergence between goods inflation—sticky near the national index—and the deep discount on services implies that households feel the pinch at the grocery store and gas pump just as acutely as the average American, while catching a break on medical visits, utility bills, and restaurant meals. The demographic profile reinforces this: a younger, renting population with moderate educational attainment benefits disproportionately from low service costs and rent levels but lacks the income elasticity to absorb further rental acceleration. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the asymmetry between fixed and variable costs. The sub-$900 median rent and low service index create a high floor of disposable income after shelter and utilities, but the goods index at 96.5 means grocery carts and fuel tanks offer little discount. A household moving from a high-cost metro should not expect proportional savings on consumables; the windfall arrives in the monthly lease, the electric bill, and the clinic copay. With rent appreciation outpacing wage growth by over ten percentage points since 2010, the primary budget risk is not current affordability but the velocity of shelter cost increases in a market where inventory constraints could push that 18 percent burden ratio upward quickly.

What this means for residents of Ellis County, Kansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 66611.4, a median household income of $59,889 in Ellis County, Kansas has the equivalent buying power of $39,892,901 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 90.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
