📊 Local Market Analysis

Bullock County’s cost of living sits well below the national benchmark, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 89.1 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices are the most elevated component at 94.6, still under the national level, while services are markedly cheaper at 61.6. Housing costs, measured by rental‑price parity, register at 89.9, indicating that renting a typical unit costs roughly 10 percent less than the average American household. The composite picture is one of a region where everyday expenses are generally affordable, especially in the service sector, but where the modest premium on goods offsets some of the savings. The dominant driver of the local cost structure is the goods category, which accounts for the highest index value among the three components. Although still below the national average, the 94.6 reading suggests that groceries, apparel, and fuel cost about 5 percent more than the U.S. mean, narrowing the gap created by the deep discount on services. Rental costs, at 89.9, reinforce the overall affordability theme: a median monthly rent of $635 is 31 percent lower than the national median of $928. Consequently, daily life in Bullock County is inexpensive relative to the broader economy, primarily because utilities, healthcare, and dining out are priced at roughly two‑thirds of the national level. The modestly higher goods price reflects limited local competition and transportation costs for imported items, but it does not outweigh the substantial savings in services and housing. Disposable income calculations reveal a constrained purchasing power despite the low cost environment. The median household earns $36,723 annually, which translates to $3,060 per month before taxes. After allocating $635 to rent—a 20.7 percent rent burden—the remaining $2,425 must cover utilities, food, transportation, and discretionary spending. The Purchasing Power Index of 41,216.9, when juxtaposed with the national figure (implicitly 100,000), indicates that residents can purchase roughly 41 percent of the goods and services that an average American household can afford with comparable income. In practical terms, a family of four would need to stretch each dollar considerably more than a counterpart in a higher‑cost area, even though absolute expenditures on housing and services are lower. Demographic characteristics shape this economic reality. Only 6.7 percent of the adult population holds a bachelor’s degree or higher, limiting access to higher‑wage occupations and reinforcing the modest median income. The median age of 40.4 years points to a mature workforce, while the 5.9 percent unemployment rate hovers near the national average, suggesting a relatively stable but not booming labor market. The limited educational attainment and modest job growth keep wages anchored, which in turn sustains the low‑price equilibrium for services and housing. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped from 89.78 in 2010 to 89.10 in 2023, a 0.8 percent decline, indicating that consumer prices have softened slightly relative to the nation. Median household income, however, rose 15.0 percent from $31,938 to $36,723, outpacing the 11.4 percent increase in median rent ($570 to $635). The faster growth in earnings relative to housing costs suggests that wages have kept modestly ahead of expenses, enhancing the ability of residents to absorb price changes, though the gains remain modest compared with national income growth. Prospective residents should budget with the goods premium in mind, allocating a larger share of their monthly income to groceries, fuel, and apparel than they might expect given the low rent and service costs. With rent consuming roughly one‑fifth of household earnings, the remaining 80 percent must stretch across utilities, transportation, and a goods basket that costs about 5 percent above the national average. Planning for a higher proportion of discretionary spending on goods, while taking advantage of the inexpensive service sector, will be essential for maintaining a balanced household budget in Bullock County.

What this means for residents of Bullock County, Alabama

With a Purchasing Power Index of 41216.9, a median household income of $36,723 in Bullock County, Alabama has the equivalent buying power of $15,136,082 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 89.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
