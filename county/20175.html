📊 Local Market Analysis

Seward County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 10.1 percentage points below the national benchmark, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity for 2023 registering 89.9 against a U.S. average of 100. This aggregate figure masks significant divergence across consumption categories: goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — run at 96.5, only marginally cheaper than the national norm, while services including healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 68.6, the lowest component by a wide margin. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 90.8, meaning housing expenditure tracks closer to goods than to the deeply discounted service economy. The consumption breakdown reveals that daily life in Seward County is defined by cheap services rather than broadly low prices. A household's monthly budget benefits disproportionately from utility bills, medical visits, and restaurant meals that cost roughly two-thirds of what the average American pays, while the weekly grocery run and fuel stops deliver only modest savings. This structure favors residents who consume services heavily — families with children requiring pediatric care, older adults managing chronic conditions, or households outsourcing domestic labor — but offers less relief to single-person households whose spending skews toward goods. The rent burden of 17.8 percent of median income, against a national median rent of $928 versus the local $924, confirms housing affordability is not the primary driver of the low overall index; rather, it is the service discount that pulls the composite down. Purchasing power data quantifies the advantage: the Purchasing Power Index of 69,258.6 exceeds the nominal median household income of $62,269 by roughly $7,000, indicating that a dollar earned locally commands significantly more consumption than a dollar earned nationally. However, that nominal median still trails the national median of $65,047 by 4.3 percent, a gap that narrows but does not disappear after adjusting for local prices. The labor market context — a 4.2 percent unemployment rate, a median age of 31.0, and only 9.6 percent of adults holding a bachelor's degree or higher — suggests a workforce concentrated in younger, less-credentialed occupations where wage growth often lags productivity gains, potentially limiting the durability of the purchasing power premium. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP declined 3.3 percent from 92.991 to 89.908, even as median income rose 32.1 percent from $47,134 to $62,269 and median rent climbed 39.2 percent from $664 to $924. Wages have outpaced the overall cost level, but rent growth has exceeded income growth by a meaningful margin, gradually eroding the shelter affordability edge. For a prospective resident, the budgeting takeaway is clear: allocate a larger share of income to groceries and transportation than the national average would suggest, expect notably lower outlays for healthcare and utilities, and monitor rental escalation closely — it has been the fastest-moving cost component for over a decade.

What this means for residents of Seward County, Kansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 69258.6, a median household income of $62,269 in Seward County, Kansas has the equivalent buying power of $43,126,638 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 90.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
