📊 Local Market Analysis

Glascock County’s cost of living sits modestly below the national norm, with the overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) index at 96.5 versus the United States benchmark of 100. Goods prices are the closest to the national level at 97.7, while services are markedly cheaper at 88.3, and rental costs trail at 92.3. The median household income of $55,250 also lags the national median of $65,047 by roughly 15 %, underscoring a price environment that is less expensive but also paired with lower earnings. The composition of expenses reveals that services drive the county’s affordability advantage. At an index of 88.3, healthcare, utilities, and dining out cost about 12 % less than the average American. By contrast, goods—groceries, apparel, and fuel—are only 2.3 % cheaper than the national average, indicating that everyday necessities such as food and transportation do not enjoy the same discount. Rental costs, measured by median monthly rent of $813, are 12 % below the national median rent of $928, yet they have risen 71.9 % since 2010, outpacing the 35.6 % increase in median income over the same period. The rent‑burden metric shows that housing consumes 17.7 % of household income, well under the 30 % threshold commonly used to flag affordability stress, suggesting that, despite rapid rent growth, residents still allocate a relatively small share of earnings to housing. Disposable income calculations illustrate the net purchasing power of the median worker. After deducting the median rent of $813 (approximately $9,756 annually) from the median household income, roughly $45,494 remains for all other expenditures. The Purchasing Power Index of 57,257.4 translates this residual income into a dollar‑for‑dollar comparison with a national baseline of 100, indicating that a Glascock household can buy about 57 % of the goods and services that a median U.S. household can afford, after accounting for local price levels. This gap reflects both the lower income and the modest price advantage, especially in services. Demographic factors shape the lived experience of cost pressures. The unemployment rate of 1.1 % signals a tight labor market, yet only 13.8 % of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, limiting access to higher‑paying occupations that typically accompany advanced education. The median age of 43.2 years points to a mature population, likely with established consumption patterns and potentially lower discretionary spending on items such as entertainment or travel. The combination of low unemployment and limited educational attainment suggests that while jobs are plentiful, they may be concentrated in lower‑wage sectors, reinforcing the modest income profile. Over the past decade, the RPP index climbed from 94.574 to 96.494, a 2.0 % rise, indicating that overall price pressures have been mild. In contrast, median household income surged 35.6 % from $40,759 to $55,250, outpacing the modest price increase and expanding real purchasing power. However, rental costs accelerated faster than income, rising 71.9 % from $473 to $813, which could erode future affordability if the trend continues. Prospective residents should budget with the knowledge that everyday goods will cost near national levels, while services and housing are comparatively cheap. Allocating roughly 18 % of income to rent leaves about 82 % for food, transportation, healthcare, and discretionary spending. Given the low service cost index, dining out and utility bills will stretch dollars further, but grocery bills will reflect near‑average prices. Planning for a modest rent increase—historically steep—will be prudent, as will accounting for the limited high‑wage job pool implied by low educational attainment. A realistic monthly budget might allocate $300–$350 to groceries, $150 to utilities and internet, $100 to transportation fuel, and $150–$200 to healthcare and insurance, leaving discretionary funds for savings or entertainment. This framework aligns household outlays with the county’s price structure while respecting the median income ceiling.

What this means for residents of Glascock County, Georgia

With a Purchasing Power Index of 57257.4, a median household income of $55,250 in Glascock County, Georgia has the equivalent buying power of $31,634,714 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 92.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
