📊 Local Market Analysis

Kenai Peninsula Borough’s cost of living sits modestly above the national baseline, with the overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) index at 103.3 versus the United States average of 100. Goods—groceries, apparel, and fuel—register a 103.7 index, indicating a 3.7 % premium over the countrywide price level, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are priced 3.3 % below national norms at 96.7. Housing, measured by rental costs, is the outlier: the rental component of the RPP climbs to 113.3, a 13.3 % surcharge that drives the borough’s total cost structure. Median household income in the region is $77,722, outpacing the national median of $65,047 by 19.6 %, yet the rent burden—defined as rent as a share of income—remains modest at 18.0 % of earnings, well under the 30 % threshold commonly cited as a stress point. The dominant influence of housing on the local price picture means daily life feels expensive despite cheaper services. A resident spending the national average on groceries would encounter a 3–4 % price lift, but the savings on utilities and dining partially offset that increase. The true cost driver is the $1,163 median monthly rent, which exceeds the national median rent of $928 by 25.9 %. Even with a higher median income, the net disposable income after rent is $71,559 annually, or roughly $5,963 per month. The Purchasing Power Index of 75,241.3—derived from the ratio of median income to the overall RPP—confirms that residents retain about 75 % of the purchasing power of an average American household, reflecting a modest gap created primarily by elevated housing costs. Demographic and labor‑market characteristics shape this picture. The borough’s unemployment rate of 6.9 % trails the national average, suggesting a relatively stable labor supply, yet only 19.1 % of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, limiting the pool of high‑skill, high‑wage workers. A median age of 41.5 years points to a mature population, often associated with higher household formation and steady consumption patterns. These factors together temper wage growth, as the 22 % rise in median income since 2010 (from $63,684 to $77,722) barely eclipses the 23.7 % increase in median rent (from $940 to $1,163). The RPP itself has moved only 0.2 % upward over the same period, indicating that price inflation has been muted while housing costs have surged, eroding the net benefit of higher earnings. Prospective residents should calibrate budgets around the pronounced housing premium. Allocating roughly 18 % of gross income to rent leaves about 82 % for all other expenses; with services priced below national levels, utilities and healthcare will consume less than the typical American household. Grocery bills will be marginally higher, so a 5 % buffer above the national average is prudent. Given the median disposable income of $5,963 per month after rent, a realistic monthly budget might allocate $600–$700 to food, $300 to transportation and fuel, $250 to healthcare, and $200–$300 to discretionary spending. Maintaining a cushion for the 6.9 % unemployment risk and the limited local supply of high‑skill jobs will help households preserve financial resilience in a market where housing remains the primary cost pressure.

What this means for residents of Kenai Peninsula Borough, Alaska

With a Purchasing Power Index of 75241.3, a median household income of $77,722 in Kenai Peninsula Borough, Alaska has the equivalent buying power of $58,479,043 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 113.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
