📊 Local Market Analysis

Kenai Peninsula Borough’s cost of living sits modestly above the national norm, with the overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) index at 103.3 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices—groceries, apparel, and fuel—track closely at 103.7, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are 3.3 % cheaper than the country average at 96.7. Housing, measured by median monthly rent, is the outlier: the rent index of 113.3 signals that renters pay roughly 13 % more than the typical American household. The median rent of $1,163 eclipses the national median of $928, confirming housing as the primary driver of the borough’s price structure. The composition of expenses reveals a mixed affordability picture. Everyday purchases—food, clothing, fuel—are only slightly higher than elsewhere, suggesting that routine consumption does not impose a heavy burden. Conversely, the elevated rent level pushes the overall cost structure upward, offsetting the savings in services. With a rent‑burden rate of 18 % of household income, residents allocate a smaller share of earnings to housing than the national rent‑burden threshold of 30 %, yet the absolute dollar amount remains sizable. Because services are below national levels, discretionary spending on dining out or personal care may stretch farther, but the net effect is a household budget that feels tighter on the housing front while remaining relatively comfortable on other fronts. Disposable income calculations underscore this nuance. The median household income of $77,722 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 19.6 %, positioning Kenai residents above average earners. After subtracting the median rent, the median family retains roughly $66,559 annually, or $5,546 per month for non‑housing expenses. The Purchasing Power Index of 75,241.3—well below the national benchmark of 100—reflects that, despite higher earnings, the elevated cost of housing erodes real purchasing power. In practical terms, a family can afford fewer goods and services than a counterpart in a lower‑cost region with comparable income. Demographic factors shape this dynamic. The borough’s unemployment rate of 6.9 % exceeds the national average, limiting the pool of supplemental earnings that could offset higher rents. Only 19.1 % of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting a labor market concentrated in sectors such as tourism, fishing, and public services, which typically offer wages below those in high‑skill industries. The median age of 41.5 years points to a mature workforce, likely with established household responsibilities and less flexibility to absorb cost spikes. These characteristics combine to produce a cost‑of‑living experience where stable, moderate incomes must be carefully allocated to cover a disproportionately expensive housing component. Over the past decade, the RPP rose marginally from 103.099 in 2010 to 103.297 in 2023—a 0.2 % increase, indicating price stability across the board. Median household income, however, surged 22 % from $63,684 to $77,722, outpacing the 23.7 % rise in median rent ($940 to $1,163). Although both income and rent grew, the slightly faster rent inflation narrows the gap between earnings and housing costs, tempering gains in disposable income. The modest RPP shift suggests that non‑housing prices have remained largely flat, reinforcing the view that housing is the singular pressure point. Prospective residents should anchor their budgets around the $1,163 monthly rent figure, allocating roughly 18 % of projected income to housing to avoid excessive burden. With services priced below national levels, budgeting for utilities, healthcare, and dining can be modest; however, the higher cost of goods means grocery bills will be about 3.7 % above the national average. A prudent household plan would reserve at least 30 % of net income for discretionary spending after covering rent and essential services, ensuring a buffer against the limited local labor market flexibility and the borough’s elevated housing costs.

What this means for residents of Kenai Peninsula Borough, Alaska

With a Purchasing Power Index of 75241.3, a median household income of $77,722 in Kenai Peninsula Borough, Alaska has the equivalent buying power of $58,479,043 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 113.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
