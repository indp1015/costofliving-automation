📊 Local Market Analysis

North Slope Borough’s cost of living sits modestly above the national baseline, with the overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) index at 103.3 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices—groceries, apparel, and fuel—track closely at 103.7, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are 3.3 % cheaper than the national average at 96.7. Housing, measured by median monthly rent, is the outlier: the rental index of 113.3 signals rents that are 13.3 % higher than the countrywide norm. The median rent of $1,167 eclipses the national median of $928, yet the rent‑burden ratio of 16.2 % of household income remains comfortably below the 30 % threshold often cited as a stress point. The rental sector drives the borough’s cost structure. Although everyday goods are only marginally pricier and services are slightly cheaper, the elevated rent pushes the overall expense profile upward. A household earning the local median income of $86,313 faces a monthly rent bill that consumes roughly one‑sixth of its earnings, leaving $71,146 for all other expenditures. After accounting for the 96.7 service index, the Purchasing Power Index (PPI) of 83,558.1 indicates that, on a comparable basket of goods and services, residents retain about 83 % of the purchasing power of an average American household. In dollar terms, the median worker’s discretionary income after rent and typical service costs hovers near $4,200 per month, a figure that, while higher than the national median income, is partially offset by the higher housing outlay. Demographically, the borough’s labor market is characterized by a relatively young median age of 35.2 years and a low college attainment rate of 8.9 % with a 6.6 % unemployment rate. The modest educational attainment suggests a labor force concentrated in sectors such as energy extraction and government services, where wages have risen faster than the national average—median income climbed 18.9 % since 2010, outpacing the 7.4 % increase in rent over the same period. This wage growth cushions the impact of higher housing costs, but the limited pool of highly skilled workers may constrain upward pressure on salaries in knowledge‑intensive occupations. Over the past decade the RPP index has moved from 103.099 to 103.297, a negligible 0.2 % rise, indicating price stability across most consumer categories. Meanwhile, median household income surged from $72,576 to $86,313 (+18.9 %), comfortably outstripping the modest rent increase. The divergence suggests that wages have kept pace with, and indeed exceeded, the incremental cost pressures. Prospective residents should budget for a rent commitment that consumes roughly one‑seventh of gross earnings, allocate a slightly lower share to services than the national average, and anticipate modest price growth in goods. A realistic monthly budget would therefore prioritize securing housing within the $1,100‑$1,200 range, planning for utilities and healthcare costs that are marginally below national levels, and reserving the remaining income for food, transportation, and discretionary spending, recognizing that the overall purchasing power remains about 17 % below that of a typical U.S. household.

What this means for residents of North Slope Borough, Alaska

With a Purchasing Power Index of 83558.1, a median household income of $86,313 in North Slope Borough, Alaska has the equivalent buying power of $72,121,503 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 113.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
