📊 Local Market Analysis

Page County, Iowa, registers an overall Regional Price Parity of 88.8, positioning local consumer prices 11.2 percent below the national benchmark and establishing the area as a distinctly low-cost environment for household budgets. The composite figure masks significant internal variation: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel carry an index of 96.6, nearly converging with national pricing, while rental costs sit at 87.3. The dominant deflationary force is the services category, which clocks in at a remarkable 66.0—34 percent below the U.S. average—driven by substantially lower outlays for healthcare, utilities, and dining. This structural divergence means daily life is inexpensive primarily because labor-intensive services are cheap, whereas the unavoidable, recurring expense of goods offers residents little arbitrage relative to metropolitan counterparts. The consumption breakdown reveals that goods function as the highest cost component at 96.6, effectively anchoring the local cost structure near parity for tangible necessities. A median household income of $57,752 translates into a Purchasing Power Index of 65,058.7, indicating that after adjusting for the 88.8 price level, the typical family commands roughly $7,300 more in real purchasing capacity than their nominal earnings suggest. Housing affordability reinforces this advantage: median monthly rent of $731 consumes only 15.2 percent of gross income, a fraction of the national median rent of $928 and well below standard burden thresholds. Consequently, disposable income retention is high, allowing households to absorb the near-national pricing of fuel and groceries without severe friction. Local demographics and labor dynamics contextualize this affordability. An unemployment rate of 5.7 percent exceeds the national norm, signaling slack in the labor market that likely suppresses wage growth and service-sector pricing simultaneously. The workforce skews older, with a median age of 45.0 years, and educational attainment is modest—only 13.0 percent hold a bachelor’s degree or higher—factors that correlate with the region’s concentration in sectors where wage pressure remains muted. This demographic profile sustains the low services index; cheaper labor inputs keep healthcare, personal care, and hospitality costs down, creating a feedback loop where low wages beget low service prices, which in turn preserve real purchasing power despite nominal incomes trailing the national median of $65,047 by over $7,000. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP declined from 91.1 to 88.8, a 2.6 percent drop in relative price levels, while nominal median income surged 31.5 percent from $43,912 to $57,752. Median rent increased 29.6 percent over the same span, from $564 to $731, nearly matching income growth but starting from a much lower base. Wages have not merely kept pace with expenses; they have outpaced the local inflation trajectory, expanding real purchasing power considerably. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus is clear: allocate near-national budgets for groceries and transportation, expect significant savings on rent and services, and recognize that the local labor market offers limited high-wage mobility. The primary financial risk lies not in cost-of-living spikes but in income stagnation should the household rely on local employment rather than remote or transfer income.

What this means for residents of Page County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 65058.7, a median household income of $57,752 in Page County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $37,572,700 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
