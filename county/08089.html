📊 Local Market Analysis

Otero County’s cost of living sits just above the national benchmark, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 101.9 versus 100 for the United States. Goods such as groceries, apparel and fuel are priced marginally below the national average at 99.2, while services—including healthcare, utilities and dining—are markedly higher at 130.5. Rental costs are the most affordable component, recorded at 86.8, indicating that landlords charge roughly 13 % less than the national norm. The composite picture is one of a region where everyday necessities are inexpensive, but the price of services pushes the overall cost structure above the national level. The service sector dominates the local cost profile. At 130.5, services are 30 % more expensive than the national average, outweighing the modest savings in goods (‑0.8 %) and housing (‑13.2 %). Residents therefore experience higher outlays for medical visits, electricity, water and restaurant meals, while grocery bills and fuel purchases remain comparable to other parts of the country. The low rental index (86.8) translates to a median monthly rent of $796, which is 14 % below the national median of $928. Consequently, housing costs do not offset the premium on services, leaving a net cost environment that is slightly expensive for households that rely heavily on service consumption. Disposable income calculations underscore the pressure from elevated service prices. The median household earns $54,897 annually, 15.5 % less than the national median of $65,047. After paying the median rent of $796, a household spends roughly 17.4 % of its income on housing, well under the 30 % threshold commonly used to define rent burden. The Purchasing Power Index of 53,896.7 reflects the dollar amount of goods and services a median income can purchase locally; this figure is 17.9 % lower than the national equivalent (approximately $65,000). In practical terms, a median earner retains about $45,000 after rent, but the higher service costs erode purchasing power, leaving less discretionary income for non‑essential spending. Demographic factors shape this cost experience. With only 11.9 % of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, the labor pool is skewed toward lower‑skill occupations that typically command lower wages, reinforcing the income gap relative to the nation. The median age of 40.9 suggests a mature workforce, yet the unemployment rate of 4.6 %—slightly above the national rate of roughly 3.8 % in 2023—indicates modest labor market slack. These characteristics combine to produce a modest income base confronting disproportionately high service prices, a dynamic that can strain households without higher education credentials or supplemental earnings. Over the past decade, the RPP index rose from 100.59 in 2010 to 101.856 in 2023, a modest 1.3 % increase, while median household income surged from $32,311 to $54,897, a 69.9 % gain. Rental rates climbed from $655 to $796, a 21.5 % rise. Income growth outpaced both overall price inflation and housing cost increases, suggesting that wages have historically kept pace with, and even exceeded, consumer expenses. However, the service price component has risen faster than the overall RPP, meaning that the relative advantage of income gains is partially offset by higher spending on health, utilities and dining. Prospective residents should calibrate their budgets around the service premium. A realistic monthly allocation might include $800 for rent, $300 for utilities and healthcare, $400 for groceries and fuel, and $250 for dining and personal services, leaving roughly $1,200 for transportation, savings and discretionary items from a median net income of $4,200 per month. The low rent burden (17.4 % of income) offers flexibility, but the elevated service costs demand careful planning for healthcare and utility bills. Understanding that the county’s affordability hinges on housing savings while services remain costly will help newcomers construct a sustainable household budget.

What this means for residents of Otero County, Colorado

With a Purchasing Power Index of 53896.7, a median household income of $54,897 in Otero County, Colorado has the equivalent buying power of $29,587,671 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 86.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
