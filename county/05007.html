📊 Local Market Analysis

Benton County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 13.2 percent below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 86.8 in 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals a distinct bifurcation in daily expenses. Goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—represent the highest local cost component at 93.1, only marginally cheaper than the national baseline, while services including healthcare, utilities, and dining plummet to 56.7, nearly half the national price level. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 81.9, meaning housing expenditure runs roughly 18 percent below the U.S. average. This profile suggests that while discretionary shopping and transportation fuel costs track closely with national norms, the essential service basket—medical visits, electricity, and restaurant meals—delivers substantial savings that anchor the county’s overall affordability advantage. The median household income of $89,879 towers 38.2 percent above the national median of $65,047, translating into a Purchasing Power Index of 103,535.3 that effectively grants residents a six-figure standard of living on paper. Rent burden remains remarkably low at 15.9 percent of gross income, with the median monthly rent of $1,193 consuming a far smaller slice of the paycheck than the national median rent of $928 does for the typical U.S. earner. Over the last decade, this dynamic has improved: the RPP declined 2.0 percent from 88.6 to 86.8 while median income surged 59.8 percent from $56,239, outpacing the 49.9 percent rise in median rent from $796. Wage growth has not merely kept pace with shelter costs; it has widened the margin for discretionary spending and savings accumulation. Local labor market conditions reinforce this purchasing power trajectory. An unemployment rate of 3.1 percent signals a tight market that supports wage negotiation, while a median age of 35.8 years indicates a workforce in prime earning years. However, the 24.0 percent bachelor’s degree attainment rate trails many high-income metros, suggesting the income premium is driven less by dense knowledge-economy clustering and more by the presence of major corporate headquarters and logistics operations that elevate the regional wage floor. This demographic mix creates a consumer base with high liquidity but potentially different consumption patterns than coastal peers, sustaining demand for the goods category where prices remain sticky near national levels. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the divergence between sticky goods pricing and deeply discounted services. A household relocating from a major metro should anticipate grocery and fuel bills that feel familiar, but healthcare premiums, utility bills, and dining-out tabs that may drop by 40 percent or more. The low rent burden—roughly $200 below what the national median income earner pays proportionally—frees cash flow for vehicle ownership, childcare, or accelerated debt repayment. The critical variable remains the goods basket: because it sits at 93.1, any inflationary pressure on food or energy transmits almost directly to the local wallet, limiting the insulation the low service costs otherwise provide.

What this means for residents of Benton County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 103535.3, a median household income of $89,879 in Benton County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $93,056,492 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
