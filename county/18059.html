📊 Local Market Analysis

Hancock County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 7.9 percent below the national average, reflected in an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 92.1 for 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals distinct pressure points for household budgets. Goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—represent the highest local cost component at an index of 95.6, only 4.4 percent below the national baseline, meaning daily consumables offer minimal savings relative to the broader U.S. market. Conversely, the Services category, covering healthcare, utilities, and dining, plunges to 71.3, a staggering 28.7 percent discount that acts as the primary engine suppressing the aggregate index. Rental costs sit at 84.7, roughly 15 percent below national levels, positioning housing as a moderate relief valve rather than a deep bargain. This bifurcated price environment creates a consumption dynamic where fixed, recurring service obligations are unusually affordable, but the variable, high-frequency spending on goods erodes the theoretical advantage of the low overall index. The median household income of $91,326 towers 40.4 percent above the national median of $65,047, translating into a Purchasing Power Index of 99,163—effectively meaning the median dollar earned in Hancock County commands nearly $99,200 worth of national-average goods and services. Rent burden sits at a remarkably low 14.7 percent of gross income, with median monthly rent at $1,121 compared to the national $928, a gap far narrower than the income disparity suggests. Consequently, the median worker retains substantial disposable income after shelter costs, though the near-parity pricing of groceries and fuel ensures that a significant portion of that surplus flows back into non-discretionary goods consumption rather than pure savings. Demographic and labor market fundamentals reinforce this high-earning, moderate-cost equilibrium. An unemployment rate of 2.7 percent signals an exceptionally tight labor market that sustains wage premiums, while a median age of 40.2 years indicates a workforce in prime earning years. However, with only 23.1 percent of adults holding a bachelor’s degree or higher—below typical thresholds for high-income metros—the income strength appears driven by industrial or skilled-trade employment clusters rather than knowledge-economy concentration. This composition suggests the cost advantages in services may stem from lower local labor intensity pricing in healthcare and personal care, while the goods index reflects the inescapable logistics and commodity pricing of the broader Midwest supply chain. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP drifted marginally lower from 93.0 to 92.1, a negligible 1.0 percent decline, while nominal median income surged 37.1 percent against a 34.7 percent rise in median rent. Real income growth has marginally outpaced shelter inflation, yet the persistence of the goods index near 96 implies that household budgets remain exposed to national food and energy volatility. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that feel virtually identical to higher-cost metros, while anticipating meaningful discounts on insurance premiums, utility bills, and restaurant meals. The financial calculus hinges on leveraging the service-sector arbitrage to offset the unavoidable retail parity of daily essentials.

What this means for residents of Hancock County, Indiana
