📊 Local Market Analysis

The cost of living in Colquitt County sits modestly below the national benchmark, with the overall Relative Price Parity (RPP) index at 96.5 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices—groceries, apparel, and fuel—register an index of 97.7, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are markedly cheaper at 88.3. Housing, measured by median monthly rent, contributes a rental cost index of 92.3, reflecting a rent level of $779 that is 16 % lower than the national median of $928. Collectively, these figures indicate that everyday purchases in the county are generally less expensive than the average American market, especially in service‑related categories. The composition of the local cost structure is driven primarily by the goods segment, which, at 97.7, is the highest of the three components. However, even the goods index remains under the national average, suggesting that staple items are only marginally pricier than the national norm. Services, at 88.3, provide the greatest relief to household budgets, lowering the effective cost of living. Rental costs, while not the cheapest, are still below national levels, contributing to a rent‑burden ratio of 18.8 % of median household income—well under the 30 % threshold commonly cited as a stress point. The net effect is a relatively affordable daily life, where lower service and housing expenses offset the near‑national price of goods. Median household income in Colquitt County stands at $49,691, roughly 24 % below the national median of $65,047. After accounting for the average rent of $779, a typical household retains approximately $45,912 annually for discretionary spending, utilities, food, and transportation. The Purchasing Power Index of 51,496.5 quantifies this residual buying capacity, indicating that residents can purchase roughly 51.5 % of the national basket of goods and services with their after‑rent income. While the index is lower than the national average, the combination of cheaper services and housing cushions the impact of lower wages, preserving a modest but tangible level of disposable income. Demographically, the county’s labor market is characterized by a 4.1 % unemployment rate and a low educational attainment—only 8.6 % hold a bachelor’s degree or higher. The median age of 37.2 years aligns with a working‑age population, yet the limited share of highly educated workers correlates with the lower median income. The labor market’s modest unemployment figure suggests relative job stability, but the earnings profile remains constrained by the occupational mix typical of a region with limited higher‑skill employment opportunities. Consequently, residents experience a cost of living that is calibrated to their income realities, with affordable services and housing offsetting the scarcity of high‑wage jobs. Over the past decade, the RPP rose from 94.574 in 2010 to 96.494 in 2023, a modest 2 % increase, while median household income surged from $32,409 to $49,691, a 53.3 % gain. Rental costs climbed from $598 to $779, a 30.3 % rise. Income growth outpaced both price inflation and rent escalation, expanding real purchasing power and reducing the proportion of income devoted to housing. The divergence between wage growth and cost increases underscores a favorable trend for household budgets, though the gains are tempered by the county’s persistent lag behind national income levels. Prospective residents should anchor their budgeting on three pillars: allocate roughly 19 % of income to rent, anticipate service expenses that are 12 % cheaper than the national average, and plan for goods costs that hover near parity with broader markets. With a median monthly rent of $779, a household earning the county median can comfortably cover housing and retain over $3,800 per month for food, transportation, healthcare, and discretionary spending. Awareness of the lower service cost base can guide choices toward local healthcare providers and utility plans, while the near‑national price of groceries and fuel suggests that savings will primarily derive from housing and service categories rather than from everyday consumables.
