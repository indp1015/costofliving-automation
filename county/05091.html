📊 Local Market Analysis

Miller County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 13.2 percent below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 86.8 in 2023. This aggregate figure masks a sharp bifurcation in daily expenses: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel run only 6.9 percent below the national baseline at 93.1, while services—including healthcare, utilities, and dining—plummet to 56.7, nearly half the national price level. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 81.9, making shelter the single most expensive line item relative to other local categories, though still significantly cheaper than the U.S. median rent of $928. The median household income of $49,983 trails the national median by roughly 23 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 57,577.5 indicates that local wages stretch considerably further than nominal figures suggest. A rent burden of 21.2 percent—well below the standard 30 percent affordability threshold—confirms that housing consumes a manageable share of the monthly budget, leaving discretionary room for the higher relative cost of goods. However, the labor market context complicates this picture; an unemployment rate of 7.2 percent and a bachelor’s degree attainment rate of just 9.4 percent signal a workforce concentrated in lower-wage sectors, limiting upward mobility even as the cost floor remains low. Over the past decade, the RPP has drifted downward from 88.6 to 86.8, a modest 2.0 percent decline in relative price levels. During the same period, median income rose 24.0 percent from $40,317, nearly matching the 24.6 percent increase in median rent from $708 to $882. This parity suggests that while nominal purchasing power has improved, the primary driver of household expense—shelter—has absorbed virtually all wage gains at the median, preventing a meaningful reduction in the rent-to-income ratio over time. For a prospective resident, the budgeting reality is defined by cheap services offsetting sticky goods prices. Utility bills and healthcare outlays will likely feel negligible compared to coastal or urban benchmarks, but the weekly grocery run and fuel costs will feel only marginally discounted. With a median age of 39.0, the population skews toward prime working years, yet the educational profile suggests limited high-income earning potential locally. Households should calibrate expectations around a goods-heavy consumption basket where savings on rent and services are partially eroded by the necessity of purchasing nationally priced commodities.

What this means for residents of Miller County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 57577.5, a median household income of $49,983 in Miller County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $28,778,962 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
