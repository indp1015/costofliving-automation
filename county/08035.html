📊 Local Market Analysis

Douglas County residents navigate a cost structure that sits marginally above the national baseline, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity index registering 101.9 for 2023 against a national average of 100. This aggregate figure masks a stark bifurcation in household budgets: services command a premium of 30.5 percent above the national norm at 130.5, while goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel track slightly below average at 99.2. The most significant deviation appears in rental costs, which register at 86.8—13.2 percent cheaper than the U.S. average—creating a rare dynamic where shelter expenses exert downward pressure on the overall index despite elevated service pricing. The consumption breakdown reveals that daily life in the county is defined by the service economy rather than housing or retail markets. Healthcare, utilities, and dining out constitute the primary inflationary drivers for local households, meaning discretionary spending on medical care, childcare, and restaurant meals absorbs a disproportionate share of income compared to peer jurisdictions. While the rental index of 86.8 suggests relative affordability for tenants, the median monthly rent of $2,095 consumes 17.3 percent of the $145,737 median household income—a low burden ratio facilitated by earnings that exceed the national median of $65,047 by 124 percent. This income advantage translates into a Purchasing Power Index of 143,081.4, indicating that after adjusting for local prices, the median household retains purchasing capacity significantly higher than the nominal national benchmark. Demographic fundamentals reinforce this high-earning, service-intensive profile. An unemployment rate of 3.6 percent signals a tight labor market, while a bachelor’s degree attainment rate of 38.9 percent and a median age of 39.5 years describe a workforce concentrated in professional, technical, and managerial sectors that command wages sufficient to offset the 130.5 services index. The labor market structure suggests that the elevated service costs are partly endogenous—driven by high local demand for premium healthcare, dining, and personal services from a highly educated, high-income population—rather than purely a function of supply constraints. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the RPP drifted upward a modest 1.3 percent from 100.59 to 101.856, yet nominal median income surged 41.5 percent from $102,964 to $145,737. Median rent accelerated faster at 49.7 percent, climbing from $1,399 to $2,095, though the rent-to-income ratio remains contained at 17.3 percent. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the services premium: a household relocating from an average-cost metro should anticipate allocating roughly 30 percent more for healthcare premiums, utility bills, and restaurant meals, while capturing modest savings on rental outlays relative to income. The data indicates that maintaining a standard of living equivalent to the national median requires substantially higher nominal earnings, but the local labor market appears structured to deliver them.

What this means for residents of Douglas County, Colorado

With a Purchasing Power Index of 143081.4, a median household income of $145,737 in Douglas County, Colorado has the equivalent buying power of $208,522,540 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 86.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
