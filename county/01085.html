📊 Local Market Analysis

Lowndes County’s cost of living sits well below the national benchmark, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) indicating an overall index of 89.1 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices—groceries, apparel, and fuel—register at 94.6, still under the national level but the highest component within the county. Services, encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining, are markedly inexpensive at 61.6, while rental costs are modestly lower than the national average, reflected in a housing index of 89.9. The disparity between goods and services suggests that everyday necessities such as food and transportation cost roughly five percent less than elsewhere, whereas discretionary expenditures like restaurant meals and medical services are nearly forty percent cheaper. The cost structure is driven primarily by the goods sector, which accounts for the bulk of consumer outlays. Even though services are the cheapest segment, the modestly elevated goods index means that residents spend a larger share of their budget on items that cannot be postponed. Median monthly rent of $663—22.6 % of median household income—places housing pressure well under the national rent‑burden threshold of 30 %, indicating that shelter costs are relatively affordable. However, the combination of a low services index and a goods index only slightly below national levels translates into a daily life that feels inexpensive in terms of discretionary spending but only moderately cheap for essential purchases. The net effect is a household budget where the biggest strain comes from grocery and fuel bills rather than from utilities or dining out. Purchasing power in Lowndes County is quantified by an index of 39,462.6, a figure that captures the real income left after accounting for local price levels. With a median household income of $35,160, the median resident retains roughly $19,500 of discretionary earnings after covering the average cost of goods, services, and rent. By contrast, the national median income of $65,047 paired with a cost‑of‑living index of 100 yields a higher absolute disposable income but also higher absolute expenses. The county’s lower rent burden and cheap services mean that a larger proportion of income is available for savings or non‑essential consumption, even though the absolute dollar amount remains modest. Demographic characteristics shape this economic picture. Only 9.1 % of the adult population holds a bachelor’s degree or higher, and the median age of 42.7 years points to a mature workforce. The unemployment rate of 7.2 % exceeds the national average, suggesting limited job mobility and a labor market that leans toward lower‑wage occupations. These factors reinforce the modest income levels and explain why goods—often tied to wages—remain the dominant cost driver. A less educated, older labor pool typically commands lower salaries, which in turn depresss local price pressures, especially for services that are labor‑intensive. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped from 89.78 in 2010 to 89.10 in 2023, a decline of 0.8 %, indicating that consumer prices have barely moved relative to the nation. Median household income, however, surged by 35.9 % from $25,876 to $35,160, outpacing the 16.7 % rise in median rent ($568 to $663). Real wages, therefore, have improved substantially, allowing residents to absorb the modest increase in housing costs while still benefiting from a stable price environment. The gap between income growth and price stability underscores an expanding purchasing power for the median household. Prospective residents should anchor their budgeting on three pillars: a modest rent of $663, a goods expenditure that will likely consume the largest slice of the budget, and very low service costs. Assuming a median income of $35,160, a household can expect to allocate roughly 30 % of earnings to rent, another 30‑35 % to groceries, fuel, and related goods, and the remaining 35‑40 % to utilities, healthcare, and discretionary spending. The low services index provides a cushion for healthcare and utility bills, while the higher goods index cautions against underestimating food and transportation costs. Understanding this allocation framework equips newcomers to plan a realistic household budget that reflects the county’s unique blend of inexpensive services and moderately priced essentials.

What this means for residents of Lowndes County, Alabama

Because the Housing Rent index is 89.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
