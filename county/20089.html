📊 Local Market Analysis

Jewell County presents a cost of living profile that sits 10.1 percent below the national benchmark, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 89.9 in 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals a distorted consumer landscape where the price of tangible necessities barely budges from the U.S. average. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — carry an index of 96.5, meaning residents pay nearly full metropolitan freight for daily consumables despite the county’s rural isolation. The primary deflationary force stems from services, which clock in at a striking 68.6, driven by below-average healthcare, utility, and dining-out costs, while rental housing sits modestly discounted at 90.8. This structure creates a paradox: the baseline survival budget for food and fuel remains stubbornly high relative to income, while the "optional" service economy is deeply discounted. The median household income of $52,344 trails the national median of $65,047 by nearly 20 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of $58,219.5 indicates that local wages stretch significantly further once the 89.9 RPP is applied, effectively granting residents a 11.2 percent real-income premium over their nominal paychecks. Housing affordability underscores this dynamic; median monthly rent of $591 consumes only 13.5 percent of gross income, a fraction of the 30-percent burden threshold and roughly half the national rent-to-income ratio implied by the $928 U.S. median rent. However, the low service-cost environment likely reflects limited local supply — fewer specialists, limited dining, sparse public transit — meaning the savings are partly involuntary, representing consumption foregone rather than efficiency gained. Demographic and labor metrics sharpen the picture of a shrinking, aging workforce where labor scarcity has not translated into broad-based wage growth. An unemployment rate of 0.4 percent signals an exceptionally tight market, yet only 14.6 percent of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, and a median age of 50.3 years suggests a population exiting peak earning years. This composition suppresses aggregate income growth while the tight labor market supports the modest nominal wage gains observed over the last decade. The county’s economic engine appears reliant on a shrinking pool of experienced workers in sectors that do not require advanced credentials, capping household earning potential even as the cost of goods remains tethered to national logistics networks. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP declined 3.3 percent from 92.99 to 89.91, indicating that Jewell County became incrementally cheaper relative to the nation, while median income surged 29.1 percent against a mere 14.5 percent rise in median rent. This divergence confirms that local purchasing power has strengthened materially; the median earner today commands significantly more real housing capacity than in 2010. For a prospective resident, the budgeting reality is clear: shelter is remarkably cheap, services are inexpensive but potentially inaccessible, and the grocery bill will look nearly identical to one in a major city. The financial friction point is not rent, but the 96.5 goods index — every tank of fuel and cart of groceries absorbs a disproportionate share of a paycheck that remains 20 percent below the national nominal median.

What this means for residents of Jewell County, Kansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 58219.5, a median household income of $52,344 in Jewell County, Kansas has the equivalent buying power of $30,474,415 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 90.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
