📊 Local Market Analysis

Colbert County’s cost of living sits well below the national norm, with the overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) index at 89.1 compared with the United States benchmark of 100. Prices for goods—groceries, apparel, and fuel—register at 94.6, still under the national average, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are markedly cheaper at 61.6. Rental costs follow the same pattern, with a housing index of 89.9 and a median monthly rent of $838, roughly 10 % lower than the national median of $928. The dominant driver of the county’s price structure is the services sector, whose index is the lowest of the three components. Because essential services cost roughly 38 % less than the national average, everyday expenditures on medical care, electricity, and restaurant meals are substantially restrained. Goods remain modestly below national levels, and housing, while not as cheap as services, still offers a rent burden of only 17.7 % of median household income—well under the often‑cited 30 % affordability threshold. Consequently, daily life in Colbert County can be characterized as inexpensive, particularly for households that rely heavily on local services and modest housing costs. Median household income in the county stands at $56,736, 13 % below the national median of $65,047. After subtracting the median rent of $838, a typical household retains roughly $48,900 in annual discretionary income, equating to a purchasing‑power index of 63,679. This figure indicates that, despite lower earnings, residents enjoy roughly 63 % of the purchasing capacity of an average American household when local price levels are accounted for. The modest rent burden and low service costs amplify the effective buying power of the median worker, allowing more of the income to stretch toward non‑housing consumption. Demographically, the county’s labor market is anchored by a relatively low unemployment rate of 3.3 % and a median age of 42.3 years, suggesting a stable, mature workforce. However, only 12.9 % of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, a factor that may limit access to higher‑paying occupations and keep median earnings below national levels. The combination of low unemployment and modest educational attainment yields a labor environment where wages are steady but not rapidly advancing. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped marginally from 89.78 to 89.10, a 0.8 % decline, while median income rose 39.8 % from $40,576 to $56,736. Rental prices increased 32.6 % in the same period, from $632 to $838. Income growth outpaced both overall price inflation and rent escalation, enhancing real purchasing power for residents. Prospective movers should budget around $838 for monthly rent, allocate a larger share of income to goods and discretionary spending given the low service costs, and anticipate that a sizable portion of earnings will remain after housing costs. Understanding the interplay of modest service prices, affordable rents, and a stable but modest wage base is essential for realistic household budgeting in Colbert County.
