📊 Local Market Analysis

Johnson County’s cost of living sits well below the national average, with the overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) index at 88.8 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices are the closest to the benchmark at 96.6, while services are markedly cheaper at 66.0, and rental costs trail the national level at 87.3. The gap between local and national price levels translates into a tangible advantage for residents: every dollar spent on groceries, apparel, or fuel buys roughly the same amount as in the rest of the country, but healthcare, utilities, and dining out cost about one‑third less than the national norm. Housing, measured by median monthly rent of $1,104, is 19 % higher than the national median rent of $928, yet the rent‑burden metric—17.7 % of household income—remains comfortably low, reflecting the county’s relatively high earnings. The cost structure is driven primarily by goods, which sit at 96.6 of the national index, while services and rental costs pull the overall figure down. Daily life feels inexpensive in the service sector: a resident can expect utility bills and medical expenses to be roughly a third of what peers pay elsewhere. However, the higher rent offset by a modest premium is mitigated by the county’s robust median household income of $74,721, 14.7 % above the national median of $65,047. After deducting the median rent, a typical household retains $63,617 annually, or $5,301 per month, before taxes and other obligations. The Purchasing Power Index of 84,174.7 quantifies this advantage: residents enjoy roughly 16 % more buying power than the average American, a figure that incorporates both higher earnings and lower service‑sector prices. Demographically, the county’s labor market reinforces the purchasing power narrative. An unemployment rate of 3.7 % signals a tight labor market, while 30.6 % of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, indicating a relatively skilled workforce that can command wages above the national average. The median age of 30.8 years suggests a youthful population, often associated with higher mobility and spending on discretionary items such as dining and entertainment—categories that are cheaper locally. This combination of low unemployment, higher educational attainment, and a young demographic creates a consumer environment where disposable income is more readily available for non‑essential purchases. Over the past decade, the RPP fell from 91.139 in 2010 to 88.769 in 2023, a 2.6 % decline, indicating that consumer prices have softened relative to the nation. In the same period, median household income rose from $55,700 to $74,721, a 34.1 % increase, outpacing the 26.9 % rise in median rent (from $870 to $1,104). The divergence between income growth and price inflation suggests that wages have more than kept pace with living costs, expanding real purchasing power for residents. Prospective residents should budget with the lower service costs and higher rent in mind. Allocating roughly 18 % of gross income to rent aligns with the county’s rent‑burden average, leaving about 82 % for utilities, healthcare, food, transportation, and discretionary spending. Given the 66.0 services index, utilities and medical expenses will consume a smaller slice of the budget than in most states, allowing more flexibility for groceries, apparel, and fuel, which sit near parity with national prices. A realistic monthly budget might allocate $300–$350 to utilities and healthcare, $400–$500 to groceries, $150–$200 to transportation fuel, and the remainder to discretionary categories, all supported by a median disposable income that comfortably exceeds national benchmarks.

What this means for residents of Johnson County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 84174.7, a median household income of $74,721 in Johnson County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $62,896,178 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
