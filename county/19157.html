📊 Local Market Analysis

The cost of living in Poweshiek County sits well below the national benchmark, with an overall RPP of 88.8 against the United States index of 100, indicating that a typical consumer basket costs roughly 11 percent less than the average American household. Prices for goods—groceries, apparel, and fuel—are the most elevated component at 96.6, only 3.4 points shy of the national level, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are markedly cheaper at 66.0, a full 34 points below the national average. Rental costs follow the same pattern, registering at 87.3, which translates to a 12.7‑point discount relative to the countrywide figure. The median monthly rent of $864 further underscores this affordability, falling $64 short of the national median rent of $928. The composition of the local cost structure reveals that goods drive the expense profile, accounting for the highest index among the three categories. Despite the relatively modest price advantage in services and housing, the near‑parity of goods prices means that everyday necessities such as food and fuel consume a larger share of household budgets than in many other regions. Consequently, daily life in Poweshiek County can be described as moderately inexpensive, primarily because the savings realized in services and rent outweigh the higher cost of goods. For a household earning the median income of $64,837, rent represents 16 percent of earnings, well under the conventional 30 percent affordability threshold, leaving ample margin for discretionary spending. Purchasing power in the county is quantified by an index of 73,040.1, which reflects the dollar value of goods and services that a median earner can afford after accounting for local price levels. When the median household income of $64,837 is adjusted by the overall RPP of 88.8, the effective purchasing power equates to roughly $57,600 in national‑price terms, indicating that residents retain a substantial portion of their earnings for non‑housing consumption. This residual income is further bolstered by the low rent burden and the inexpensive service sector, allowing households to allocate a larger share of their budget to savings, education, or leisure activities. Demographic factors shape the cost‑of‑living experience in distinct ways. With only 16.6 percent of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, the labor market is skewed toward lower‑skill occupations, which typically command lower wages but also align with the region’s lower price environment. The median age of 40.8 years suggests a mature workforce, likely with established household expenses and fewer dependents, while the unemployment rate of 4.9 percent sits modestly above the national average, hinting at a relatively stable, though not booming, job market. These characteristics combine to produce a modest income ceiling that is nevertheless sufficient to cover the county’s lower living costs without imposing severe financial strain. Over the past decade, the RPP has slipped from 91.139 in 2010 to 88.769 in 2023, a 2.6 percent decline that signals a gradual erosion of price pressures. In the same period, median household income rose from $51,006 to $64,837, a robust 27.1 percent increase, outpacing the 2.6 percent drop in the cost index. However, median rent surged from $640 to $864, a 35 percent jump that exceeds both income growth and overall price deflation, indicating that housing costs have become the most volatile component. While wages have generally kept pace with the broader decline in consumer prices, the disproportionate rise in rent suggests that housing affordability could become a tighter constraint for future residents. Prospective residents should structure their budgets around three core pillars: housing, goods, and services. With rent at $864 per month, allocating roughly 15‑16 percent of a median income to housing is realistic, leaving about $4,300 per month for all other expenses. Given the near‑national price level for groceries and fuel, a family of four can expect to spend approximately $600–$700 monthly on food, while utilities and healthcare will be considerably cheaper than the national average, often under $200 combined. Transportation costs will align closely with national figures due to the goods index, so budgeting for fuel and vehicle maintenance at around $150–$200 per month is prudent. The remaining discretionary income—approximately $2,500 after essential outlays—provides flexibility for education, recreation, or savings. By anchoring the household budget to these calibrated figures, residents can navigate Poweshiek County’s cost landscape with confidence, leveraging the region’s low service and housing costs while accounting for the relatively higher price of everyday goods.

What this means for residents of Poweshiek County, Iowa

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
