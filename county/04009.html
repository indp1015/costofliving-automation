📊 Local Market Analysis

Graham County’s cost of living sits marginally above the national baseline, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 100.7 versus 100 for the United States. Goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel are priced 2.1 % lower than the national average (97.9), while services—including healthcare, utilities, and dining—run 8.6 % higher (108.6). Rental costs are the most pronounced deviation, trailing the national level by 16.7 % (83.3). The median household income of $67,326 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 3.5 %, but the income advantage is partially offset by the elevated service prices and modest rent savings. The expense structure is dominated by services, which lift the overall cost profile despite inexpensive housing. Residents spend a larger share of their budget on health‑care, utility bills, and restaurant meals than the average American, while everyday necessities like food and fuel remain comparatively affordable. The low rent index translates into a median monthly rent of $890, well below the national median of $928, and represents only 15.9 % of household income—a rent burden comfortably under the 30 % threshold commonly used to flag affordability concerns. Consequently, daily life feels cheaper in terms of shelter, but the higher service costs erode that advantage, producing a mixed affordability picture. Purchasing power, measured by the index of $66,830.1, indicates that a typical Graham County household retains roughly 66.8 % of its income for discretionary spending after covering the local price level. Subtracting the median rent ($890 × 12 = $10,680) from the median annual income leaves $56,646, or $4,720 per month for all other expenses. After accounting for the service price premium (approximately 8.6 % above national levels), the residual discretionary income narrows further, suggesting that while housing costs are benign, residents must allocate a larger slice of their budget to health, utilities, and dining out than peers elsewhere. Demographically, the county’s labor market reflects modest educational attainment (9.7 % with a bachelor’s degree or higher) and a relatively young median age of 34.4 years. The unemployment rate of 6.3 % exceeds the national average, implying that a portion of the workforce may face income volatility, which compounds the impact of higher service costs. The limited pool of highly educated workers can restrain wage growth, reinforcing the reliance on lower‑skill, lower‑pay occupations that are more sensitive to price fluctuations in essential services. Over the past decade, the RPP rose from 100.558 in 2010 to 100.742 in 2023—a modest 0.2 % increase—while median household income surged 46.5 % from $45,964 to $67,326. Rental rates climbed 22.1 % in the same period, from $729 to $890. Income growth outpaced both overall price inflation and rent escalation, enhancing real purchasing power. However, the service component’s steady premium has limited the full benefit of wage gains, especially for households near the median income bracket. Prospective residents should budget for a rent bill that consumes roughly one‑sixth of their earnings, but plan for service expenses that are nearly 9 % above national norms. A realistic monthly allocation might include $900 for rent, $300–$350 for utilities and health‑care, $400 for groceries and fuel, and $250–$300 for dining and personal services. The remaining income will cover transportation, savings, and discretionary spending. Understanding this composition helps new arrivals gauge the true cost of everyday life in Graham County, where affordable housing is tempered by comparatively pricey services.

What this means for residents of Graham County, Arizona

With a Purchasing Power Index of 66830.1, a median household income of $67,326 in Graham County, Arizona has the equivalent buying power of $44,994,033 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 83.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
