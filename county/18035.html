📊 Local Market Analysis

Delaware County’s cost of living sits comfortably below the national norm, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 92.1 versus 100 for the United States. Prices for everyday goods—groceries, apparel, and fuel—register at 95.6, still under the national benchmark, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are markedly cheaper at 71.3. Housing costs, measured by median monthly rent of $874, are also modest; the rental‑cost component of the RPP stands at 84.7, indicating that renters in the county pay roughly 15 % less than the average American household. By contrast, the national median rent sits at $928, confirming that Delaware County’s rental market is 5.8 % cheaper than the countrywide average. The cost structure is driven primarily by the services sector, which is the lowest‑priced component at 71.3, pulling the overall index down. Goods remain the second‑largest contributor, hovering just under parity with the nation, while rental costs occupy the middle ground. Daily life therefore leans toward affordability, especially for expenditures tied to health, utilities, and dining out. A household spending $1,000 on services nationally would need only $713 in Delaware County, freeing cash for discretionary items or savings. However, the modest advantage in goods pricing means that grocery bills and fuel expenses remain comparable to other regions, tempering the overall cheapness of everyday living. Disposable income calculations reveal that the median household, earning $56,932 annually, retains a purchasing power index of 61,817.4. After accounting for the median rent of $874 per month—equating to $10,488 annually and representing 18.4 % of income—approximately $46,444 remains for all other expenses. When adjusted for the county’s lower price levels, this residual income translates into a real purchasing power roughly 7 % below the national median income, reflecting the combined effect of slightly lower wages and the modest cost advantage. The purchasing power index thus underscores that while housing is affordable, the overall budgetary cushion is tighter than in higher‑income locales. Demographically, the county’s labor market shapes the cost experience. With a 6.2 % unemployment rate, slightly above the national average, job seekers may face limited wage growth, reinforcing the modest disposable income. Only 15 % of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting a workforce concentrated in lower‑skill, lower‑pay occupations, which aligns with the median income figure of $56,932—13.5 % below the national median of $65,047. The median age of 35.7 years points to a relatively young population, likely early in their earning trajectories, further constraining household budgets. These demographic factors combine to produce a cost of living environment where affordability is driven more by low service and rental prices than by high earnings. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped from 93.022 in 2010 to 92.097 in 2023, a 1 % decline, indicating that consumer prices have softened relative to the nation. Median household income, however, surged 46.6 % from $38,830 to $56,932, outpacing the 26.5 % rise in median rent (from $691 to $874). The disparity between income growth and price growth suggests that wages have generally kept pace with, and even exceeded, the modest inflation in the county, enhancing real purchasing power for most residents. Prospective residents should budget with rent consuming roughly one‑fifth of gross earnings, leaving $3,874 per month for food, transportation, utilities, and discretionary spending. Given the low service cost index, allocating $300–$350 for utilities and $400–$500 for healthcare and dining is realistic, while grocery and fuel expenses will approximate national averages, requiring careful planning. A prudent household budget would reserve about 30 % of post‑rent income for essential goods, 20 % for services, and the remaining 50 % for savings, debt repayment, or variable costs, ensuring financial stability in a region where affordability stems from low service and housing prices rather than high wages.

What this means for residents of Delaware County, Indiana

With a Purchasing Power Index of 61817.4, a median household income of $56,932 in Delaware County, Indiana has the equivalent buying power of $35,193,882 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 84.7 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
