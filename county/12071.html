📊 Local Market Analysis

Lee County residents face a cost of living that sits 3.6 percent above the national baseline, yet the aggregate figure masks a severe structural distortion driven almost entirely by service-sector inflation. The Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity for 2023 registers an overall index of 103.6, but the Services component—encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining—surges to 123.2, making it the single most expensive category relative to the country. Conversely, Goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel come in below average at 98.2, while Rental Costs surprisingly register at 93.7, a figure that contradicts the typical Florida premium narrative but aligns with a market where median monthly rent of $1,597 consumes 26.2 percent of the $73,099 median household income. This composition indicates that daily life is expensive not because of shelter or staples, but because the recurring costs of maintaining a household—electricity in a cooling-intensive climate, medical care for an older population, and hospitality-sector pricing—carry a heavy premium. The demographic profile sharpens the impact of that service-heavy cost structure. With a median age of 49.3 years and only 18.8 percent of adults holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, the workforce skews toward retirement-age service workers and lower-wage hospitality roles rather than high-earning knowledge professionals. An unemployment rate of 4.2 percent suggests a tight labor market, yet the educational attainment gap limits wage growth potential for the median earner. The Purchasing Power Index of 70,534.4 quantifies this squeeze: although local nominal income exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 12.4 percent, the real value of those dollars erodes once the 123.2 service index is applied. Residents effectively operate with purchasing power that struggles to stretch far beyond the national average despite the higher headline paycheck, a dynamic that forces trade-offs on discretionary spending and savings rates. Over the past decade, the erosion has accelerated. The RPP climbed from 100.392 in 2010 to 103.636 in 2023, a cumulative 3.2 percent increase in the price level, but nominal median income rose 50.6 percent—from $48,537 to $73,099—while median rent jumped 67.9 percent, from $951 to $1,597. Rent growth has outpaced income growth by a 17.3 percentage-point margin, pushing the rent-to-income ratio higher even as the BEA rental index remains below 100. This divergence suggests that while regional rents may appear cheap relative to major metros, the local income base has not expanded quickly enough to absorb the velocity of housing cost appreciation, compressing disposable income for renters specifically. For a prospective resident, the budgeting reality centers on the service bill. A household earning the median income allocates roughly $1,597 to rent, leaving approximately $4,495 in monthly gross remainder. However, the 23.2 percent premium on services implies that utilities, health insurance premiums, and routine dining or personal care will consume a disproportionate share of that remainder compared to a median U.S. city. Groceries and fuel offer modest relief at 1.8 percent below average, but they lack the weight to offset the service overhang. The critical variable is not the lease payment but the monthly outflow for electricity during summer months, healthcare utilization consistent with an older population, and the service-tip economy that dominates local commerce. Anyone relocating should model a budget where fixed service costs run 20-to-25 percent above their previous locale, because that is where the cost of living actually lives.

What this means for residents of Lee County, Florida

With a Purchasing Power Index of 70534.4, a median household income of $73,099 in Lee County, Florida has the equivalent buying power of $51,559,941 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 93.7 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
