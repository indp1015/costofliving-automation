📊 Local Market Analysis

Crawford County presents a cost of living profile that sits 11.2 percentage points below the national benchmark, registering an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 88.8 in 2023. This aggregate figure masks a stark bifurcation in consumer pricing: goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel — track near parity at 96.6, while services, including healthcare, utilities, and dining, plunge to a deeply discounted 66.0. Rental costs occupy a middle ground at 87.3. Consequently, the local cost structure is driven not by housing or daily necessities, which remain nationally competitive, but by the exceptional affordability of service-sector consumption. For a household, this translates to a daily experience where filling a grocery cart or a gas tank feels familiar, but a hospital visit, a utility bill, or a restaurant meal carries a significantly lower nominal price tag than the national average. The purchasing power calculus reinforces this advantage. With a median household income of $65,039 — virtually identical to the national median of $65,047 — residents retain substantially more real income after local price adjustments. The Purchasing Power Index of 73,267.7 indicates that the median dollar stretches roughly 12.6 percent further here than in the typical U.S. county. Housing cost pressure is minimal; median monthly rent of $727 consumes only 13.4 percent of gross income, a burden roughly half the 30-percent affordability threshold and well below the national median rent of $928. This low shelter overhead, combined with cheap services, creates a wide margin for discretionary spending or savings accumulation that is rare in jurisdictions with comparable income levels. Demographic and labor market conditions underpin this dynamic without signaling overheating. An unemployment rate of 1.7 percent reflects an exceptionally tight labor market, yet the modest educational attainment — only 14.3 percent of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher — suggests the local economy relies on sectors that do not command the wage premiums seen in knowledge hubs. The median age of 37.9 years aligns closely with the national figure, indicating a stable, working-age population rather than a transient or retirement-driven demographic. This composition supports the current equilibrium: employers compete for labor in a low-unemployment environment, but the absence of high-wage professional clusters prevents income inflation from eroding the county’s cost advantage. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP declined 2.6 percent from 91.1 to 88.8, even as nominal median income surged 35.3 percent from $48,084 to $65,039 and median rent rose 34.4 percent from $541 to $727. Wage growth has not merely kept pace with shelter costs; it has outstripped the broader consumer price trajectory, expanding real purchasing power considerably. A prospective resident budgeting for relocation should anticipate near-national prices for tangible goods — food, clothing, gasoline — but should plan for significantly lower outlays on healthcare premiums, utility deposits, and recurring service fees. The primary budgeting risk lies not in escalating local costs, but in the potential volatility of goods prices, which remain tethered to national supply chains and offer no local discount buffer.

What this means for residents of Crawford County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 73267.7, a median household income of $65,039 in Crawford County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $47,652,579 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
