📊 Local Market Analysis

Washington County’s cost of living is modestly above the national benchmark, with an overall RPP of 103.6, indicating that the average basket of goods and services costs 3.6 % more than the U.S. average of 100. The goods component, at 98.2, runs slightly below the national index, while services are the primary driver of the elevated total at 123.2, and housing (rental costs) remains cheaper than the national median at 93.7. This pattern shows that consumer prices for everyday items are generally comparable, but expenses for services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are markedly higher than the national norm. Services dominate the local cost structure, making daily life relatively expensive despite a lower housing index. The 123.2 reading for services suggests that a household must allocate a larger share of its budget to medical care, utility bills, and restaurant meals than it would nationally. In contrast, goods—including groceries, apparel, and fuel—are priced about 2 % below the national average, offering some relief for routine purchases. Rental costs, while still a major expense, are priced roughly 6 % lower than the national median, which helps offset the higher service costs for many residents. The median household income of $52,723 provides a baseline for assessing purchasing power. Rent consumes 20.1 % of this income, leaving roughly $42,000 for other expenditures before taxes and discretionary spending. The Purchasing Power Index of 50,873.2 reflects the relative ability of local residents to afford consumption after accounting for the elevated service prices; the figure is lower than the national average, underscoring that the higher service costs reduce overall buying capacity despite a solid income level. The modest unemployment rate of 4.0 % suggests a relatively stable labor market, but the low educational attainment—only 7.1 % of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher—may limit opportunities for higher‑earning jobs, influencing the income distribution. Over the past decade, consumer costs have risen modestly while incomes have accelerated. The RPP increased from 100.392 in 2010 to 103.636 in 2023, a 3.2 % rise, whereas median household income grew by 35.3 % to $52,723. Median rent climbed 37.9 % to $884, outpacing the overall cost‑of‑living increase. This divergence indicates that wages have generally kept pace with, and even exceeded, the growth in housing expenses, though service inflation remains a pressure point. The combination of higher service prices and relatively stable unemployment suggests that residents must budget carefully for recurring service expenses. Prospective residents should anticipate that while rent is affordable compared with national averages, the high service index will dominate monthly outlays. A household earning near the median should allocate roughly one‑fifth of its income to housing, then plan for additional service‑related costs that could easily exceed 30 % of the budget. Goods purchases remain comparatively inexpensive, providing some margin for discretionary spending. Understanding these dynamics enables realistic budgeting that accounts for the local cost structure without overextending household finances.

Analysis prepared by the Data in DP Research Team. Based on BEA RPP and Census ACS data.

What this means for residents of Washington County, Florida

With a Purchasing Power Index of 50873.2, a median household income of $52,723 in Washington County, Florida has the equivalent buying power of $26,821,877 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 93.7 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
