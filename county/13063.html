📊 Local Market Analysis

Clayton County’s cost of living sits modestly below the national average, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 96.5 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices—groceries, apparel, and fuel—are the most elevated component at 97.7, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are markedly cheaper at 88.3. Housing, measured by median monthly rent of $1,307, registers an index of 92.3, indicating that renters pay roughly 8 percent less than the national benchmark of $928. The composite picture is one of a region where everyday necessities cost slightly less than elsewhere, but the rental market remains a relative drag on household budgets. The consumption structure is dominated by housing costs, which account for the largest share of outlays despite the lower housing index. At $1,307 per month, rent consumes 26.8 percent of the median household’s $58,507 annual income, leaving 73.2 percent for all other expenses. Goods, while priced near the national level, still represent a sizable portion of discretionary spending, whereas services are substantially cheaper, cushioning the overall cost burden. For a typical household, the combination of affordable services and slightly higher goods prices yields a mixed affordability profile: daily life is not inexpensive, but it is not prohibitive either, largely because the rent‑to‑income ratio remains within the commonly cited affordability threshold of 30 percent. Purchasing power in Clayton County is quantified by an index of 60,632.8, a figure that translates to roughly 92 percent of the national median disposable income after accounting for local price levels. After deducting the median rent, a household retains about $46,200 in annual income for food, transportation, healthcare, and leisure. This residual amount is modestly lower than the national disposable average, reflecting the combined effect of a median income that lags the national $65,047 by 10 percent and a rent level that exceeds the national median by 40 percent. The net result is a purchasing power that, while respectable, leaves less room for discretionary spending compared with many other metros. Demographically, the county’s labor market shapes the cost experience. With an unemployment rate of 6.7 percent—above the national average—the pool of earners is constrained, limiting upward pressure on wages. Only 14.1 percent of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting a workforce concentrated in lower‑skill occupations that typically command lower pay. The median age of 33.3 years points to a relatively young population, often early in their career trajectories and more sensitive to rent burdens. These factors together explain why the rent‑to‑income ratio hovers near the affordability ceiling and why services remain cheap: a younger, less‑educated labor force exerts limited demand for premium services, keeping prices subdued. Over the past decade, the RPP rose from 94.574 in 2010 to 96.494 in 2023, a modest 2.0 percent increase. In the same period, median household income surged 42.9 percent, climbing from $40,938 to $58,507, while median rent jumped 48.4 percent, from $881 to $1,307. Income growth outpaced the overall price index but fell short of keeping pace with rental inflation, which grew slightly faster than both. Consequently, the rent burden edged upward, nudging the affordability balance toward tighter margins for renters despite broader price stability. Prospective residents should anchor their budgeting around three core figures: $1,307 for monthly rent, roughly $400–$500 for combined groceries and fuel (reflecting the 97.7 goods index), and $250–$300 for utilities, healthcare, and dining, given the 88.3 services index. Allocating no more than 27 percent of income to rent preserves a buffer for the remaining 73 percent, which must cover transportation, childcare, and savings. With a median income of $58,507, a realistic monthly disposable pool hovers around $3,850 after rent, enough to meet basic needs but leaving limited flexibility for high‑cost leisure or extensive savings. Understanding these constraints enables newcomers to gauge whether Clayton County’s modest overall price level aligns with their personal financial thresholds.

What this means for residents of Clayton County, Georgia

Because the Housing Rent index is 92.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
