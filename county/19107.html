📊 Local Market Analysis

Keokuk County presents a cost of living profile that sits 11.2 percentage points below the national benchmark, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 88.8 for 2023. This aggregate figure masks significant internal divergence: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel trade at 96.6, nearly converging with national pricing, while services—encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining—plummet to 66.0, marking the county’s most profound discount. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 87.3, suggesting that while shelter is affordable relative to the country, the daily basket of consumable goods exerts the strongest upward pressure on local budgets, preventing the overall index from falling further. The consumption breakdown reveals that daily life is inexpensive primarily because service intensity is cheap, not because the weekly grocery run or fuel stop offers a bargain. With goods commanding the highest component index at 96.6, residents experience near-national price levels for the most frequent, non-discretionary purchases. This dynamic compresses the practical savings a household realizes; a median monthly rent of $822 consumes only 16.2 percent of the $60,856 median household income, a remarkably low shelter burden that frees cash flow for the goods basket where prices are sticky. The resulting Purchasing Power Index of 68,555.5 indicates that after adjusting for local prices, the median household commands real resources roughly 5.4 percent above the nominal national median income of $65,047, a tangible standard-of-living premium derived almost entirely from the services and housing discounts. Local demographics reinforce this economic structure. An unemployment rate of 3.6 percent signals a tight labor market, yet educational attainment remains modest at 11.4 percent holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, and a median age of 42.8 years suggests a settled, experienced workforce rather than a transient student population. This composition likely sustains the low service costs—healthcare and personal care pricing often correlates with local wage floors for service-sector labor—while the goods index reflects the inescapable logistics of national supply chains serving a rural distribution network. The labor market appears stable enough to support income growth without igniting broad-based inflation in the non-tradable sectors. Over the past decade, the RPP has drifted downward from 91.1 to 88.8, a 2.6 percent decline in relative price levels, even as nominal median income surged 38.8 percent from $43,838 to $60,856. Rent increased 42.7 percent over the same span, outpacing income growth slightly, yet the starting base was so low that the rent-to-income ratio remains negligible by national standards. Wages have not merely kept pace; they have expanded real purchasing power significantly because the cost curve for the heaviest-weighted components—services and housing—has bent downward relative to the nation. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that mirror urban prices, but expect healthcare premiums, utility bills, and restaurant meals to carry a distinct small-town discount, creating a budget environment where discretionary income survives the necessities.

What this means for residents of Keokuk County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 68555.5, a median household income of $60,856 in Keokuk County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $41,720,135 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
