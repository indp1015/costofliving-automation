📊 Local Market Analysis

Lewis County’s cost‑of‑living index sits at 92.2, meaning the average consumer price level is 7.8 % below the national benchmark of 100. Goods prices are the most elevated component at 95.9, still modestly under the national average but noticeably higher than the county’s overall index. Services—healthcare, utilities, and dining—register at 86.9, a full 13.1 % cheaper than the nation, while rental costs are the cheapest element at 68.1, indicating that housing expenses are 31.9 % lower than the national norm. This mix creates a price environment where everyday necessities such as groceries and apparel cost slightly more than the national average, yet the bulk of household outlays—particularly shelter and utility bills—are markedly cheaper. The dominant driver of the local cost structure is housing, despite its low price level. Rental costs at $708 per month are 23.7 % below the national median rent of $928, and the rent‑burden ratio of 17.1 % of median household income signals that housing consumes a relatively small slice of earnings. Goods, however, pull the overall index upward; at 95.9, they are the only category approaching parity with national prices, suggesting that day‑to‑day purchases of food, clothing, and fuel are not dramatically cheaper. Services, at 86.9, provide a cushion that offsets the higher goods cost, making daily life overall inexpensive for residents who can secure affordable housing. The combination of low rent and modest service costs yields a net cost environment that is cheaper than the United States average, even though the price of consumables is near‑national. Median household income in Lewis County stands at $49,643, 24.2 % below the national median of $65,047. After subtracting the median rent of $708 (8.5 % of income), a typical household retains roughly $44,935 for all other expenses. The Purchasing Power Index of 53,821.7 quantifies this residual buying capacity, indicating that, after accounting for local price levels, residents have about 54 % of the purchasing power of an average American household facing national prices. In practical terms, a family can stretch its income farther on groceries, utilities, and discretionary spending than a counterpart in a higher‑cost region, though the lower income base tempers the advantage. Demographically, the county’s median age of 48.9 years and a bachelor's‑degree attainment rate of 14.5 % shape consumption patterns. An older population typically spends a larger share on healthcare and less on education or child‑related goods, aligning with the low services index. The unemployment rate of 3.5 % suggests a tight labor market, which can support wage stability but also reflects limited job diversity, potentially constraining income growth for younger workers. The modest educational attainment may limit access to higher‑paying occupations, reinforcing the importance of affordable housing in maintaining disposable income levels. Over the past decade, the Regional Price Parities (RPP) index slipped from 93.2 to 92.2, a 1.0 % decline, indicating that consumer prices have softened slightly. Median household income, however, surged 36.0 % from $36,505 to $49,643, outpacing the modest price decline. Rental costs rose 18.2 % from $599 to $708, a slower pace than income growth but faster than the overall price index. The disparity between income growth and price stability suggests that wages have more than kept pace with expenses, enhancing real purchasing power for residents. Prospective residents should budget around $708 for monthly rent, leaving roughly $4,140 per month from the median income for all other costs. With goods prices near national levels, allocating 30–35 % of remaining income to groceries, fuel, and apparel is realistic, while utilities and healthcare can be covered with another 15–20 % given the low services index. The low rent‑burden ratio leaves room for savings or discretionary spending, but the lower median income relative to national figures means that households should monitor non‑housing expenses closely, especially if they have higher-than‑average consumption of goods. Understanding that housing is cheap while everyday consumables are only modestly cheaper equips newcomers to plan a balanced budget that leverages the county’s affordability without overestimating disposable income.

What this means for residents of Lewis County, Idaho

Because the Housing Rent index is 68.1 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
