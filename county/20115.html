📊 Local Market Analysis

Marion County residents operate on a consumer price level roughly ten percent below the national baseline, with the BEA's 2023 Regional Price Parability index registering 89.9 against a national value of 100. That single figure compresses an entire cost structure: the same basket of goods and services that costs $100 elsewhere in the United States costs $89.90 here. The gap is not uniform across categories, and the composition matters more than the headline number for anyone constructing a household budget. Services form the county's decisive cost advantage. At 68.6, the services component — healthcare, utilities, dining away from home, and personal services — sits 31.4 percent below the national average and is the lowest of the three measured categories. Goods, covering groceries, apparel, and fuel, run closest to national norms at 96.5, meaning shelf prices at the grocery store and the pump offer almost no discount. Rental costs index at 90.8, and the underlying dollar figure confirms it: a median monthly rent of $727 compares with $928 nationally, a 21.7 percent shortfall. Daily life in Marion County is inexpensive primarily because of what residents pay for services and shelter, not because of what they pay at retail counters. A household relocating from a metro area would notice little relief in the grocery aisle and substantial relief in utility bills, medical visits, and rent. That price advantage translates into measurable purchasing power. The purchasing power index of 70,558.8 expresses what the county's median household income of $63,438 would be worth at national price levels — a real-income premium of roughly $7,100. The median income itself trails the national median of $65,047 by 2.5 percent, yet the price structure more than offsets that shortfall. Housing consumes the largest single fixed obligation: $727 monthly, or $8,724 annually, absorbs 13.8 percent of median household income and leaves approximately $54,714, or $4,559 per month, for food, transportation, healthcare, insurance, and discretionary spending. A rent burden in the low teens sits well below the 30 percent threshold conventionally used to flag housing stress. Labor market conditions reinforce that cushion. Unemployment at 3.0 percent indicates a tight local labor market, which historically supports wage bargaining and reduces the incidence of involuntary income loss. Educational attainment is more modest — 19.4 percent hold a bachelor's degree or higher — consistent with an economy weighted toward agriculture, healthcare support, trade, and local services rather than high-wage professional sectors, and consistent with the low services price index. The median age of 43.7 points to an established, settled population with predictable consumption patterns: less spending on child-rearing and entry-level household formation, more on healthcare and vehicle maintenance, both categories where local prices are comparatively favorable. The decade-long trend shows expenses falling relative to the nation while incomes rose. The RPP declined from 92.991 in 2010 to 89.908 in 2023, a 3.3 percent reduction, meaning local prices grew more slowly than national prices. Median household income rose from $45,927 to $63,438, a 38.1 percent gain, while median rent climbed from $561 to $727, a 29.6 percent increase. Income growth outpaced rental growth by 8.5 percentage points, and the rent-to-income ratio compressed from roughly 14.7 percent to 13.8 percent over the period. What this means for residents of Marion County, Kansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 70558.8, a median household income of $63,438 in Marion County, Kansas has the equivalent buying power of $44,761,092 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 90.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
