📊 Local Market Analysis

Eagle County’s cost of living sits just above the national baseline, with the overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) index at 101.9 versus 100 for the United States. Goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel are priced marginally below the national average at 99.2, while services—including healthcare, utilities, and dining—are markedly higher at 130.5. Rental costs are the most affordable component, reflected in a housing index of 86.8, which translates to a median monthly rent of $2,032—still more than double the national median rent of $928 but proportionally lower than the national cost‑of‑housing weight in the RPP calculation. The disparity between the low housing index and the high services index indicates that everyday expenditures for health, energy, and leisure dominate the local price structure, making routine consumption comparatively expensive despite relatively cheap rents. The purchasing power picture sharpens when median income is layered onto these price signals. Eagle County households earn $103,174 annually, a 58.8% premium over the national median of $65,047. After accounting for the typical rent burden of 23.6% of income, a median earner spends roughly $24,384 on housing each year, leaving $78,790 for all other expenses. The Purchasing Power Index of 101,294 underscores that, after adjusting for the county’s price levels, residents retain slightly more real income than the national average. However, the high services index erodes a portion of that advantage; for example, a family spending $500 per month on utilities and healthcare in a typical U.S. market would face roughly $650 in Eagle County, a 30% increase that directly reduces discretionary cash. Consequently, while the county’s wage premium cushions the impact of elevated service costs, the net disposable income advantage is modest. Demographic and labor‑market characteristics help explain the cost dynamics. With 33.9% of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher and a median age of 39.3, the workforce is relatively skilled and mature, supporting higher earnings and a willingness to pay for premium services. The unemployment rate of 4.2% sits below the national average, indicating a stable labor market that sustains demand for higher‑priced healthcare and utilities. Over the past decade, the RPP rose from 100.59 to 101.86, a modest 1.3% increase, while median household income surged 42.9% from $72,214 to $103,174. Rental costs, however, outpaced income growth, climbing 59.7% from $1,272 to $2,032. The gap suggests that wages have not fully kept pace with housing price acceleration, even though rents remain the cheapest component of the overall cost index. Prospective residents should anchor budgeting around the county’s service premium. A realistic monthly budget might allocate $2,032 for rent, $500–$600 for utilities and health insurance, $400 for groceries (aligned with the near‑national goods index), and $300–$400 for dining out, reflecting the 130.5 services index. Transportation fuel costs will be close to the national norm, given the 99.2 goods index, but winter heating expenses can add another $150–$200 during colder months. After covering these core categories, a median earner would retain roughly $4,500–$5,000 per month for savings, debt service, or discretionary spending. Understanding that service costs drive the budget, rather than housing, is essential for accurate financial planning in Eagle County.

What this means for residents of Eagle County, Colorado

With a Purchasing Power Index of 101294.0, a median household income of $103,174 in Eagle County, Colorado has the equivalent buying power of $104,509,072 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 86.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
