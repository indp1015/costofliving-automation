📊 Local Market Analysis

Russell County’s cost of living sits well below the national average, with a composite Relative Price Parity (RPP) of 89.1 versus 100 nationwide. Grocery, apparel and fuel prices are only modestly lower than the country’s benchmark, reflected in a Goods index of 94.6, while the Services index plunges to 61.6, indicating that healthcare, utilities and dining out cost roughly 38 % less than the national norm. Rental costs, measured by the housing component of the RPP, are also subdued at 89.9, aligning closely with the overall index and reinforcing the county’s affordability profile. The dominant driver of the local price structure is the services sector, whose index is the lowest of the three components. Because everyday necessities such as medical care, electricity and restaurant meals are markedly cheaper, residents experience a generally inexpensive daily life despite Goods prices being only slightly under national levels. Median monthly rent of $898, while 3 % below the national median of $928, consumes 21.5 % of the median household income of $50,046, leaving roughly $39,200 of annual earnings for all other expenses. After subtracting the average rent payment, the median household retains about $2,600 per month for food, transportation, health, and discretionary spending—a figure that translates into a Purchasing Power Index of 56,170.2, roughly 56 % of the national benchmark, underscoring that disposable income is constrained relative to the broader United States. Demographic characteristics shape this affordability landscape. With only 10.8 % of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher and an unemployment rate of 6.6 %, the labor market leans toward lower‑wage occupations, which dovetails with the modest median income. The median age of 37.2 suggests a workforce in its prime earning years, yet limited educational attainment curtails upward wage mobility, reinforcing the reliance on low‑cost services and housing to stretch limited earnings. The unemployment figure, modestly above the national rate, signals a labor market that offers fewer high‑paying opportunities, further emphasizing the importance of the county’s cheap service costs for household budgeting. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped from 89.78 in 2010 to 89.10 in 2023, a marginal 0.8 % decline, indicating that consumer prices have held steady while national inflation has outpaced local price growth. Median household income, however, surged 35.1 % from $37,049 to $50,046, outstripping the 22.7 % rise in median rent that moved from $732 to $898. The faster pace of income growth relative to housing costs has improved real purchasing power, yet the modest increase in goods prices and the persistently low services index mean that the net benefit to residents is primarily felt in discretionary spending capacity rather than in a dramatic shift in living standards. Prospective residents should budget rent as the single largest fixed expense, allocating roughly one‑fifth of gross income to housing. The remaining 80 % of earnings must cover goods and services, with the latter being especially inexpensive; a realistic monthly allocation might be $400–$500 for groceries, $150 for transportation fuel, and $200 for utilities and healthcare. Because services are cheap, discretionary spending on dining and entertainment can stretch further than in higher‑cost regions, but the limited wage base and modest purchasing power index advise a cautious approach to nonessential expenditures.

What this means for residents of Russell County, Alabama

With a Purchasing Power Index of 56170.2, a median household income of $50,046 in Russell County, Alabama has the equivalent buying power of $28,110,938 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 89.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
