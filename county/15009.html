📊 Local Market Analysis

Maui County’s cost of living exceeds the national benchmark by nearly 10%, with an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 109.7 (national = 100). Goods such as groceries, apparel and fuel register at 110.3, while services—including healthcare, utilities and dining—reach 128.7. Rental costs dominate the index at 191.7, indicating that housing is the primary driver of the local expense structure. Consumption patterns reveal that daily life is comparatively expensive, not because of everyday goods but because of shelter. The housing component is more than 80 points above the national baseline, dwarfing the modest 10‑point premium for goods. Services sit roughly 29 points above the baseline, suggesting that routine expenses like medical care and utilities also weigh heavily on household budgets. Consequently, residents allocate a disproportionate share of income to rent, making Maui’s cost profile rent‑intensive rather than broadly inflationary. Median household income of $95,076 provides a purchasing power index of 86,666.7, yet rent consumes 23.5% of that earnings, leaving limited margin for discretionary spending. The purchasing power index, while elevated, reflects that a sizable portion of earnings is directed toward housing rather than discretionary consumption. This balance underscores that even with above‑average incomes, the high cost of shelter constrains overall purchasing flexibility. Local demographics shape this environment. A 20% share of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher suggests a relatively educated workforce, yet the median age of 42.9 points to an older population with established housing needs. The unemployment rate of 5..6% is modest, indicating a stable labor market that supports income levels but does not pressure wages upward significantly. The age distribution and education level likely sustain demand for rental housing, contributing to the elevated rent index. Trend data show minimal change in the overall RPP, moving from 109.56 in 2010 to 109.703 in 2023—a marginal 0.1% increase. Median income rose 43% from $66,476 to $95,076, while median rent climbed 44.8% from $1,287 to $1,863. The parallel percentage growth in income and rent suggests that wage gains have roughly tracked housing cost inflation, preserving the rent‑burden ratio near 23.5%. This alignment indicates that while nominal earnings have improved, the cost structure has not outpaced compensation. Prospective residents should budget for rent at $1,863 per month, recognizing that this alone represents a significant share of typical earnings. Goods and services, though higher than the national average, remain secondary to housing in overall expense allocation. The high rent burden implies that discretionary spending on dining, entertainment and non‑essential goods will require careful prioritization. Understanding that housing dominates the cost profile helps households plan for a budget where shelter consumes roughly one‑quarter of income, leaving the remainder to cover daily consumption and savings goals.
