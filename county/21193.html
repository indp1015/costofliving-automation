📊 Local Market Analysis

Perry County presents a cost of living profile significantly below the national baseline, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 89.9 in 2023, meaning local consumer prices sit roughly 10 percent under the U.S. average. This aggregate figure masks a sharp bifurcation in the consumption basket: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel carry an index of 94.5, only modestly discounted from the national level, while services—encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining—plummet to an index of 62.9, the lowest component by a wide margin. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 80.9, translating to a median monthly rent of $795 against a national median of $928. Consequently, daily life is inexpensive relative to the country, but the savings are heavily concentrated in service consumption rather than the tangible goods households purchase most frequently. The median household income of $45,261 lags the national median of $65,047 by over 30 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 50,351.5 indicates that the lower price level partially offsets the nominal earnings gap. After accounting for local costs, the typical household retains purchasing power equivalent to roughly $50,350 in national-average dollars, a figure that remains materially below the U.S. median income but represents a meaningful stretch of local wages. Rent burden stands at a manageable 21.1 percent of gross income, well under the 30 percent stress threshold, suggesting housing costs—while rising—are not currently the primary squeeze on disposable income. Instead, the goods index near parity implies that essentials like fuel and food consume a disproportionate share of the local paycheck compared to service-heavy budgets in higher-cost metros. Demographic and labor market fundamentals reinforce the structural constraints on earning potential. An unemployment rate of 5.9 percent exceeds the national norm, and educational attainment remains low, with only 6.2 percent of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher against a backdrop of a median age of 41.4 years. This profile suggests a labor market anchored in sectors that typically offer limited wage growth, restricting the ability of households to outpace inflation through career advancement. The workforce composition effectively caps the upside for median income growth, making the county’s low service-cost environment a critical stabilizer for household solvency rather than a temporary windfall. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP drifted marginally lower from 90.4 to 89.9, a negligible 0.6 percent decline, while median income surged 38.6 percent from $32,667 to $45,261. However, median rent accelerated faster, climbing 48.6 percent from $535 to $795, outpacing wage growth by a full 10 percentage points. This divergence signals that the primary inflationary pressure on local budgets is shelter, even though the rental index remains below 100. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus is clear: expect near-national prices at the grocery store and gas pump, significant discounts on healthcare and utilities, and a rental market where costs have risen sharply over the last decade but still sit comfortably below the national median, leaving a modest buffer for other expenditures provided income aligns with local wage scales.

What this means for residents of Perry County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 50351.5, a median household income of $45,261 in Perry County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $22,789,592 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
