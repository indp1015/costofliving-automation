📊 Local Market Analysis

McLean County registers an overall Regional Price Parity of 89.9, placing local consumer prices roughly ten percent below the national baseline, yet the aggregate figure masks sharp divergence across spending categories. Goods — groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — carry an index of 94.5, only marginally cheaper than the U.S. average, while services including healthcare, utilities, and dining sit at a strikingly low 62.9, the lowest component by a wide margin. Rental costs, indexed at 80.9, also track well under the national level, but the goods category exerts the strongest upward pressure on daily budgets because households cannot easily substitute away from food and fuel. Consequently, daily life is inexpensive relative to metropolitan areas, but the savings concentrate in discretionary services rather than essential consumables, meaning a resident’s grocery bill and commute costs feel familiar even as utility and medical outlays drop sharply. The median household income of $70,549 exceeds the national median of $65,047, and when adjusted by the local price level the purchasing power index rises to $78,484, indicating that the typical earner commands roughly twenty percent more real purchasing capacity than the nominal wage suggests. Rent consumes only 10.2 percent of gross income — a median monthly outlay of $602 versus the national $928 — leaving an unusually large share of cash flow for savings, debt reduction, or goods consumption. Demographic context reinforces this dynamic: a median age of 43.2 years and a bachelor’s attainment rate of just 10.6 percent point to a workforce anchored in skilled trades, manufacturing, and healthcare support rather than high-wage knowledge sectors, while the 6.6 percent unemployment rate signals modest labor slack that may suppress wage growth but also limits competitive bidding for rental units. Over the 2010–2023 period the RPP slipped only 0.6 percent, from 90.43 to 89.89, reflecting remarkable price stability in a region often subject to commodity volatility. Meanwhile, nominal median income surged 73 percent, from $40,770 to $70,549, far outpacing the 22.9 percent increase in median rent from $490 to $602. Real wage growth has therefore been substantial, expanding the gap between earnings and shelter costs and boosting the purchasing power index well above the national benchmark. The data suggest that income gains have not merely kept pace with expenses but have decisively outrun them, a trajectory uncommon in many rural counties where stagnant wages coincide with rising housing pressure. A prospective resident should budget for goods expenses that mirror national averages — expect grocery and fuel receipts comparable to suburban Ohio or Indiana — while allocating significantly less for utilities, insurance premiums, and restaurant meals. The low rent burden creates flexibility to absorb goods-price spikes or fund vehicle maintenance, a critical consideration given limited public transit. However, the narrow educational base and elevated unemployment rate imply that income volatility remains a risk; households should maintain liquid reserves equivalent to at least three months of goods and rent outlays to weather sectoral layoffs without resorting to high-interest credit.

What this means for residents of McLean County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 78483.7, a median household income of $70,549 in McLean County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $55,369,466 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
