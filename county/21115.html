📊 Local Market Analysis

Johnson County, Kentucky, registers an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 89.9, positioning local consumer prices roughly 10 percent below the national average, though this aggregate masks significant divergence across spending categories. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — represent the highest cost component at 94.5, tracking just 5.5 percent below the U.S. benchmark, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining plunge to 62.9, a staggering 37 percent discount. Rental costs sit at 80.9, offering meaningful shelter savings relative to the national median rent of $928, with the local median monthly rent resting at $698. This structure creates a bifurcated reality: residents pay near-national prices for tangible necessities but capture deep discounts on labor-intensive services, a dynamic typical of rural Appalachian economies where wage floors depress service-sector pricing. The consumption breakdown reveals that daily life is structurally inexpensive regarding services but less advantageous for goods purchasers. With services comprising a substantial share of household budgets, the 62.9 index acts as a powerful deflator on the overall cost of living, yet the 94.5 goods index means trips to the grocery store or gas station deliver limited relief compared to urban centers. Median household income stands at $43,014, roughly 34 percent below the national $65,047, but the Purchasing Power Index of 47,851.8 indicates that local wages stretch further than nominal figures suggest. Rent burden consumes only 19.5 percent of gross income, a notably low threshold that preserves discretionary capacity for a population where 9.1 percent hold a bachelor’s degree or higher and the median age sits at 42.0 years. An unemployment rate of 4.7 percent suggests a labor market with slack, likely suppressing wage growth in the service sectors that keep the services RPP so depressed. Over the past decade, the RPP has remained remarkably stable, slipping marginally from 90.433 in 2010 to 89.89 in 2023, a negligible 0.6 percent decline that signals persistent price stability. However, nominal income growth of 20.9 percent — rising from $35,570 to $43,014 — has lagged behind median rent appreciation of 28.5 percent, which climbed from $543 to $698. This divergence indicates that while the broader consumption basket has not inflated, the single largest fixed expense for renters has accelerated faster than earnings, gradually eroding the shelter affordability advantage. The labor market’s educational profile and age demographic suggest limited upward pressure on high-wage employment, meaning income gains may continue to trail housing cost trajectory if current trends persist. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the trade-off between low service costs and sticky goods pricing. A household relocating from a metro area should anticipate grocery and fuel bills that mirror national averages, while healthcare copays, utility bills, and restaurant meals will likely cost significantly less. The sub-20 percent rent-to-income ratio provides a buffer against volatility in variable expenses, but the 28.5 percent decade-long rent escalation warrants scrutiny of lease terms and annual increase clauses. With median income trailing the national figure by over $22,000, the purchasing power gain derived from the 89.9 RPP does not fully close the earnings gap; residents effectively earn less in real terms but pay disproportionately less for shelter and services, creating a viable but constrained household budget environment where goods inflation poses the primary risk to disposable income.

What this means for residents of Johnson County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 47851.8, a median household income of $43,014 in Johnson County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $20,582,973 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
