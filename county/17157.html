📊 Local Market Analysis

Randolph County presents a distinct cost-of-living profile where the overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 98.8 sits marginally below the national baseline, masking a sharp divergence between consumption categories that defines the local household budget. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — run 1.6 percent above the national average at 101.6, marking the highest cost component in the county and imposing a persistent premium on daily necessities that residents cannot easily substitute. Conversely, services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining register at 92.4, while rental costs sit deeply discounted at 80.4, a full 19.6 percent below the national level. This structural split means the median monthly rent of $760 consumes only 13.2 percent of the $68,870 median household income, a burden significantly lighter than the national median rent of $928 against a national median income of $65,047, effectively insulating tenants from the shelter-cost pressures prevalent in metropolitan corridors. The purchasing power calculus translates these price differentials into a tangible income advantage. Adjusting the median household income by the local RPP yields a Purchasing Power Index of $69,740.4, exceeding the nominal median by roughly $870 and surpassing the national median income benchmark. This surplus arises almost entirely from the housing discount; because shelter represents the largest single line item in most family budgets, the 80.4 rental index functions as an implicit wage subsidy. However, the 101.6 goods index erodes a portion of that gain at the checkout counter, meaning households feel the pinch on variable essentials — food and fuel — even as their fixed housing obligation remains remarkably low. The net effect is a budget environment where discretionary capacity is higher than nominal wages suggest, provided consumption patterns lean toward services rather than goods. Local labor market dynamics reinforce this affordability narrative while highlighting structural constraints. An unemployment rate of 5.1 percent sits above the national lows seen in recent cycles, suggesting slack in the labor market that may suppress wage growth, yet the 43 percent surge in median income from $48,158 in 2010 to $68,870 in 2023 has dramatically outpaced the 22 percent climb in median rent over the same period. The RPP itself has drifted downward from 101.778 to 98.752, a 3 percent decline indicating that local consumer prices have grown slower than the national basket. With only 9.4 percent of adults holding a bachelor’s degree or higher and a median age of 43, the workforce skews toward established, non-degreed occupations where wage gains have historically tracked cost-of-living adjustments rather than productivity premiums, stabilizing the purchasing power trajectory. For a prospective resident, the budgeting reality centers on a favorable fixed-cost foundation offset by variable-cost volatility. The sub-$800 median rent allows for aggressive savings or debt reduction, a rarity in the current national climate, but the grocery and fuel premium demands disciplined procurement strategies — bulk purchasing, fuel-efficient routing, or cross-border shopping where geography permits. Utility and healthcare costs, indexed at 92.4, offer modest relief on recurring service bills. The critical variable remains income trajectory: with unemployment above the national rate and educational attainment low, household income growth relies on tight labor markets rather than credential-driven advancement. Residents benefit from a decade of real wage appreciation, but sustaining that margin requires monitoring whether goods inflation re-accelerates relative to the national trend, which would disproportionately impact a county where the consumption basket is weighted toward tangible necessities.

What this means for residents of Randolph County, Illinois

With a Purchasing Power Index of 69740.4, a median household income of $68,870 in Randolph County, Illinois has the equivalent buying power of $48,030,213 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.4 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
