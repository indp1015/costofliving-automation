📊 Local Market Analysis

Powell County registers an overall Regional Price Parity of 89.9, placing local consumer prices roughly ten percent below the national baseline, yet the aggregate figure masks a sharply bifurcated cost structure. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — carry an index of 94.5, only marginally cheaper than the U.S. average, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 62.9, the lowest component by a wide margin. Rental costs sit at 80.9, meaning housing consumes a smaller share of the budget than in most counties, but the near-parity pricing of everyday goods ensures that trips to the supermarket or gas station feel far less discounted than the headline number suggests. Daily life is therefore inexpensive in structural categories like rent and medical care, yet residents absorb nearly national-level outlays for the consumables they purchase most frequently. The median household income of $39,811 translates into a purchasing power index of 44,288.6 after adjusting for local prices, a figure that still trails the unadjusted national median of $65,047 by nearly thirty-two percent. Rent burden stands at a manageable 22.4 percent of gross income — well below the thirty-percent stress threshold — with the median monthly rent of $743 comparing favorably to the $928 national benchmark. However, the labor market context complicates the arithmetic: a 7.1 percent unemployment rate and a bachelor's attainment rate of just 10.5 percent signal limited high-wage employment, while a median age of 39.3 years suggests a workforce in its prime earning years that nonetheless lacks the credential density to command metropolitan salaries. The demographic profile effectively caps upward income mobility, anchoring purchasing power gains to the modest cost advantages already embedded in the RPP. Over the 2010–2023 window, the overall RPP drifted downward a fractional 0.6 percent, from 90.433 to 89.89, indicating near-stagnant consumer price levels relative to the nation. Nominal median income rose 33.9 percent, from $29,736 to $39,811, yet median rent accelerated slightly faster at 36.8 percent, climbing from $543 to $743. The divergence, though narrow, means housing costs have marginally outpaced wage growth, eroding a portion of the real income gains that the stable RPP would otherwise imply. Because goods prices remain sticky near the national index, the modest deflation in services and rent has not been sufficient to widen the real wage gap meaningfully; the median worker’s inflation-adjusted gains are positive but thinner than the nominal thirty-three percent increase suggests. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that mirror national averages despite the county’s low overall cost profile, while anticipating meaningful savings on healthcare visits, utility statements, and restaurant meals. The sub-$750 median rent leaves room in a typical paycheck for transportation or debt service, but the elevated unemployment rate and thin professional labor market imply that income disruption risk runs higher than in diversified economies. Households moving from higher-wage regions will experience an immediate boost in discretionary capacity, yet those reliant on local earnings must plan for a ceiling on wage growth that keeps long-term purchasing power tethered to the current 44,288.6 index level.

What this means for residents of Powell County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 44288.6, a median household income of $39,811 in Powell County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $17,631,735 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
