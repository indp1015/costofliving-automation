📊 Local Market Analysis

Kenton County residents navigate a cost environment running 10.1 percent below the national benchmark, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity for 2023 registering 89.9 against a U.S. average of 100. This aggregate discount masks significant divergence across consumption categories: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel sit at 94.5, only marginally cheaper than the national norm, while rental housing costs settle at 80.9. The dominant distortion lies in services—healthcare, utilities, and dining—which plummet to an index of 62.9, the lowest component by a wide margin and the primary driver pulling the overall index below parity. Consequently, daily life is structurally inexpensive for service-intensive households, though the relative price of tangible goods remains sticky near national levels. The median household income of $79,421 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 22.1 percent, translating into a purchasing power index of 88,353.5 that reflects substantial real wage advantage after local price adjustments. Rent burden illustrates this dynamic clearly: median monthly rent of $1,018 consumes just 15.4 percent of gross income, compared to a national median rent of $928 that typically demands a larger share of the lower national income. This gap suggests the median worker retains significant disposable income after shelter costs, a buffer that absorbs the near-average goods index. The labor market reinforces this stability; a 3.8 percent unemployment rate signals tight conditions, while a median age of 37.8 years and a 24.1 percent bachelor’s degree attainment rate indicate a workforce positioned in prime earning years with credentials that command above-average wages in the Greater Cincinnati metro area. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the RPP drifted downward marginally from 90.433 to 89.89, a 0.6 percent decline in relative price levels, while nominal median income surged 46.3 percent from $54,296 to $79,421. Median rent increased 36.3 percent over the same span, from $747 to $1,018, lagging income growth by roughly ten percentage points. This trajectory confirms that wage appreciation has outpaced both general inflation and shelter costs, expanding real purchasing power considerably. The goods category’s persistence near 94.5 implies that national supply chain dynamics and commodity pricing limit local discount potential, whereas the services index at 62.9 likely reflects regional healthcare provider competition and regulated utility structures that have contained cost growth more effectively than the national average. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that mirror national averages, requiring no special adjustment from a relocation originating in a typical U.S. metro. The outsized savings emerge in recurring service obligations—health insurance premiums, electricity, water, and restaurant meals—where the 37.1 percent discount to the national index generates monthly surpluses. Rental housing, while 19.1 percent below the national price level, has risen faster than the overall RPP, suggesting future lease renewals may erode a portion of the current rent-to-income advantage. Households should weigh the high goods floor against the deep services trough; those with elevated healthcare utilization or energy-intensive lifestyles capture the maximum arbitrage, while single-person households spending heavily on goods see a narrower benefit.

What this means for residents of Kenton County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 88353.5, a median household income of $79,421 in Kenton County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $70,171,233 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
