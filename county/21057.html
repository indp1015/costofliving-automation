📊 Local Market Analysis

Cumberland County presents a cost of living profile that sits 10.1 percentage points below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 89.9 in 2023, a marginal improvement from 90.4 in 2010. This aggregate figure masks significant internal divergence: the Goods component, encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel, runs at 94.5, merely 5.5 points below the national baseline, while Services—covering healthcare, utilities, and dining—plummets to 62.9, a staggering 37.1-point discount. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 80.9. Consequently, daily life is structurally inexpensive regarding labor-intensive services, yet residents face near-national pricing for tangible goods, creating a consumption environment where a trip to the grocery store or gas station extracts a disproportionately large share of the local paycheck compared to a visit to a medical provider or utility payment. The median household income of $39,662 trails the national median of $65,047 by 39 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 44,122.8 reveals that the deep discount on services effectively boosts real income by roughly 11 percent above nominal levels. Housing affordability is a distinct bright spot; median monthly rent of $553 consumes only 16.7 percent of gross income, a figure roughly half the standard 30 percent burden threshold and far below the national median rent of $928. Over the 2010–2023 period, nominal income growth of 23.9 percent narrowly outpaced rent growth of 21.8 percent, suggesting a slight improvement in shelter affordability even as the overall RPP remained essentially flat. This dynamic indicates that wage gains have translated into genuine discretionary capacity rather than being fully absorbed by shelter inflation. Local demographics sharpen the picture of economic vulnerability beneath the favorable affordability ratios. An unemployment rate of 3.0 percent signals a tight labor market, yet educational attainment remains low—only 9.3 percent of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher—and the median age of 45.1 years skews significantly older than the national average. This composition suggests a workforce concentrated in lower-wage, service-oriented or manual sectors with limited upward mobility, explaining the persistent income gap versus the nation. The low Services RPP likely reflects both lower local labor costs for healthcare and personal care and potentially reduced access to specialized providers, a critical nuance for an aging population that may require more frequent medical intervention despite the lower sticker price. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the asymmetry between fixed and variable costs. Shelter is remarkably cheap, locking in a low baseline for the largest household line item, and utility or healthcare outlays carry a substantial structural discount. However, the Goods index at 94.5 means food, transportation fuel, and clothing offer little arbitrage opportunity; these expenses will feel nearly as heavy as in a major metro area on a nominal income that is 39 percent smaller. A household moving from a higher-cost region should not assume proportional savings across the basket; the effective "raise" from lower services and rent is real but partial, and the high marginal propensity to consume goods on a $39,662 income leaves limited buffer for unexpected price spikes in fuel or groceries.

What this means for residents of Cumberland County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 44122.8, a median household income of $39,662 in Cumberland County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $17,499,985 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
