📊 Local Market Analysis

Monroe County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 11.2 percentage points below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 88.8 in 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals a distinct bifurcation in daily expenses. Goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—run only 3.4 percent below the U.S. baseline at 96.6, meaning a trip to the supermarket or gas station delivers minimal savings relative to coastal metros. Conversely, the services category, covering healthcare, utilities, and dining, plunges to 66.0, a staggering 34 percent discount that serves as the primary engine suppressing the aggregate index. Rental costs, indexed at 87.3, occupy the middle ground, contributing to a housing environment where the median monthly outlay of $787 claims just 13.0 percent of the median household income of $72,518, a burden significantly lighter than the national median rent of $928 against a national income of $65,047. This consumption breakdown dictates that daily life is inexpensive primarily because labor-intensive services are cheap, not because physical goods are bargains. The purchasing power index of 81,692.9 quantifies this advantage: the median household effectively commands $9,175 more in real goods and services than their nominal income suggests, a surplus driven almost entirely by the 66.0 services index. A household budget here benefits disproportionately from low healthcare premiums, utility bills, and restaurant tabs, while grocery bills and vehicle fuel costs track near national norms. The labor market reinforces this dynamic; an unemployment rate of 2.9 percent signals tight demand for workers, yet educational attainment remains modest at 15.0 percent holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, and a median age of 42.1 suggests a settled, experienced workforce rather than a transient, high-earning professional class. Wage growth has been robust historically, with median income surging 56.6 percent since 2010 compared to a 33.4 percent rise in median rent, but the goods-heavy consumption basket limits the full translation of nominal gains into real discretionary spending. Over the past decade, the overall RPP has drifted downward from 91.1 to 88.8, a 2.6 percent decline indicating that local consumer prices have grown slower than the national average. This disinflationary trend, combined with income growth that has outpaced rent escalation by a wide margin, has expanded the real wage buffer considerably. However, the persistence of the goods index near parity (96.6) acts as a floor on the cost of living; residents cannot arbitrage food or energy costs downward by moving here. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the asymmetry between fixed and variable costs: housing and services offer deep, structural discounts that free up cash flow for savings or debt service, but the weekly grocery run and commute fuel expenses will feel familiar to anyone relocating from a median-cost metro. The financial profile favors households with high service consumption needs—families requiring childcare, older adults managing healthcare utilization, or remote workers leveraging low utility overhead—while offering less relative advantage to single-person households whose budgets weight heavily toward goods.

What this means for residents of Monroe County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 81692.9, a median household income of $72,518 in Monroe County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $59,242,057 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
