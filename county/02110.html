📊 Local Market Analysis

Juneau’s cost of living sits modestly above the national baseline, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) indicating an overall index of 103.3 versus the U.S. average of 100. Goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—register a 3.7 % premium (103.7), while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are 3.3 % cheaper than the countrywide norm (96.7). Housing, measured by rental costs, is the outlier, climbing to an index of 113.3, which translates to a 13.3 % surcharge on the national rental price level. The median monthly rent of $1,462 in Juneau confirms this disparity, outpacing the national median of $928 by roughly 57 %. The rental component dominates the local cost structure. Even though goods are slightly more expensive and services are marginally cheaper, the 13.3 % housing premium drives the bulk of the expense burden. Daily life for a typical household is therefore skewed toward higher shelter costs rather than everyday consumption. The rent‑to‑income ratio—17.5 % of median household earnings—remains comfortably below the conventional 30 % affordability threshold, suggesting that while housing is pricier, incomes are sufficiently robust to mitigate severe cost pressure. Nonetheless, the elevated rental index means that discretionary spending on non‑housing items must accommodate a higher baseline outlay for shelter. Median household income in Juneau stands at $100,513, a 54 % increase over the national median of $65,047. After accounting for the median rent, a resident retains roughly $92,549 of annual earnings, equating to a disposable income of $7,712 per month. The Purchasing Power Index of 97,304.9 reflects this balance: despite higher prices, the region’s earnings preserve purchasing power close to the national average (the index is calibrated to a national baseline of 100,000). In practical terms, a Juneau household can afford a comparable basket of goods and services to a median‑income household elsewhere, but a larger share of its budget is earmarked for housing, leaving less flexibility for savings or non‑essential consumption. Local demographics reinforce the economic picture. With 25.6 % of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, the labor force is moderately educated, supporting wages that outstrip the national median. The median age of 39.8 years aligns with a mature, established workforce, while the unemployment rate of 4.0 %—below the national average—indicates a relatively tight labor market. These factors combine to sustain higher earnings, which in turn cushion the impact of elevated rental costs. However, the modest educational attainment also suggests limited concentration in high‑skill, high‑pay sectors, meaning the premium on housing is not offset by a proportionate surge in high‑wage occupations. Over the past decade, the RPP index has risen from 103.099 in 2010 to 103.297 in 2023, a negligible 0.2 % increase, indicating price stability across most categories. In contrast, median household income surged 17.2 % (from $85,746 to $100,513), while median rent climbed 23.1 % (from $1,188 to $1,462). The faster rent growth relative to income suggests that housing costs have eroded a portion of the income gains, even though the rent‑burden metric remains low. The near‑flat RPP trajectory implies that non‑housing price pressures have been muted, allowing wages to outpace general inflation but not fully offset the steeper rise in rental expenses. Prospective residents should structure their budgets around a pronounced housing component. Allocating roughly 15–18 % of gross income to rent will keep the household within the observed rent‑burden range. The remaining 82–85 % of earnings must cover utilities, healthcare, food, and transportation, with services costing slightly less than the national average—potentially freeing modest funds for discretionary spending. Given the median rent of $1,462, a household earning the local median would need to budget about $3,000–$3,500 per month for all non‑housing expenses to maintain a comfortable standard of living. Awareness of the high rental index and the relatively generous income level is essential for accurate financial planning in Juneau.

What this means for residents of Juneau City and Borough, Alaska

Because the Housing Rent index is 113.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
