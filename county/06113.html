📊 Local Market Analysis

Yolo County residents face a cost structure running 12.2 percent above the national baseline, driven almost entirely by a services sector that costs 57.8 percent more than the U.S. average. The Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity for 2023 places the overall index at 112.2, yet this headline figure masks extreme divergence: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel sit at a relatively modest 106.8, while rental costs register 147.3. The services component—encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining—stands as the primary inflationary engine at 157.8, meaning daily household operations from medical visits to electricity bills absorb disproportionate shares of local budgets compared to virtually any other expenditure category. This consumption breakdown reveals that daily life is expensive specifically where substitution is difficult. A median household income of $88,818 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 36.5 percent, yet the purchasing power index of $79,164 indicates that nearly $9,654 of nominal earnings evaporates simply covering the local price premium. Rent burden sits at 23.7 percent of gross income against a median monthly rent of $1,757—nearly double the national median of $928—but this shelter cost represents only one facet. The services index of 157.8 suggests that a household spending $1,000 nationally on healthcare, childcare, and utilities would allocate roughly $1,578 locally, creating a persistent drag on disposable income that rent metrics alone fail to capture. Demographic and labor dynamics amplify these pressures. An unemployment rate of 5.3 percent exceeds typical full-employment thresholds, signaling slack in a labor market where only 22.9 percent of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, yet the median age of 32.3 years indicates a population in prime household-formation years. This cohort faces rising family-service costs—pediatric care, early education, insurance—precisely where the local index peaks. The labor market’s inability to fully absorb workers at wage levels commensurate with the 157.8 services index creates a structural mismatch: earnings growth has been robust, but the composition of local employment may not generate the marginal income required to offset service-sector inflation. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP climbed from 109.7 to 112.2, a cumulative 2.3 percent increase that appears modest until juxtaposed with income and rent trajectories. Median income surged 61.5 percent from $54,989 to $88,818, while median rent advanced 59.4 percent from $1,102 to $1,757. Nominal wage growth has marginally outpaced shelter costs, preserving the 23.7 percent rent-to-income ratio, yet the services component—now 57.8 percent above national norms—has likely absorbed much of the real income gain. For a prospective resident, the budgeting reality centers on the inelasticity of the services basket: healthcare premiums, utility rates, and childcare fees offer limited behavioral offsets compared to discretionary goods. A household relocating from a median-cost metro should model a 15–20 percent upward adjustment to non-housing operating expenses rather than focusing solely on the rent differential.

What this means for residents of Yolo County, California

With a Purchasing Power Index of 79164.0, a median household income of $88,818 in Yolo County, California has the equivalent buying power of $70,311,882 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 147.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
