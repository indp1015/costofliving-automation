📊 Local Market Analysis

Crawford County registers an overall Regional Price Parity of 86.8, placing local consumer prices 13.2 percent below the national benchmark, yet the aggregate figure masks sharp divergence across spending categories. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — run at 93.1, only marginally cheaper than the U.S. average, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 56.7, the lowest component by a wide margin. Rental costs sit at 81.9, offering meaningful shelter savings relative to the national index of 100. The result is a cost structure where daily necessities like food and gasoline absorb a disproportionate share of the household budget, while services — often the largest expenditure category in higher-cost metros — are unusually affordable, creating a consumption environment that feels inexpensive for discretionary services but tight on essential goods. Purchasing power data confirms the arithmetic advantage: the median household income of $60,362 translates to a Purchasing Power Index of 69,533.5, meaning the typical earner commands roughly $9,200 more in real goods and services than the nominal wage suggests. Rent burden stands at a modest 16.5 percent of gross income, with the median monthly rent of $828 comparing favorably to the national median of $928. However, the local labor market introduces friction. An unemployment rate of 5.2 percent exceeds the national norm, and only 13.7 percent of adults hold a bachelor's degree or higher, signaling a workforce concentrated in lower-wage sectors. The median age of 39.1 years suggests a stable, working-age population, but the educational profile limits upward income mobility, anchoring many households near the current earnings floor. Over the past decade, the RPP has drifted downward from 88.6 to 86.8, a 2.0 percent decline in relative price levels, while nominal median income surged 46.3 percent from $41,268 to $60,362. Median rent rose 30.0 percent over the same span, from $637 to $828, a pace substantially slower than income growth. This divergence has expanded real purchasing power considerably; the typical household today retains a larger share of post-shelter income than in 2010, even after accounting for the sticky goods index near 93. The disinflationary pressure on services — down to 56.7 — has been the primary driver of the improving real wage trajectory, offsetting the persistence of near-national grocery and fuel costs. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that mirror national averages, with limited ability to substitute cheaper alternatives given the goods index at 93.1. Healthcare, utilities, and restaurant meals, by contrast, deliver outsized savings — the services index at 56.7 implies nearly half-price consumption relative to the typical U.S. city. Housing costs run roughly 18 percent below the national rental benchmark, keeping shelter well within the 30 percent affordability threshold at current income levels. The critical variable remains earned income: with unemployment above five percent and a low college-attainment rate, wage growth depends heavily on sector-specific demand rather than broad-based productivity gains. Households relocating from higher-cost regions will experience immediate relief on services and rent but should not assume goods expenditures will shrink proportionally.

What this means for residents of Crawford County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 69533.5, a median household income of $60,362 in Crawford County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $41,971,811 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
