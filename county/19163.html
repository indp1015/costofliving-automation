📊 Local Market Analysis

Scott County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 11.2 percentage points below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 88.8 for 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals a distinct bifurcation in daily expenses. Goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—run only 3.4 percent below the national baseline at 96.6, effectively mirroring average U.S. retail pricing, while services collapse to 66.0, a staggering 34 percent discount driven by below-average healthcare, utility, and dining costs. Rental housing occupies the middle ground at 87.3, translating to a median monthly outlay of $958 that consumes just 15.1 percent of the median household income of $76,363, a burden significantly lighter than the national median rent of $928 against a national median income of $65,047. The services category functions as the primary anchor pulling the overall index down, creating an environment where daily operational costs—keeping the lights on, accessing medical care, or eating out—are remarkably cheap relative to the rest of the country. This dynamic elevates the real purchasing power of the median earner to an adjusted $86,024, effectively granting households an $11,000 annual premium over their nominal wages. However, the near-parity pricing on goods means that trips to the grocery store or the gas pump offer no such arbitrage; residents pay mainstream prices for tangible consumables, limiting the discretionary gains derived from the service-side discount. Local labor market fundamentals reinforce this affordability profile. An unemployment rate of 3.7 percent signals a tight labor market supporting wage growth, evidenced by a 38.6 percent surge in median income over the last thirteen years, outpacing the 34.0 percent climb in median rent. The demographic profile—median age 39.0 with 22.6 percent holding a bachelor’s degree or higher—suggests a workforce concentrated in prime earning years with moderate educational attainment, likely clustered around the Quad Cities' logistics, manufacturing, and healthcare corridors. This composition sustains income growth without the extreme cost-push inflation seen in high-density tech hubs, allowing the RPP to actually decline from 91.1 in 2010 to 88.8 in 2023, a rare instance of real cost deflation relative to the national basket. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the divergence between fixed and variable costs. Housing consumes a low share of income, and services provide consistent savings, but the goods basket demands national-level pricing. A household relocating from a high-cost metro should not assume universal discounts; the grocery bill and vehicle fuel costs will feel familiar, while the cable bill, insurance premiums, and restaurant tabs will shrink noticeably. The data indicates that wage momentum has historically exceeded shelter inflation, but the goods component acts as a floor on the cost of living, preventing the deep overall affordability suggested by the headline RPP figure alone.

What this means for residents of Scott County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 86024.4, a median household income of $76,363 in Scott County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $65,690,813 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
