📊 Local Market Analysis

The Prince of Wales‑Hyder Census Area registers a cost of living that sits modestly above the national baseline, with the overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) at 103.3 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices—groceries, apparel, and fuel—track closely at 103.7, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are cheaper than average at 96.7. Housing, measured by rental costs, is the outlier, scoring 113.3, indicating that renters pay roughly 13 % more than the national median. Median monthly rent of $915 exceeds the national median of $928 only slightly, but the higher rental index reflects a tighter supply of affordable units in this remote region. The rental component dominates the local cost structure. Although goods are priced near the national level, the elevated housing index pushes the overall expense profile upward. Daily life for most households is therefore more expensive in the housing dimension while remaining competitive on groceries and fuel. The lower services index offsets some of the housing premium, suggesting that utilities, medical care, and restaurant meals cost less than elsewhere, which can soften the impact of higher rents for residents who limit discretionary spending. Disposable income calculations reveal a constrained purchasing environment. Median household income stands at $59,079, 9 % below the national median of $65,047. After allocating the median rent of $915, rent consumes 18.6 % of household earnings, leaving roughly $48,000 for all other expenses. The Purchasing Power Index of 57,193.3—well under the national benchmark—confirms that, after accounting for higher housing costs, the median resident retains about 12 % less real purchasing power than a typical American household. The lower services index partially mitigates this gap, but the net effect is a tighter budget for essentials and discretionary items. Demographically, the area’s labor market is characterized by an 8.1 % unemployment rate and a relatively low educational attainment, with only 12 % holding a bachelor’s degree or higher. The median age of 43.7 years points to a mature workforce, likely concentrated in sectors such as government, tourism, and resource extraction, which traditionally offer modest wages. The combination of higher unemployment and limited high‑skill labor contributes to the lower income levels and reinforces the rent‑burden pressure observed. Over the past decade, RPP has risen marginally from 103.099 to 103.297, a 0.2 % increase, while median household income grew 21.8 % from $48,523 to $59,079. Rental prices, however, climbed 12.8 % from $811 to $915. Income growth outpaced overall price inflation, but it lagged behind the acceleration in housing costs, widening the rent‑to‑income gap. Prospective residents should budget for a housing expense that will consume close to one‑fifth of gross earnings, allocate a slightly lower share for utilities and healthcare than the national average, and anticipate that discretionary spending will be constrained by the limited surplus after rent. Careful planning around housing choice, energy use, and local service pricing will be essential to maintain a balanced household budget in Prince of Wales‑Hyder.

What this means for residents of Prince of Wales-Hyder Census Area, Alaska
