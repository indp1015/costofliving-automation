📊 Local Market Analysis

Black Hawk County presents a cost of living profile significantly below the national baseline, registering an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 88.8 against the national 100, a discount that has deepened modestly from 91.1 in 2010. This aggregate figure masks a sharp bifurcation in consumer expenditure categories: goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—sit at 96.6, nearly tracking the national average, while services including healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 66.0, the lowest component and the primary driver of the county’s affordability advantage. Rental costs occupy a middle ground at 87.3, suggesting housing consumes a smaller share of local budgets than in most metropolitan areas, though the gap between goods and services pricing creates a unique consumption dynamic where discretionary service spending is exceptionally cheap relative to staple goods. The median household income of $64,581 sits marginally below the national median of $65,047, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 72,751.7 reveals that local wages stretch considerably further once the 11.2 percent price discount is applied. Rent burden stands at a low 17.9 percent, with median monthly rent of $962 exceeding the national $928 only slightly, indicating that shelter costs have not eroded the real income gains implied by the RPP. Over the 2010–2023 period, nominal median income rose 33.5 percent from $48,369, while median rent increased 37.4 percent from $700; despite rent outpacing income growth slightly, the declining RPP—down 2.6 percent over the decade—suggests broader consumer price deflation relative to the nation has preserved, and likely expanded, real purchasing power for the median worker. Local labor market characteristics reinforce this affordability narrative. An unemployment rate of 4.8 percent signals a reasonably tight market, while a median age of 35.7 years and a bachelor’s degree attainment rate of 19.3 percent—well below the national average—point toward an economy anchored in sectors that do not require high credentialization, potentially suppressing wage ceilings but also limiting cost-push inflation in services. The low services RPP of 66.0 likely reflects this demographic composition; lower demand density for high-end healthcare, professional services, and dining depresses prices for haircuts, restaurant meals, and utility administration, creating a daily expense environment where a household’s marginal dollar buys significantly more service consumption than in higher-cost metros. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the inversion of typical urban cost structures. Grocery bills and fuel costs will feel familiar, tracking within 3.4 percent of national norms, but healthcare premiums, utility deposits, and recurring service subscriptions—internet, landscaping, childcare—should be modeled at roughly two-thirds of expected national outlays. The 17.9 percent rent-to-income ratio leaves substantial discretionary capacity, yet the modest educational attainment level suggests limited high-wage upward mobility without sector-specific credentials. Households relocating from high-cost corridors should not assume the goods discount will persist; the 96.6 goods index implies exposure to national supply chain volatility for food and energy is nearly identical to coastal peers, meaning the primary hedge against inflation here remains the structural cheapness of locally delivered services.

What this means for residents of Black Hawk County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 72751.7, a median household income of $64,581 in Black Hawk County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $46,983,775 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
