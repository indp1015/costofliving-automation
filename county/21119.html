📊 Local Market Analysis

The cost of living in Knott County, Kentucky, sits well below the national norm, with the overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) index at 89.9 versus the United States benchmark of 100. Goods prices—groceries, apparel, and fuel—register an index of 94.5, still under the national average but the highest component locally. Services, encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining, are markedly cheap at 62.9, while rental costs are 80.9, indicating that housing expenses are roughly 19 % lower than the countrywide level. The dominant driver of the county’s price structure is the services sector, whose index is more than 35 points beneath the national figure. This deep discount on utilities, medical care, and restaurant meals translates into a daily life that feels inexpensive for most routine expenditures. Housing, although not the cheapest category, remains affordable; median rent of $731 is 21 % lower than the national median of $928, and rent burden consumes only 23.2 % of median household income. By contrast, goods costs are only modestly below national levels, so price advantages are concentrated in non‑essential spending rather than staple items. Disposable income for the median household can be gauged through the Purchasing Power Index of 41,980.2, which reflects the amount of goods and services that $1 of local income can purchase relative to the national baseline. With a median household income of $37,736, a resident retains roughly $29,200 after rent (23.2 % of income), leaving about $7,500 for other expenses. When adjusted for the local price environment, that residual purchasing power equates to roughly 56 % of what a median‑income household elsewhere could afford, underscoring the modest financial cushion available to most families. Demographic factors amplify the affordability narrative. Only 8.3 % of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, and the median age of 44.4 years suggests a mature population, likely with established consumption patterns and limited upward mobility. Unemployment stands at 11.6 %, well above the national rate, constraining labor‑market earnings and reinforcing reliance on low‑cost services and housing. The limited educational attainment curtails access to higher‑paying occupations, which in turn sustains the low price equilibrium for services as providers adjust to local wage constraints. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped marginally from 90.433 to 89.89, a 0.6 % decline, indicating that consumer prices have barely shifted relative to the nation. Median household income, however, rose 24.1 % from $30,411 to $37,736, outpacing the modest price movement. Rent, by contrast, surged 52.6 % from $479 to $731, eroding part of the income gain. The divergence suggests that while wages have improved, housing costs have accelerated faster than overall price stability, squeezing the budgetary margin for renters. Prospective residents should anchor their budgeting on three pillars: housing, goods, and services. With rent at $731 and a typical rent‑burden threshold of 30 % of income, a household earning the median $37,736 can comfortably allocate about $945 per month to rent, leaving room for utilities, healthcare, and food. Grocery and apparel bills will be slightly higher than the cheapest states, given the 94.5 goods index, so allocating roughly 30 % of disposable income to these categories is prudent. The exceptionally low services index permits discretionary spending on dining and entertainment without jeopardizing basic needs, but the high unemployment backdrop advises maintaining an emergency fund equivalent to at least three months of rent and essential expenses.

What this means for residents of Knott County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 41980.2, a median household income of $37,736 in Knott County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $15,841,648 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
