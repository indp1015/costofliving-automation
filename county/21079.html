📊 Local Market Analysis

Garrard County’s cost of living sits well below the national average, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 89.9 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices are the least discounted component, registering 94.5, while services are markedly cheaper at 62.9. Housing, measured by rental costs, comes in at 80.9, indicating that renters pay roughly 19 % less than the national norm. The median household income of $62,572 trails the national median of $65,047 by 3.8 %, yet the county’s lower price environment narrows the gap between earnings and expenses. The consumption profile is dominated by goods, which account for the highest relative price index. Even though groceries, apparel, and fuel are only 5.5 % below the national level, the pronounced discount on services—healthcare, utilities, and dining—drives the overall affordability. Daily life in Garrard County is therefore inexpensive in terms of discretionary spending on services, while essential goods remain only modestly cheaper. Rental costs reinforce this picture: the median monthly rent of $793 is 14.5 % below the national median of $928, and rent burden sits at 15.2 % of household income, well under the 30 % threshold that defines housing stress. Disposable income calculations reveal a purchasing power index of 69,609.5, which translates to roughly $69,610 of real purchasing capacity per household when national price levels are used as a benchmark. Subtracting the median rent of $793 (≈$9,516 annually) from the median income leaves $53,056 of pre‑tax earnings for all other expenses. After accounting for the lower service costs, a typical household can stretch its budget further than a counterpart in a higher‑priced region, despite earning slightly less than the national median. Demographically, the county’s labor market shapes this affordability. An unemployment rate of 6.1 % exceeds the national average, suggesting that job security is modest, but the low educational attainment—only 9.9 % hold a bachelor’s degree or higher—correlates with the prevalence of lower‑wage occupations that keep price pressures subdued. The median age of 42.9 years points to a mature population, likely with established consumption patterns focused on essential goods and services rather than high‑frequency discretionary spending. These factors collectively temper demand for premium services, reinforcing the low service price index. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped from 90.433 to 89.89, a 0.6 % decline, indicating a slight easing of price pressures. Median household income surged 41.4 % from $44,243 to $62,572, outpacing the 25.9 % rise in median rent (from $630 to $793). Real wages have therefore improved relative to housing costs, enhancing net purchasing power. The modest reduction in the overall price index, coupled with robust income growth, suggests that residents have experienced a tangible increase in disposable resources. Prospective residents should budget around $793 per month for rent, allocate roughly 15 % of income to housing, and anticipate lower-than‑average expenditures on utilities, healthcare, and dining due to the 62.9 service index. Grocery and fuel bills will be only slightly below national levels, so modest savings can be expected there. With a median disposable income of about $53,000 after rent, households can comfortably cover essential expenses and retain a meaningful cushion for savings or discretionary items, provided they navigate the local labor market’s limited high‑skill opportunities.

What this means for residents of Garrard County, Kentucky

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
