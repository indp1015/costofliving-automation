📊 Local Market Analysis

Cheyenne County residents operate within a cost structure that sits 10.1 percentage points below the national average, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity for 2023 registering 89.9 against a baseline of 100. This aggregate figure masks significant internal variation: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel run at 96.5, nearly matching national pricing, while services — healthcare, utilities, and dining — collapse to 68.6, the lowest component by a wide margin. Rental costs settle at 90.8, moderately discounted but not as deeply as the services basket. The goods category effectively anchors the local cost floor; households cannot easily substitute away from food and fuel, meaning the 3.5 percent discount on daily necessities offers limited relief compared to the 31.4 percent services discount that reshapes discretionary spending capacity. Purchasing power data reveals the practical consequence of this structure. The median household income of $55,765 translates to a purchasing power index of 62,024.5, implying the median dollar stretches roughly 11.2 percent further than its nominal value suggests. Rent burden stands at a remarkably low 15.1 percent of income, with median monthly rent of $703 comparing favorably to the national median of $928. However, the local labor market context complicates the picture: unemployment sits at 1.8 percent, signaling extreme labor tightness, yet only 15.8 percent of adults hold a bachelor's degree or higher and the median age of 39.8 skews older than many growth corridors. This suggests a workforce concentrated in occupations that may not command the national median income of $65,047, capping absolute earnings even as low costs amplify their reach. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP declined from 92.991 to 89.908, a 3.3 percent drop in relative price levels, while median income rose 25.3 percent from $44,500 to $55,765 and median rent increased 24.6 percent from $564 to $703. Wage growth has modestly outpaced rent growth, improving the rent-to-income ratio over the decade, but the near-parity between income and rent appreciation rates indicates that housing cost pressure has not dissipated. The declining RPP reflects a widening gap between local service costs and national inflation, particularly in healthcare and utilities, rather than a broad-based deflationary trend. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that mirror national averages, meaning a household spending $800 monthly on food and transportation nationally should expect roughly $772 here. The substantial savings emerge in services: a $300 utility bill nationally might run closer to $206, and healthcare out-of-pocket costs likely track similarly lower. Rent for a typical unit consumes roughly one-sixth of gross income, leaving unusual headroom for savings or debt service. The critical variable remains earned income; with local wages trailing the national median by 14.3 percent, the purchasing power advantage functions as a partial offset rather than a surplus generator. Households relocating from higher-wage metros should model post-tax income against the 96.5 goods index to avoid underestimating the fixed-cost floor.

What this means for residents of Cheyenne County, Kansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 62024.5, a median household income of $55,765 in Cheyenne County, Kansas has the equivalent buying power of $34,587,962 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 90.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
