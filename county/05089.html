📊 Local Market Analysis

Marion County residents navigate a cost structure that sits 13.2 percent below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 86.8 in 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals distinct pressure points for household budgets. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel — represent the highest cost component at 93.1, only marginally cheaper than the national baseline, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 56.7, the lowest segment by a wide margin. Rental costs sit at 81.9, providing meaningful shelter savings relative to the national median rent of $928, with the local median settling at $792. This bifurcation creates a daily experience where filling a cart at the supermarket or a tank at the pump feels nearly as expensive as in a major metro, but a visit to the doctor or a restaurant meal carries a steep discount. The median household income of $46,953 trails the national figure of $65,047 by 27.8 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 54,087.1 indicates that the lower price level partially offsets the earnings gap, effectively boosting real income by roughly 15 percent above the nominal wage. Rent burden stands at a manageable 20.2 percent of gross income, well below the 30 percent stress threshold, suggesting housing costs are not the primary driver of financial fragility. Instead, the goods-heavy consumption basket — where prices are 93.1 percent of national levels — consumes a disproportionate share of take-home pay for a population with a median age of 51.9 years, likely facing elevated healthcare utilization despite the cheap services index. Demographic and labor market dynamics sharpen the affordability picture. An unemployment rate of 6.5 percent signals slack in the local labor market, while only 9.1 percent of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, constraining upward wage mobility and locking a significant portion of the workforce into lower-wage service or goods-producing sectors. Over the 2010–2023 period, nominal median income rose 37.3 percent, from $34,195 to $46,953, but median rent accelerated faster at 43.2 percent, climbing from $553 to $792. The overall RPP drifted downward from 88.6 to 86.8, a modest 2.0 percent decline, meaning the real income gains were largely nominal rather than driven by deepening deflation in local consumer prices. A prospective resident should budget for a "high floor" on daily essentials: grocery bills and transportation costs will track close to national averages, offering little relief compared to larger urban centers where discount retailers compete aggressively. The savings materialize in discretionary services — dining out, personal care, and utility bills — where the 56.7 index translates to tangible monthly surplus potential. However, the combination of an aging population, elevated unemployment, and low educational attainment suggests limited local bargaining power for wage growth, meaning any future inflation in the goods category would erode purchasing power faster than in regions with tighter labor markets and higher skill premiums.

What this means for residents of Marion County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 54087.1, a median household income of $46,953 in Marion County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $25,395,516 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
