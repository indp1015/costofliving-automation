📊 Local Market Analysis

Jerome County’s cost‑of‑living index sits at 92.2, meaning consumer prices are roughly 8 percent below the national benchmark of 100. Goods prices, measured at 95.9, are only 4 percent under the national average, while services are markedly cheaper at 86.9, a 13 percent discount. Housing, the most influential component for household budgets, registers an index of 68.1, indicating rents are 31.9 percent lower than the national norm. The disparity between the three categories shows that, despite modestly higher grocery and apparel costs, the county’s affordability hinges on its exceptionally low rental market. The consumption structure is dominated by housing, which accounts for the greatest deviation from national levels. With median monthly rent at $862—$66 below the national median of $928—the rent‑burden metric stands at 14.9 percent of median household income, well under the 30 percent threshold that defines affordability stress. Services, encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining, are also inexpensive, reinforcing a daily‑life environment that feels cheap for most essential outlays. Goods, however, are the only segment approaching parity with national prices, suggesting that discretionary spending on items such as clothing or non‑essential groceries will not benefit from the same discount. Overall, a resident’s budget is weighted heavily toward savings on shelter and routine services, while the cost of consumables remains near‑average. Median household income in Jerome County is $69,338, 6.4 percent higher than the national median of $65,047. After subtracting the average rent of $862, a typical household retains $68,476 in annual disposable income, or roughly $5,706 per month. The Purchasing Power Index of 75,174.6 quantifies this advantage: residents can purchase about 75 percent of the goods and services that a national median‑income household can buy with the same nominal dollars, reflecting the combined effect of higher earnings and lower housing costs. The modest rent‑burden further amplifies disposable income, leaving most households with ample margin for savings or non‑essential consumption. Demographically, the county’s labor force is young (median age 33.6) and relatively unskilled, with only 9 percent holding a bachelor’s degree or higher. Unemployment is low at 3.8 percent, indicating a tight labor market that supports steady wage growth despite limited educational attainment. The youthful profile tends to drive demand for rental housing rather than homeownership, reinforcing the low‑rent environment. Limited higher‑education attainment may constrain upward mobility, but the low cost of essential services mitigates pressure on disposable income for this cohort. Over the past decade, the Regional Price Parities index slipped from 93.2 to 92.2, a 1 percent decline, while median household income surged 66.6 percent—from $41,630 to $69,338. Rental rates rose 21.2 percent, from $711 to $862, outpacing inflation but lagging far behind income growth. The gap between wage growth and price increases suggests that residents have experienced a real rise in purchasing power, with earnings outstripping the modest uptick in living costs. Prospective residents should allocate roughly 15 percent of gross income to rent, leaving over 80 percent for utilities, groceries, transportation, and discretionary spending. Given the near‑national cost of goods, budgeting for food and apparel should follow typical national guidelines, while utilities and healthcare will likely be cheaper than average. A prudent monthly budget might earmark $300 for utilities, $400 for groceries, $200 for transportation, and $250 for discretionary items, preserving a comfortable cushion for savings or emergencies.

What this means for residents of Jerome County, Idaho

With a Purchasing Power Index of 75174.6, a median household income of $69,338 in Jerome County, Idaho has the equivalent buying power of $52,124,564 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 68.1 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
