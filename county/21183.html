📊 Local Market Analysis

Ohio County presents a cost-of-living profile that sits 10.1 percentage points below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 89.9 in 2023. This aggregate figure masks a sharp bifurcation in consumer pricing: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel run only modestly discounted at 94.5, while services—encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining—plummet to 62.9, the lowest component in the local basket. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 80.9, translating to a median monthly outlay of $774 against a national benchmark of $928. For a household navigating daily expenses, the data suggests that discretionary spending on tangible goods offers limited arbitrage, whereas the service-intensive portions of the budget—medical visits, electricity, and restaurant meals—deliver substantial relief relative to metropolitan counterparts. The consumption breakdown reveals that goods act as the primary anchor preventing the overall index from falling further, effectively setting a floor for daily expenditures. Because the goods index hovers near parity with the nation at 94.5, residents cannot easily substitute away from these essentials; a trip to the supermarket or a fill-up at the pump costs nearly as much as it does in higher-wage regions. Conversely, the deeply discounted services index of 62.9 functions as the primary driver of affordability, suppressing the composite RPP significantly. This structure creates a peculiar budgeting dynamic: fixed costs for shelter and utilities are manageable—the median rent consumes only 17.1 percent of the $54,369 median household income—but variable costs for food and fuel exert disproportionate pressure on disposable income because they lack the deep discount found in services. Purchasing power metrics confirm that the median worker retains considerable real income after local price adjustments. The Purchasing Power Index of 60,483.9 exceeds the nominal median income by roughly $6,100, indicating that a dollar earned in Ohio County stretches further than the national average dollar. However, this advantage is tempered by an income gap: local median earnings trail the national median of $65,047 by over $10,600. The labor market context sharpens this picture. An unemployment rate of 5.2 percent sits above typical full-employment thresholds, while educational attainment remains low—only 7.7 percent of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher—and the median age of 40.9 suggests a workforce skewed toward established, potentially lower-mobility occupations. These demographics imply that the low cost of services may reflect limited local demand density as much as efficient delivery, and the wage ceiling remains constrained by the skill composition of the labor pool. Over the past decade, the affordability calculus has shifted against the resident. The RPP has remained remarkably stable, edging down only 0.6 percent from 90.433 in 2010 to 89.89 in 2023, signaling that consumer prices have not meaningfully decoupled from national trends. Meanwhile, nominal median income rose 35.3 percent, from $40,189 to $54,369, yet median rent accelerated faster at 45.2 percent, climbing from $533 to $774. This divergence means shelter costs have claimed a growing slice of the paycheck despite the low overall rent burden ratio. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that mirror national levels, anticipate healthcare and utility costs that run roughly 37 percent below average, and recognize that wage growth, while positive, has not fully insulated households from rental inflation. The budget reality is one of low fixed overhead but high variable friction, where the savings on a doctor’s visit are partially offset by the price at the pump.

What this means for residents of Ohio County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 60483.9, a median household income of $54,369 in Ohio County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $32,884,492 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
