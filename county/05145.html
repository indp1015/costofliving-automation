📊 Local Market Analysis

White County, Arkansas operates with a cost of living 13.2 percent below the national baseline, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 86.8 in 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals a distorted consumer landscape where the price of tangible goods barely budges while service costs collapse. The Goods index sits at 93.1, only 6.9 points shy of the national average, meaning residents pay near-national prices for groceries, apparel, and motor fuel despite significantly lower local wages. Conversely, the Services index plunges to 56.7 — a staggering 43.3 percent below the national level — driven by deeply discounted healthcare, utilities, and dining expenses. Rental costs, indexed at 81.9, occupy the middle ground, tracking closer to goods than services but still delivering an 18.1 percent shelter discount relative to the country. This bifurcation dictates daily financial reality: a household budget weighted toward services — healthcare consumption, utility loads, restaurant meals — stretches considerably further here than in metropolitan areas, while a basket heavy on physical goods offers minimal arbitrage. The median household income of $53,435 trails the national median of $65,047 by 17.8 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 61,554 indicates that after adjusting for local price levels, the typical earner commands real resources roughly equivalent to a $61,554 income in an average-cost jurisdiction. Rent burden sits at a manageable 18.1 percent of gross income against a national median rent of $928, compared to the local $806, leaving disproportionate discretionary capacity for service consumption but exposing grocery and fuel budgets to near-full national price pressure. Demographic and labor market structures reinforce this dynamic. An 11.2 percent bachelor’s degree attainment rate and a 5.1 percent unemployment rate signal a labor market anchored in lower-wage service and goods-producing sectors rather than knowledge-intensive industries, capping wage growth potential. The median age of 38.4 suggests a workforce in prime earning years, yet the educational profile limits upward mobility into higher-paying remote or professional roles that could exploit the local cost advantage. This composition likely sustains the low service-cost environment — labor-intensive services remain cheap because local wage floors are low — while goods prices remain tethered to national supply chains indifferent to local income levels. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the RPP declined marginally from 88.6 to 86.8, a 2.0 percent drop in relative price levels, while nominal median income rose 25.6 percent from $42,554 and median rent increased 25.7 percent from $641. Wages and rents have effectively marched in lockstep, preserving the rent-to-income ratio but failing to widen the real income gap. The modest RPP improvement suggests goods and services inflation ran slightly cooler here than nationally, yet the near-identical growth rates for income and shelter costs indicate that housing affordability has not materially improved in over a decade. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that mirror national averages, anticipate significantly lower outlays for medical visits, electricity, and dining, and recognize that rental savings versus the national median amount to roughly $1,464 annually — a meaningful but not transformative buffer against a goods budget that refuses to discount.

What this means for residents of White County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 61554.0, a median household income of $53,435 in White County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $32,891,380 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
