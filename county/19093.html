📊 Local Market Analysis

Ida County registers an overall Regional Price Parity of 88.8, placing local consumer prices 11.2 percent below the national benchmark, yet the composition of that discount reveals a distorted affordability picture. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — sit at 96.6, merely 3.4 points off the U.S. average, while services including healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 66.0, a 34-point gap that ranks among the steepest service-sector discounts recorded in the Midwest. Rental costs, indexed at 87.3, track closer to goods than to services, suggesting that shelter, while cheap relative to coastal metros, does not drive the county's low headline figure. Daily life is inexpensive primarily because labor-intensive services — haircuts, restaurant meals, in-home care — are priced at roughly two-thirds the national level, a dynamic that benefits retirees and remote workers but offers less relief to households spending heavily on tradable goods like vehicles, pharmaceuticals, or electronics that follow national pricing. The median household income of $62,841 trails the national median of $65,047 by 3.4 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 70,792 — derived by adjusting nominal income for the 88.8 RPP — indicates that the typical resident commands real purchasing capacity roughly 8.8 percent above the national average after local prices are accounted for. Rent burden stands at a remarkably low 12.8 percent of gross income, with median monthly rent of $669 compared to $928 nationally, freeing substantial cash flow for savings, transportation, or discretionary spending. The labor market reinforces this cushion: unemployment sits at 1.9 percent, signaling tight employer competition that historically supports wage growth, while a median age of 42.3 and a bachelor's attainment rate of 16.2 percent reflect an older, less credentialed workforce that may limit upward mobility in high-skill sectors but stabilizes demand for essential services. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP declined from 91.1 to 88.8, a 2.6 percent drop in relative price levels, while nominal median income surged 33.7 percent from $46,993 to $62,841. Median rent, however, accelerated 61.6 percent from $414 to $669, outpacing income growth by nearly a factor of two. This divergence means that while the county has grown cheaper relative to the nation overall, the rental component — critical for younger households and newcomers — has eroded a significant share of the real income gains. The goods index at 96.6 implies that national inflation in food and energy transmits almost fully to local budgets, whereas the services deflation has acted as a partial offset. A prospective resident should budget for near-national prices at the grocery store and gas pump, with a typical weekly food-and-fuel bill for a two-adult household likely exceeding $180–$220 depending on commute distance. Healthcare and utility outlays will run substantially below urban benchmarks, but access to specialists may require travel to Sioux City or Omaha, adding time and transportation costs. Renters capture the clearest advantage: a one-bedroom unit at the $669 median consumes roughly one-eighth of gross income, leaving latitude for student debt repayment or retirement contributions. Homebuyers face a different calculus not captured in the rental index, though the low service-cost environment reduces ongoing carrying costs for insurance, maintenance labor, and property services.

What this means for residents of Ida County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 70791.6, a median household income of $62,841 in Ida County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $44,486,149 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
