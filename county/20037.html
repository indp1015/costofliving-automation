📊 Local Market Analysis

Crawford County presents a cost of living profile significantly below the national baseline, registering an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 89.9 against the national 100, a discount driven primarily by a services sector index of just 68.6 — the lowest component in the local basket. Goods, encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel, sit much closer to parity at 96.5, while rental costs index at 90.8, suggesting that while shelter is affordable relative to the country, the daily consumables basket offers less relative savings than the headline figure implies. This composition creates a distinct bifurcation: residents capture substantial value on healthcare, utilities, and dining out, but pay near-national rates at the grocery store and gas pump, making the "cheapness" of daily life highly dependent on consumption habits. The purchasing power calculus reveals a meaningful gap between nominal earnings and real command over resources. Median household income stands at $50,311, roughly 22.6 percent below the national median of $65,047, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 55,958.3 indicates that local wages stretch approximately 11.2 percent further than the raw dollar figure suggests. Rent burden sits at a manageable 20.0 percent of gross income — median rent of $837 compares favorably to the $928 national median — leaving a larger residual for discretionary spending or savings than the income deficit alone would signal. However, the low educational attainment rate of 20.3 percent holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, paired with a young median age of 33.8, suggests a labor market concentrated in sectors where wage growth may lag productivity gains, a dynamic reinforced by a low 3.5 percent unemployment rate that has not translated into income parity. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the county’s cost structure has become incrementally more advantageous, with the overall RPP declining 3.3 percent from 92.991 to 89.908. During that same window, median income surged 37.7 percent from $36,534 to $50,311, outpacing a 24.4 percent rise in median rent from $673 to $837. This divergence indicates that real wage growth has been robust, expanding the buffer between housing costs and take-home pay. The decline in the RPP was likely propelled by the services component, which historically weighs heavily on healthcare and utility pricing, areas where rural Kansas often benefits from lower overhead structures compared to metropolitan centers. For a household budgeting for relocation, the critical variables are the goods floor and the services ceiling. A family allocating a standard share of expenditure to groceries and transportation will experience only a modest 3.5 percent discount versus the national average, meaning weekly supermarket trips and commuting costs feel familiar. Conversely, line items for electricity, internet, medical visits, and restaurant meals carry a steep 31.4 percent discount, effectively subsidizing a higher quality of life on a lower nominal salary. The demographic profile — young, working, with limited advanced credentialing — implies a community where dual-income households are common necessities rather than luxuries, and where the margin for absorbing goods-price inflation remains thinner than the headline affordability metrics suggest.

What this means for residents of Crawford County, Kansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 55958.3, a median household income of $50,311 in Crawford County, Kansas has the equivalent buying power of $28,153,180 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 90.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
