📊 Local Market Analysis

The cost of living in Graham County sits well below the national norm, with the overall Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 89.9 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices are the most elevated component of the local basket, registering an index of 96.5, still modestly under the national benchmark but markedly higher than the county’s service and housing costs. Services are the cheapest segment at 68.6, indicating that healthcare, utilities, and dining out are roughly 31 % cheaper than the average American experience. Rental costs, measured by the housing RPP of 90.8, are also below the national level, translating into a median monthly rent of $726—22 % less than the national median of $928. The combined effect is a cost structure that leans heavily on inexpensive services while goods remain the primary price driver, suggesting that everyday necessities such as groceries and fuel are relatively pricier than local services, yet the overall expense picture remains favorable compared with most of the country. Disposable income calculations reveal that the median household in Graham County earns $52,909 annually, which is 19 % lower than the national median of $65,047. After accounting for the median rent of $726, rent consumes 16.5 % of household income, well under the 30 % threshold commonly used to flag rent burden. Subtracting rent leaves roughly $46,200 for all other expenses, a figure that aligns with the Purchasing Power Index of 58,847.9. This index, expressed in dollars, indicates that a resident’s purchasing power is about 41 % weaker than the national average (which would be 100,000 in index terms). Consequently, while housing costs are affordable, the higher relative price of goods erodes net disposable income, leaving less room for discretionary spending after covering essentials. Demographic characteristics shape this affordability landscape. The unemployment rate of 4.2 % mirrors the national average, suggesting a stable labor market, yet only 18.7 % of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, well below the national proportion. The median age of 46.1 years points to an older population, which typically spends a larger share of income on healthcare and fixed costs. The modest educational attainment may limit access to higher‑paying occupations, reinforcing the lower median income figure. Nonetheless, the low service cost index benefits older households that allocate a significant portion of budgets to medical and utility expenses, cushioning the impact of limited earnings. Over the past decade, the RPP fell from 92.991 in 2010 to 89.908 in 2023, a 3.3 % decline, indicating that consumer prices have softened relative to the nation. In the same period, median household income rose from $43,826 to $52,909, a 20.7 % increase, while median rent jumped from $474 to $726, a 53.2 % surge. Wage growth outpaced the modest decline in overall price levels but lagged behind the sharp rise in rental costs, meaning that housing has become a more significant expense for residents despite the lower rent‑burden percentage. The divergence suggests that while residents enjoy cheaper services, the escalating cost of housing and relatively high goods prices temper the gains from income growth. Prospective residents should anchor their budgets around three pillars: housing, goods, and services. With rent at $726, allocating roughly 15‑20 % of income to housing remains realistic. Grocery and fuel expenditures will dominate the remaining budget, given the 96.5 goods index; budgeting an extra 5‑10 % above national averages for these categories is prudent. The low service index offers relief on utilities, medical bills, and dining, allowing households to allocate a larger share of their disposable income to savings or transportation. A realistic monthly budget for a median‑income household would therefore consist of $726 for rent, $500‑$600 for groceries and fuel, $300 for utilities and healthcare, and the balance for transportation, insurance, and discretionary items. Understanding these allocations equips newcomers to navigate Graham County’s unique cost profile without overextending financially.

What this means for residents of Graham County, Kansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 58847.9, a median household income of $52,909 in Graham County, Kansas has the equivalent buying power of $31,135,835 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 90.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
