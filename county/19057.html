📊 Local Market Analysis

Des Moines County presents a cost of living profile significantly below the national baseline, registering an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 88.8 against the national 100. This 11.2 percent discount is driven almost entirely by a services index of 66.0, a figure 34 points below the national average that reflects substantially lower local prices for healthcare, utilities, and dining. Goods, encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel, sit much closer to parity at 96.6, while rental costs track at 87.3. The disparity between the goods and services indices creates a distinct consumption dynamic: residents pay near-national rates for tangible daily necessities but capture deep savings on recurring service obligations, making the overall basket of consumer expenditures notably cheaper than the typical U.S. metro area. The median household income of $60,662 lags the national median of $65,047 by roughly 6.7 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 68,337 indicates that local wages stretch considerably further once the low price level is factored in. Housing affordability underscores this advantage; median monthly rent of $908 consumes only 18.0 percent of gross household income, a burden significantly lighter than the national standard where the $928 median rent claims a larger share of a higher income. This gap between nominal income and real purchasing power defines the local household budget: the median worker retains substantial disposable income after covering shelter, a direct result of rental costs running 12.7 percent below the national average while incomes trail by only 6.7 percent. Demographic and labor market conditions reinforce this affordability narrative. An unemployment rate of 3.2 percent signals a tight labor market that supports income stability, while a median age of 42.2 years suggests a workforce in its prime earning years. However, the relatively low educational attainment—only 15.8 percent of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher—correlates with the moderate nominal wage levels and likely anchors the local economy in sectors where the low services RPP provides maximum relative benefit. The labor market appears sufficient to sustain current income levels without generating the wage inflation that typically erodes purchasing power in higher-cost regions. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the RPP declined 2.6 percent from 91.1 to 88.8, meaning the county became incrementally cheaper relative to the nation even as nominal costs rose. Median income grew 36.6 percent, from $44,423 to $60,662, while median rent increased 42.5 percent, from $637 to $908. Although rent appreciation outpaced income growth on a percentage basis, the absolute dollar gap widened in favor of the household budget, and the falling RPP confirms that broader consumer price inflation remained muted compared to national trends. For a prospective resident, the budgeting implication is clear: allocate near-national budgets for groceries and fuel, but expect materially lower outlays for healthcare, utilities, and restaurant meals, resulting in a household ledger where shelter costs remain a minor line item rather than a dominant constraint.

What this means for residents of Des Moines County, Iowa

With a Purchasing Power Index of 68336.9, a median household income of $60,662 in Des Moines County, Iowa has the equivalent buying power of $41,454,530 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 87.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
