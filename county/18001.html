📊 Local Market Analysis

Adams County presents a cost of living profile that sits 7.9 percent below the national baseline, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 92.1 for 2023. This aggregate figure masks significant internal divergence: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel track closely to the national average at 95.6, while rental costs come in at a 15.3 percent discount with an index of 84.7. The dominant deflationary force is the services sector—encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining—which clocks in at a striking 71.3, nearly 29 percent below the U.S. mean. For a household managing daily cash flow, this structure means the recurring, non-discretionary outlays for shelter and services are substantially cheaper than in most metros, though the weekly grocery bill or fuel stop offers little relative savings. The consumption breakdown reveals that daily life is inexpensive primarily because the service infrastructure—medical visits, utility hookups, restaurant meals—carries a steep discount relative to the rest of the country. Goods act as the anchor preventing the overall index from falling further; at 95.6, they represent the highest cost component and the closest tether to national pricing dynamics. Consequently, a resident’s marginal dollar stretches furthest on rent and healthcare, while gains at the supermarket or gas pump are marginal. The median monthly rent of $799 consumes only 15.2 percent of the median household income of $63,128, a rent-burden ratio that is exceptionally low compared to the national median rent of $928 against a national median income of $65,047. This dynamic fuels a Purchasing Power Index of 68,545.1, indicating that after adjusting for local price levels, the median household commands real resources roughly 8.4 percent above their nominal earnings, a tangible buffer for discretionary spending or savings. Local demographics sharpen this advantage. An unemployment rate of 2.7 percent signals a tight labor market where workers possess leverage, yet educational attainment remains modest with only 10.9 percent of adults holding a bachelor’s degree or higher. The median age of 33.2 suggests a workforce in its prime earning years, likely concentrated in sectors that do not require advanced credentials but benefit from the county’s low overhead. This composition supports the current income level but caps upward mobility; the labor market appears efficient at deploying available labor into roles that sustain the median income, yet the low degree density implies a ceiling on wage growth that could pressure purchasing power if goods inflation accelerates. Over the past decade, the RPP has drifted marginally lower, moving from 93.0 to 92.1, a modest 1.0 percent decline in relative price levels. Nominal median income surged 31.0 percent, rising from $48,188 to $63,128, yet median rent accelerated faster at 46.6 percent, climbing from $545 to $799. While wages have outpaced the general cost of living, the rent component has eroded a portion of that real gain, nudging the rent-to-income ratio upward from a historical low. A prospective resident should budget for a goods basket that feels nationally priced—expecting no discount on food or fuel—while allocating significantly less for housing and healthcare than national averages suggest. The critical variable remains the services index; any structural shift in local healthcare delivery or utility regulation that pushes that 71.3 figure toward the mean would rapidly compress the current purchasing power surplus.

What this means for residents of Adams County, Indiana

With a Purchasing Power Index of 68545.1, a median household income of $63,128 in Adams County, Indiana has the equivalent buying power of $43,271,151 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 84.7 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
