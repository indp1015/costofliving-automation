📊 Local Market Analysis

Custer County’s cost of living sits well below the national benchmark, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 92.2 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices are the most elevated component at 95.9, still modestly under the national level, while services register at 86.9 and rental costs plunge to 68.1, indicating that housing is dramatically cheaper than the countrywide average. The median monthly rent of $744 contrasts sharply with the national median of $928, a 19.8% discount that directly lowers the overall expense profile for households. The structure of local consumption is dominated by the low housing cost. Rental expenses account for only 68.1% of the national price level, pulling the composite index down despite goods prices hovering near parity. Daily life for most residents is therefore inexpensive in the housing dimension, while groceries, apparel, and fuel remain close to what consumers pay elsewhere. Utilities and healthcare, grouped under services, are 13.1% cheaper than the national average, reinforcing the affordability of routine out‑of‑pocket spending. Consequently, a typical household can allocate a smaller share of income to shelter and retain more for discretionary or essential goods, even though the price of those goods is not dramatically lower. Median household income in Custer County stands at $56,957, 12.5% below the national median of $65,047. After subtracting the median rent of $744, a household retains roughly $52,213 annually for all other expenses, equating to a disposable‑income‑to‑price ratio captured by the Purchasing Power Index of 61,751.4. This figure translates to about 88% of the national purchasing power, reflecting that lower housing costs partially offset the income gap. The rent burden of 15.7% of income underscores the modest strain of housing costs; by comparison, the national rent‑to‑income ratio typically hovers near 30%, confirming that Custer County residents devote a relatively small slice of earnings to shelter. Demographically, the county skews older, with a median age of 59.5 years, and only 21.3% of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher. The labor market is tight, evidenced by a 2.1% unemployment rate, well under the national average. An older, less‑educated population often correlates with lower wage growth and a higher reliance on fixed incomes, which can magnify the impact of any price increases in essential goods. Nevertheless, the low unemployment rate suggests stable employment for those in the labor force, supporting the modest but steady income base that underpins the county’s affordability. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped from 93.2 in 2010 to 92.2 in 2023, a 1.0% decline, indicating that consumer prices have softened relative to the nation. Median household income surged 44.4% from $39,457 to $56,957, outpacing the 26.1% rise in median rent (from $590 to $744). The disparity between income growth and rent inflation demonstrates that wages have more than kept pace with housing costs, reinforcing the county’s favorable cost‑of‑living dynamics. Goods and services have remained broadly stable, preserving the modest price advantage. Prospective residents should budget with housing as the primary savings lever: allocating roughly 15–16% of income to rent leaves ample room for groceries, transportation, and healthcare, which together cost near national levels. A realistic monthly expense plan might assign $300–$350 to utilities and health services, $400–$500 to food and personal items, and the remainder to discretionary spending or savings. The low rent burden, combined with a stable labor market and modest price growth, positions Custer County as a locale where households can stretch median earnings further than in most parts of the country, provided they account for the higher relative cost of goods and the demographic tendency toward fixed‑income households.

What this means for residents of Custer County, Idaho

With a Purchasing Power Index of 61751.4, a median household income of $56,957 in Custer County, Idaho has the equivalent buying power of $35,171,745 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 68.1 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
