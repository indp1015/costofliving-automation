📊 Local Market Analysis

Clay County registers an overall Regional Price Parity of 86.8, placing local consumer prices 13.2 percent below the national baseline, yet this aggregate figure masks a severe structural distortion in the household budget. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — carry an index of 93.1, merely 6.9 percent off the national pace, while Services collapse to 56.7, a staggering 43.3 percent discount driven by depressed healthcare, utility, and dining rates. Rental costs sit at 81.9, roughly 18 percent cheaper than the U.S. average. The highest cost component is unequivocally Goods; residents pay near-national prices for daily consumables while capturing deep discounts on services that are often non-discretionary or infrequently purchased, creating a "cheap services, expensive necessities" dynamic that compresses discretionary flexibility for the median earner. Purchasing power metrics reveal the practical consequence of this structure. The median household income of $48,500 translates to a Purchasing Power Index of 55,869, implying the average dollar stretches roughly 15 percent further than in a median-cost metro, yet this gain is almost entirely theoretical for renters. With median monthly rent at $696, the rent burden rests at a manageable 17.2 percent of gross income, significantly below the national median rent of $928 and the 30-percent stress threshold. However, the 93.1 Goods index means the weekly grocery bill and fuel tank refill consume a disproportionate share of the remaining 82.8 percent of income. A household earning the local median retains roughly $40,100 annually after rent, but faces national-level pricing for the bulk of variable consumption, effectively neutralizing the rental savings for families with high transportation or nutritional needs. Demographic and labor indicators sharpen the affordability picture. An unemployment rate of 6.3 percent sits well above the national norm, signaling slack in the labor market that suppresses wage growth, while a bachelor’s attainment rate of just 9.4 percent — less than half the national average — anchors the workforce in lower-wage service and manual sectors. The median age of 42.6 years suggests a stable, older population with fixed healthcare demands, a category where the 56.7 Services index provides genuine relief, but offers little comfort to younger workers or families facing the 93.1 Goods penalty. The labor market suggests limited upward mobility; without a shift in educational attainment or industrial mix, income growth will likely struggle to outpace the sticky cost of goods. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP drifted downward from 88.6 to 86.8, a modest 2.0 percent decline in relative price levels, while median income surged 49.0 percent from $32,552 to $48,500 and median rent climbed 36.7 percent from $509 to $696. Wages have demonstrably outpaced both shelter and the broader cost basket over the decade, delivering real gains in purchasing power. However, the velocity of rent inflation — nearly three-quarters the pace of income growth — signals that the primary affordability advantage is eroding. A prospective resident should budget for near-national grocery and fuel receipts, anticipate limited high-wage employment options, and recognize that while the rent check remains light, the checkout lane offers no local discount.

What this means for residents of Clay County, Arkansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 55869.1, a median household income of $48,500 in Clay County, Arkansas has the equivalent buying power of $27,096,514 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 81.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
