📊 Local Market Analysis

Lawrence County registers an overall Regional Price Parity of 89.9, placing local consumer prices roughly ten percent below the national baseline, yet this aggregate figure masks a sharp bifurcation in the household budget. Goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel—carry an index of 94.5, only marginally cheaper than the U.S. average, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining collapse to 62.9, the lowest component by a wide margin. Rental costs sit at 80.9, offering meaningful shelter savings relative to the national median rent of $928, though the local median of $729 still commands 19.2 percent of the median household income of $45,584. This structure makes daily life inexpensive for fixed service obligations but leaves residents exposed to near-national pricing on recurring necessities like food and gasoline, a dynamic that compresses discretionary spending for the 6.0 percent of the labor force currently unemployed. The consumption breakdown reveals that goods, not housing or services, drive the effective cost structure for the typical household. Because services are deeply discounted—likely reflecting lower labor costs and provider density in a county where only 8.3 percent of adults hold a bachelor’s degree and the median age is 42—households gain purchasing power on healthcare and utilities but cannot easily substitute away from goods priced at 94.5. The Purchasing Power Index of 50,710.9 illustrates this tension: while the nominal median income trails the national $65,047 by thirty percent, the cost adjustment recovers roughly $5,100 in real value. However, that recovery is asymmetrical; a dollar spent on rent or a doctor’s visit stretches significantly further than a dollar spent at the pump or the grocery store, forcing budgeting trade-offs that disproportionately affect lower-income cohorts. Over the past decade, the RPP has remained remarkably stable, slipping only 0.6 percent from 90.4 to 89.9, yet nominal incomes and rents have diverged sharply. Median household income rose 26.9 percent from $35,935 to $45,584, but median rent accelerated 40.5 percent from $519 to $729, outpacing wage growth by a substantial margin. This rent-to-income elasticity has pushed the shelter burden higher even as the overall price level flattened, signaling that housing affordability is deteriorating faster than the headline RPP suggests. The labor market context—marked by elevated unemployment and low educational attainment—suggests limited upward pressure on wages to close this gap, meaning the rent burden ratio of 19.2 percent could climb further without corresponding income gains. For a prospective resident, the budgeting reality centers on a low fixed-cost floor for services and shelter that is periodically punctuated by goods expenses tracking close to national averages. A household earning the local median should allocate roughly $729 for rent, expect utility and healthcare bills well below urban benchmarks, but plan grocery and fuel budgets that offer little geographic discount. The data indicates that while the county delivers a 10 percent overall cost advantage, the marginal dollar saved on services does not fully offset the marginal dollar spent on goods, particularly for families with children or long commutes. Understanding this split—cheap services, average goods, rising rents—is essential for calibrating realistic disposable income expectations in this labor market.

What this means for residents of Lawrence County, Kentucky

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
