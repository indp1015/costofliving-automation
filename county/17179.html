📊 Local Market Analysis

Tazewell County presents a cost of living profile that sits marginally below the national baseline, with an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 98.8 in 2023, yet this aggregate figure masks significant internal divergence across consumption categories. Goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—run 1.6 percent above the national average at 101.6, representing the highest cost component for local households, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining come in substantially cheaper at 92.4. The dominant deflationary force is rental housing, priced at just 80.4 on the index, nearly 20 percent below the U.S. norm and the primary driver pulling the composite index down; this dynamic renders daily life structurally affordable for renters but creates a bifurcated budget where variable essentials like food and transportation exert disproportionate pressure relative to fixed shelter costs. The median household income of $76,704 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by roughly 18 percent, and when adjusted for the local price level, the Purchasing Power Index rises to $77,673, indicating that the average earner retains a real income surplus of approximately $12,600 over the national benchmark. Rent burden sits at a remarkably low 14.0 percent of gross income—median rent is $898 versus the national $928—freeing substantial cash flow for discretionary spending or savings. However, the 3.9 percent unemployment rate and a labor force where only 19.5 percent hold a bachelor’s degree or higher suggest an economy anchored in sectors that may not fully capitalize on this purchasing power advantage, potentially limiting upward wage mobility for a workforce with a median age of 41.7 years. Over the past decade, the RPP has declined from 101.8 to 98.8, a 3.0 percent drop in relative price levels, while nominal median income surged 31.8 percent from $58,194 and median rent climbed a nearly identical 31.7 percent from $682. This synchronization between rent growth and income growth has preserved the low rent-burden ratio, but the persistent premium on goods—now 1.6 percent above national levels—means the real wage gains are partially eroded at the checkout counter. The decoupling of shelter costs from the broader inflation trajectory implies that the county’s affordability advantage is heavily contingent on the rental market remaining soft relative to national trends. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on a favorable fixed-cost baseline offset by variable expenses that track at or above national norms. The sub-80 rental index translates to immediate monthly savings of roughly $30 to $50 compared to the average U.S. renter, yet grocery and fuel bills will likely match or exceed those in higher-cost metros. Households relocating from coastal markets will experience a sharp reduction in shelter outlays, but those moving from lower-cost Midwestern regions may find the goods premium negates a portion of the expected arbitrage. The demographic profile—older, less credentialed, and tightly employed—signals a stable but static consumption environment where household budgets benefit from low overhead but remain exposed to goods inflation.

What this means for residents of Tazewell County, Illinois

With a Purchasing Power Index of 77673.4, a median household income of $76,704 in Tazewell County, Illinois has the equivalent buying power of $59,578,605 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.4 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
