📊 Local Market Analysis

Adams County’s cost of living is markedly below the national benchmark, with an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) index of 92.2—meaning consumer prices are roughly 8 percent cheaper than the U.S. average of 100. Within this aggregate, goods (groceries, apparel, fuel) sit at 95.9, services (healthcare, utilities, dining) at 86.9, and rental costs at a pronounced 68.1. The divergence is significant: while everyday items are near‑neutral, housing is the most affordable component, and services are the cheapest of the three categories. The local cost structure is driven primarily by goods, which sit just under the national average and represent the highest index among the three buckets. Services, at 86.9, are notably cheaper, and rental costs, at 68.1, are the lowest, reinforcing a pattern where daily expenses are modest overall. The combination of relatively higher goods pricing and substantially lower housing costs creates a mixed affordability picture: households benefit from cheap shelter but must contend with near‑average spending on essentials like food and fuel. This balance makes everyday life inexpensive for many, though the goods component prevents the county from being uniformly low‑cost. Median household income of $59,286 falls short of the national median of $65,047, yet the rent burden remains modest at 16.7 percent—$823 per month for the typical renter. The Purchasing Power Index of 64,276.4 reflects the relative purchasing ability after accounting for local price levels, suggesting that despite a lower nominal income, residents retain a reasonable share of earnings for discretionary spending. The index underscores that the lower cost of housing and services amplifies disposable income, even when wages are below the national norm. Local demographics shape this economic environment. Only 13.5 percent of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, and the median age is 54.3 years, indicating an older, less‑educated population that tends to favor lower‑cost housing and essential services. The unemployment rate of 6.6 percent is slightly above the national average, which may exert modest pressure on wage growth but also supports a labor market that does not drive up living costs aggressively. These factors together produce a community where consumer prices are subdued, especially for shelter, while income levels, though below the national median, are sufficient to cover basic expenses. Over the past decade, consumer costs have softened slightly, with the RPP index edging down from 93.2 in 2010 to 92.236 in 2023—a marginal 1.0 percent decline. In contrast, median household income has risen sharply, climbing 49.8 percent from $39,565 to $59,286, while median rent has increased 43.6 percent from $573 to $823. Wages have outpaced rent growth, reinforcing the county’s affordability trajectory. For prospective residents, budgeting should emphasize the low rental burden—about one‑sixteenth of income—while allocating slightly above‑average funds for goods. Services are the cheapest category, and overall living expenses are roughly 8 percent below the national average, making Adams County a cost‑conscious choice for households prioritizing stable, affordable housing.

Analysis prepared by the Data in DP Research Team. Based on BEA RPP and Census ACS data.

What this means for residents of Adams County, Idaho

With a Purchasing Power Index of 64276.4, a median household income of $59,286 in Adams County, Idaho has the equivalent buying power of $38,106,907 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 68.1 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
