📊 Local Market Analysis

Sitka City and Borough presents a cost-of-living profile that sits modestly above the national baseline, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity (RPP) for 2023 registering 103.3 against a national index of 100. This aggregate figure masks significant internal divergence: rental housing costs run 13.3 percent above the national average at an index of 113.3, while goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel — carry a 3.7 percent premium at 103.7. Uniquely, services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining actually fall 3.3 percent below the national level at 96.7, creating a rare inversion where the service economy offers relative relief compared to the tangible costs of shelter and physical consumption. The local cost structure is unequivocally driven by housing. Median monthly rent of $1,350 exceeds the national median of $928 by 45.5 percent, yet the rent burden stands at a remarkably low 16.0 percent of the median household income of $101,207. This apparent contradiction resolves through income magnitude: local earnings are 55.6 percent higher than the national median of $65,047, granting residents substantial absolute purchasing power. The Purchasing Power Index of 97,976.7 confirms that after adjusting for the 3.3 percent overall price premium, the median household retains real income nearly 50 percent above the national median. Daily life is expensive in nominal terms — fuel and groceries carry the "Alaska premium" — but the high wage floor renders these costs manageable for the median earner. Demographic and labor market characteristics reinforce this dynamic. A median age of 39.6 suggests a workforce in prime earning years, while a bachelor’s degree attainment rate of 21.8 percent, though below national averages, aligns with an economy anchored by public administration, healthcare, and commercial fishing rather than knowledge-intensive sectors. The 7.1 percent unemployment rate signals notable labor market slack, potentially suppressing wage growth at the lower end of the distribution even as median figures remain robust. This slack may explain why the cost of services — labor-intensive by definition — runs below the national average; abundant local labor supply likely caps price growth for dining, personal care, and repair services. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP barely moved, inching up just 0.2 percent from 103.099 to 103.297, indicating remarkable price stability in the consumption basket. Meanwhile, nominal median income surged 43.8 percent from $70,376 to $101,207, far outpacing the 27.7 percent rise in median rent from $1,057 to $1,350. Real income growth has been substantial, expanding the buffer for discretionary spending. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that run roughly 4 percent above Lower 48 norms, but can anticipate healthcare and utility costs that are slightly cheaper than average. The critical variable remains housing availability: while the median rent consumes only 16 percent of the median income, competition for limited rental stock in this island community can push individual lease rates well above the median, compressing the favorable burden ratio for newcomers without established local income.

What this means for residents of Sitka City and Borough, Alaska

With a Purchasing Power Index of 97976.7, a median household income of $101,207 in Sitka City and Borough, Alaska has the equivalent buying power of $99,159,279 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 113.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
