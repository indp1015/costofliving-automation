📊 Local Market Analysis

The Dillingham Census Area’s cost of living sits modestly above the national norm, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 103.3 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices—groceries, apparel, and fuel—are 103.7, indicating a 3.7 % premium over the countrywide average, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining register a 3.3 % discount at 96.7. Housing, measured by rental costs, is the outlier: an index of 113.3 means rents are 13.3 % higher than the national benchmark, a gap that drives the overall cost structure. The median monthly rent of $1,085 exceeds the national median of $928 by 16.9 %, confirming that shelter is the primary expense pressure for households in this remote Alaskan region. Disposable income calculations reveal a nuanced picture. The median household earns $74,250, 14.2 % above the national median of $65,047, yet the rent burden stands at 17.5 % of that income—well under the 30 % threshold commonly cited as housing stress. After allocating the median rent, a typical household retains roughly $66,300 annually, or $5,525 per month, for all other expenditures. The Purchasing Power Index of 71,880.1 quantifies this residual buying capacity: residents can afford about 28 % less in goods and services than the average American, reflecting the combined effect of higher shelter costs and slightly elevated goods prices. Consequently, while earnings are relatively strong, the net purchasing power remains constrained by the housing premium. Demographic factors amplify these dynamics. With a median age of 30.9 years, the labor force skews younger, often correlating with lower household formation and higher rent‑to‑income ratios. Only 13.1 % hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting a limited pool of high‑skill, high‑wage jobs. The unemployment rate of 10.8 %—well above the national average—further depresses aggregate earnings and heightens reliance on lower‑paid occupations. This labor market profile explains why the cost advantage of cheaper services does not translate into broader affordability; limited earnings potential offsets the modest discount on non‑housing consumption. Over the past decade, the RPP index rose from 103.099 to 103.297, a marginal 0.2 % increase, indicating price stability relative to the nation. Median household income, however, surged 37.1 % from $54,173 to $74,250, outpacing the 14.2 % rise in median rent. The disparity suggests that wages have largely kept pace with, and even exceeded, housing cost growth, bolstering the ability of residents to meet basic needs despite higher rents. Prospective residents should budget for a rent bill that consumes roughly one‑sixth of household income, allocate a slightly higher share to groceries and fuel than the national average, and anticipate modest savings potential due to the 28 % shortfall in purchasing power. Emphasizing cost‑effective service choices and leveraging any available subsidies or employment programs will be essential for maintaining a balanced household budget in Dillingham.
