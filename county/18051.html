📊 Local Market Analysis

Gibson County’s cost of living sits comfortably below the national norm, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 92.1 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices are the most elevated component at 95.6, still under the national benchmark, while services are markedly cheaper at 71.3. Housing, measured by rental costs, registers at 84.7, indicating that renters pay roughly 15 % less than the average American household. The median household income of $67,763 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 4.2 %, reinforcing the county’s modest affordability advantage. The consumption structure is driven primarily by goods, which account for the highest relative price level. However, the low services index offsets the higher goods cost, producing a net environment where everyday expenses such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are substantially cheaper than elsewhere. Rental costs, though higher than the national median rent of $928, remain modest in absolute terms at $914 per month, representing only 16.2 % of median household income. This rent‑burden figure is well under the 30 % threshold commonly used to flag affordability stress, suggesting that most households retain a sizable portion of earnings for discretionary spending. Purchasing power in Gibson County is quantified by an index of 73,577.9, which translates to a real‑income advantage of roughly 13 % over the national average. After covering the median rent of $914, a household with the median income retains $66,849 annually, or $5,571 per month, for all other expenses. Subtracting typical utility and healthcare costs—both suppressed by the 71.3 services index—leaves a discretionary pool that comfortably exceeds the national average disposable income, reinforcing the county’s strong purchasing power despite the modest goods price premium. Demographically, the county’s labor market is tight, with an unemployment rate of 2.9 %, well below the national rate. The low educational attainment—only 13.5 % hold a bachelor’s degree or higher—correlates with a concentration of middle‑income, blue‑collar occupations that tend to favor lower housing and service costs. A median age of 40.8 years points to a mature workforce, likely with established household budgets and limited dependence on high‑cost childcare or student expenses, further easing the cost of living pressure. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped from 93.022 to 92.097, a 1.0 % decline, indicating that consumer prices have softened relative to the nation. In parallel, median household income rose from $48,303 to $67,763, a 40.3 % increase, while median rent climbed from $658 to $914, a 38.9 % rise. Income growth outpaced rent inflation by roughly 1.4 %, and both outpaced the modest decline in overall price levels, confirming that wages have kept pace with, and slightly exceeded, the pace of living‑cost increases. Prospective residents should budget around $914 for rent, allocate roughly 10–12 % of income to utilities and healthcare—benefiting from the low services index—and expect grocery and apparel expenses to sit near the national average. With a rent burden of only 16.2 % of income, a median household can comfortably allocate the remaining 84 % of earnings to transportation, savings, and discretionary spending, positioning Gibson County as a region where daily household budgets stretch further than in many parts of the country.

Analysis prepared by the Data in DP Research Team. Based on BEA RPP and Census ACS data.

What this means for residents of Gibson County, Indiana

With a Purchasing Power Index of 73577.9, a median household income of $67,763 in Gibson County, Indiana has the equivalent buying power of $49,858,592 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 84.7 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
