📊 Local Market Analysis

Treutlen County’s cost‑of‑living index sits at 96.5, meaning the average consumer price is 3.5 % below the national benchmark of 100. Goods are priced at 97.7, only 2.3 % under the national level, while services are markedly cheaper at 88.3, a full 11.7 % discount. Rental costs, measured by the housing component of the RPP, register at 92.3, indicating rents are 7.7 % lower than the countrywide average. These figures show a mixed picture: essential commodities are only modestly cheaper, but the biggest savings stem from services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining. The dominant driver of the county’s cost structure is the services sector, which pulls the overall index down. Daily life for residents therefore feels inexpensive in areas that rely heavily on service consumption—medical visits, utility bills, and restaurant meals are all substantially below national prices. By contrast, the goods category offers only a modest discount, so grocery and apparel budgets remain close to what a typical American household would spend elsewhere. Rental costs, while still below national averages, have risen sharply: median monthly rent climbed from $461 in 2010 to $668 in 2023, a 44.9 % increase, outpacing the modest 2.0 % rise in the overall RPP over the same period. The rent‑burden metric of 18.3 % of median household income suggests that housing remains affordable relative to income, especially when compared with the national median rent of $928. Purchasing‑power calculations reveal that the median household in Treutlen County earns $43,750, roughly 67 % of the national median income of $65,047. After accounting for the local cost environment, the Purchasing Power Index stands at 45,339.6, indicating that a typical resident can buy roughly 45 % of the national basket of goods and services with their income. Subtracting the median rent of $668 (about $8,016 annually) leaves $35,734 for all other expenses. Given the services index of 88.3, a resident’s discretionary spending on health, utilities, and dining enjoys a built‑in discount, effectively stretching the remaining income further than the raw income figure alone would suggest. Demographic and labor‑market conditions shape this affordability profile. The unemployment rate of 8.1 % exceeds the national average, constraining earnings potential and reinforcing the importance of low service costs. Only 10.3 % of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, limiting access to higher‑paying occupations that typically exist in more educated labor pools. The median age of 39.6 years points to a mature workforce, yet the combination of modest educational attainment and elevated unemployment suggests that many residents rely on lower‑wage sectors such as retail, agriculture, and local services. This labor composition explains why the county’s goods prices remain close to national levels—retail supply chains are not dramatically cheaper—while services, often subsidized by local government or lower labor costs, stay inexpensive. Over the past decade the RPP rose from 94.574 to 96.494, a 2.0 % increase, whereas median household income grew from $38,596 to $43,750, a 13.4 % gain. Income growth outpaced price inflation, enhancing real purchasing power. However, rent surged 44.9 % in the same interval, eroding part of that gain for housing‑budget households. The divergence between modest overall price growth and steep rental inflation underscores a potential squeeze on renters, even as other expenses remain relatively cheap. Prospective residents should structure their budgets around three pillars: housing, goods, and services. With median rent at $668, allocating roughly 18 % of income to housing leaves about 82 % for all other needs. Grocery and apparel bills will be close to national averages, so a $400‑$500 monthly grocery budget is realistic. The services discount allows for lower utility bills and healthcare costs, potentially reducing those line items by 10‑15 % compared with national figures. Because the labor market is characterized by higher unemployment and limited higher‑education attainment, job seekers should anticipate wages that may lag national averages, making the county’s service‑price advantage a critical buffer for household finances.

What this means for residents of Treutlen County, Georgia
