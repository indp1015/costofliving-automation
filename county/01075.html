📊 Local Market Analysis

Lamar County’s cost of living remains modestly below the national benchmark, with an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) index of 89.1—meaning consumer prices are roughly 11 percent cheaper than the U.S. average. The goods component, at 94.6, sits closest to parity, while services are markedly inexpensive at 61.6 and housing (rental costs) tracks the national level at 89.9. This divergence indicates that everyday purchases such as groceries, apparel, and fuel are the primary driver of local expenses, whereas healthcare, utilities, and dining are substantially more affordable than elsewhere. The consumption breakdown reveals that goods dominate the local cost structure. Despite the overall index suggesting a cheaper environment, the goods index of 94.6 is the highest among the three categories, implying that households allocate a larger share of their budgets to everyday items than to services. Consequently, daily life is relatively inexpensive overall, but the near‑parity pricing of goods offsets the savings generated by low‑cost services, keeping the total cost of living just under the national average. Median household income of $47,447 provides a tangible measure of purchasing power, yet it falls short of the national median of $65,047 by roughly 27 percent. The rent burden of 13.9 percent—derived from a median monthly rent of $548—demonstrates that housing consumes a modest slice of earnings, leaving considerable discretionary income. The Purchasing Power Index of 53,253.2 reflects the county’s ability to afford consumption relative to price levels, underscoring that residents retain a meaningful portion of income after essential expenses, especially when services are priced well below the national norm. Local demographics shape this economic picture. Only 6.9 percent of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, and the median age of 44.5 years points to an older, less‑educated population that typically commands lower wage rates. The unemployment rate of 6.4 percent exceeds the national average, suggesting a tighter labor market that can suppress wage growth. These factors collectively contribute to a cost structure where income growth has outpaced rent increases, even as the overall price level remains stable. Historical trends show that the RPP has edged down by 0.8 percent since 2010, moving from 89.783 to 89.097, while median income has risen 33.8 percent from $35,450 to $47,447 and median rent has climbed 28.6 percent from $426 to $548. The data indicate that wage growth has modestly outpaced housing cost inflation, helping to maintain affordability. For prospective residents, budgeting should emphasize the near‑national pricing of goods, the low cost of services, and the manageable rent burden, while recognizing that overall expenses remain slightly below the national average.

Analysis prepared by the Data in DP Research Team. Based on BEA RPP and Census ACS data.

What this means for residents of Lamar County, Alabama

With a Purchasing Power Index of 53253.2, a median household income of $47,447 in Lamar County, Alabama has the equivalent buying power of $25,267,046 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 89.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
