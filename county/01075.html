📊 Local Market Analysis

Lamar County’s cost of living sits well below the national norm, with the BEA Regional Price Parities index at 89.1 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices are the most elevated component at 94.6, still under the national average, while services are markedly cheap at 61.6. Rental costs track closely with the overall index at 89.9, translating to a median monthly rent of $548—30.9 % lower than the national median of $928. The combined effect is a household budget that demands less than a tenth of national spending on housing and roughly two‑thirds of national spending on services. The expense profile is driven primarily by the modestly higher goods index; however, the dominant savings stem from services and housing. A median household earning $47,447 faces a rent burden of only 13.9 % of income, far beneath the 30 % threshold commonly cited as housing stress. After allocating the median rent, approximately $44,351 remains for other expenditures. When adjusted for the local price environment, the Purchasing Power Index of 53,253.2 indicates that the median resident can purchase roughly 53 % of the goods and services that a median national earner can buy with the same nominal income. In practical terms, the disposable income after rent covers essential groceries, apparel, and fuel with a modest premium, while discretionary spending on dining, healthcare, and utilities enjoys a substantial discount. Demographically, the county’s labor force is older (median age 44.5) and less educated, with only 6.9 % holding a bachelor’s degree or higher. Unemployment stands at 6.4 %, modestly above the national rate, suggesting a labor market that offers limited high‑skill, high‑wage opportunities. The lower educational attainment and higher age profile depress wage growth, but they also temper demand for higher‑priced services, reinforcing the low services index. Consequently, residents experience a cost structure that reflects both limited premium consumption options and a pricing environment that rewards frugality. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped from 89.78 to 89.10, a 0.8 % decline, while median household income rose 33.8 % from $35,450 to $47,447. Rent increased 28.6 % in the same period, from $426 to $548. Income growth outpaced both the slight dip in overall price levels and the rise in housing costs, expanding real purchasing power for most households. Prospective residents should budget roughly $550 for rent, allocate about $300–$350 monthly for utilities and healthcare, and expect grocery and fuel expenses to hover near national averages adjusted downward by 5–10 %. The modest rent burden leaves ample room for savings or modest discretionary spending, but the limited local labor market and lower service prices suggest that higher‑earning households may find fewer premium consumption options than in more urbanized regions.

What this means for residents of Lamar County, Alabama

With a Purchasing Power Index of 53253.2, a median household income of $47,447 in Lamar County, Alabama has the equivalent buying power of $25,267,046 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 89.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
