📊 Local Market Analysis

Tipton County residents navigate a cost structure that sits 7.9 percent below the national average, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity index registering 92.1 for 2023 against a baseline of 100. The composition of that discount reveals an unusual inversion: services — encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining — come in at a striking 71.3, nearly twenty-nine points below the national norm, while goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel track much closer at 95.6. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 84.7, meaning housing consumes a smaller share of the budget than in most counties, yet daily consumables offer only marginal relief. The services depression drives the overall affordability narrative, suggesting that locally delivered labor-intensive categories — medical visits, haircuts, restaurant meals, and utility provision — are priced well below metropolitan benchmarks, while tradable goods remain tethered to national supply chains. That pricing dynamic translates into tangible purchasing power for the median household earning $78,309 annually, a figure that exceeds the national median of $65,047 by over twenty percent. After adjusting for the local price level, the purchasing power index rises to 85,029, indicating that a dollar earned here stretches further than in the average U.S. county. Rent burden sits at a remarkably low 13.1 percent of gross income, with median monthly rent of $854 comparing favorably against the national $928. The labor market reinforces this cushion: unemployment stands at 3.0 percent, signaling tight employment conditions that support wage stability. However, the demographic profile — median age 44.5 and only 17.1 percent of adults holding a bachelor's degree or higher — suggests an economy anchored in skilled trades, manufacturing, and service occupations rather than high-wage knowledge sectors, which may cap future income growth relative to college-dense regions. Over the past decade, the RPP has drifted downward from 93.0 to 92.1, a modest 1.0 percent decline in relative price levels, while median household income surged 51.7 percent from $51,613 to $78,309. Median rent increased just 22.7 percent over the same span, from $696 to $854, meaning housing costs have grown at less than half the pace of earnings. This divergence has expanded real disposable income substantially, a rare trajectory in an era where shelter inflation has outpaced wages in many markets. The goods index at 95.6 implies that grocery and fuel bills remain only slightly discounted, so households still feel national price pressures at the pump and checkout, but the services discount of 71.3 acts as a persistent offset on recurring monthly outlays. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and transportation costs that mirror national averages, while anticipating meaningfully lower outlays for healthcare visits, utility bills, and restaurant meals. The rent-to-income ratio of 13.1 percent leaves ample room for savings, debt service, or discretionary spending, though the modest educational attainment rate and older median age hint at limited upward mobility in high-skill wage tiers. Childcare, eldercare, and specialized medical services — categories embedded in the services index — may present availability constraints despite low listed prices. Households relocating from higher-cost metros will experience an immediate reduction in fixed overhead, particularly on shelter and services, but should not assume tradable goods discounts will deepen further.

What this means for residents of Tipton County, Indiana

With a Purchasing Power Index of 85028.8, a median household income of $78,309 in Tipton County, Indiana has the equivalent buying power of $66,585,203 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 84.7 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
