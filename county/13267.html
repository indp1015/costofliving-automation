📊 Local Market Analysis

Tattnall County’s cost of living sits modestly below the national norm, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) indicating an overall index of 96.5 against the United States benchmark of 100. Goods—groceries, apparel, and fuel—register the highest local price pressure at 97.7, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are the cheapest component at 88.3. Housing, measured by median monthly rent, is 92.3, translating to $660 versus the national median rent of $928. The combined effect is a consumer price environment that is roughly 3.5 % cheaper than the countrywide average, driven primarily by lower service costs and relatively affordable rental rates. The cost structure is anchored by the goods sector, which, despite being the most expensive of the three categories, remains under the national level. Services, at 88.3, pull the overall index down, suggesting that everyday expenditures on medical care, electricity, and restaurant meals are substantially less burdensome than in most U.S. metros. Rental costs, while higher than the county’s historical levels, still lag national rents by $268 per month, reinforcing the perception that daily life in Tattnall is inexpensive. The low rent‑burden figure—15.1 % of median household income—confirms that housing consumes a modest slice of earnings, leaving more room for discretionary spending on goods and services. Disposable income calculations underscore the county’s purchasing power advantage. Median household income stands at $52,351, 20 % below the national median of $65,047, yet the Purchasing Power Index (PPI) of 54,253.1 reflects a higher real‑term capacity to acquire goods and services because local prices are suppressed. After allocating the median rent of $660, a typical household retains roughly $46,731 in annual earnings, equivalent to $3,894 per month for non‑housing expenses. When adjusted for the 96.5 overall price index, this residual income translates to a real purchasing power that exceeds the national average, indicating that residents can stretch their dollars further despite lower nominal wages. Demographic and labor‑market characteristics shape this affordability landscape. The unemployment rate of 6.9 % exceeds the national average, hinting at a tighter labor market and potentially limiting wage growth. Educational attainment is low, with only 8.1 % holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, which correlates with the modest income level and the prevalence of lower‑paid occupations. A median age of 38.1 years suggests a balanced mix of working‑age adults and retirees, the latter often benefiting from the county’s low service costs. The combination of limited high‑skill labor supply and relatively cheap services creates a cost environment that cushions households from income volatility but also signals limited upward mobility for workers seeking higher wages. Over the past decade, the RPP rose from 94.574 in 2010 to 96.494 in 2023, a 2.0 % increase, while median household income surged 54.1 % from $33,980 to $52,351. Rental rates climbed 21.1 % in the same period, from $545 to $660. Income growth outpaced both price inflation and rent escalation, expanding real purchasing power and reinforcing the county’s affordability advantage. However, the modest rise in the overall price index suggests that cost pressures are gradually tightening, especially in the goods segment. Prospective residents should budget around $660 for rent, allocate roughly $300–$350 for utilities and basic services, and expect grocery and fuel expenses to align with the 97.7 goods index—slightly higher than the county’s average but still below national levels. With an average disposable income of $3,894 per month after rent, a prudent household can comfortably cover essential bills, maintain a modest savings rate, and afford occasional discretionary spending without exceeding the 30 % rent‑burden threshold that defines affordable housing.

What this means for residents of Tattnall County, Georgia

Because the Housing Rent index is 92.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
