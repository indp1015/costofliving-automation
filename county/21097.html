📊 Local Market Analysis

The cost of living in Harrison County sits well below the national benchmark, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 89.9 versus 100 for the United States. Goods prices are the most elevated component at 94.5, still under the national average, while services are markedly cheaper at 62.9. Housing costs, measured by median monthly rent of $755, translate to a rental‑cost index of 80.9, indicating that renters pay roughly 19 % less than the national median rent of $928. Together, these figures show a price environment that is modest overall but weighted toward slightly higher commodity costs and substantially lower service expenses. The consumption structure is driven primarily by the goods sector, which accounts for the highest relative price level in the county. Despite the modest uplift in grocery, apparel, and fuel prices, the low service index offsets much of the impact on day‑to‑day budgets. For a household earning the median income of $63,205, the rent burden is only 14.3 % of earnings, well under the 30 % threshold commonly used to flag housing stress. After allocating $755 per month for rent, a median earner retains roughly $4,400 of disposable monthly income. The Purchasing Power Index of 70,313.7—derived from the ratio of median income to the overall cost‑of‑living index—confirms that residents can purchase about 30 % more goods and services than the average American household with comparable earnings. Demographic characteristics reinforce the affordability narrative. Unemployment is a low 2.9 %, suggesting a tight labor market and stable wage prospects for the working‑age population, whose median age of 42.4 years reflects a mature, potentially dual‑income base. However, only 12 % of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, indicating limited high‑skill labor supply and possibly constraining upward wage mobility. The combination of low unemployment and modest educational attainment tends to keep wage growth modest, yet the existing income levels remain sufficient to cover the relatively cheap service and housing costs. Over the past decade, the RPP slipped from 90.433 to 89.89, a 0.6 % decline, while median household income surged 77.1 % from $35,681 to $63,205. Rental prices rose 26.5 % in the same period, from $597 to $755. The disparity between income growth and price inflation is pronounced: earnings have outpaced both overall consumer prices and housing costs, expanding real purchasing power for residents. The modest rise in the cost‑of‑living index suggests that wages have indeed kept pace, if not exceeded, the inflationary pressures affecting everyday expenses. Prospective residents should budget around $755 for rent, allocate roughly $250–$300 for utilities and health‑care services (reflecting the low service index), and plan $400–$500 for groceries and transportation, given the higher goods index. With a median disposable income of about $4,400 per month after rent, a household can comfortably cover these categories while retaining a sizable margin for discretionary spending, savings, or debt repayment. The key budgeting takeaway is that while goods are slightly pricier than the national norm, the overall affordability of services and housing creates a favorable net disposable income environment for most median earners in Harrison County.
