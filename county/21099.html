📊 Local Market Analysis

Hart County residents navigate a cost structure that sits 10.1 percent below the national average, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity for 2023 registering 89.9 against a baseline of 100. This aggregate figure masks significant internal divergence: goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel run only 5.5 percent cheaper than the national norm at 94.5, while rental housing costs 19.1 percent less at 80.9. The most striking deviation appears in services—healthcare, utilities, and dining—which plummet to 62.9, a full 37.1 points below the national average and the single largest driver of the county’s affordability advantage. For a household earning the local median income of $49,653, this service discount translates into tangible monthly relief on essentials that typically consume outsized portions of budgets in metropolitan areas. The consumption breakdown reveals that daily life is structurally inexpensive, though not uniformly so. The median monthly rent of $692 claims just 16.7 percent of gross household income, a burden roughly half the 30-percent threshold commonly used to define housing stress and significantly lighter than the national median rent of $928. However, the goods index at 94.5 indicates that trips to the grocery store or gas station deliver only marginal savings compared to the rest of the country. Consequently, a resident’s discretionary capacity hinges less on housing negotiations and more on the volume of services consumed; a household with high medical utilization or heavy utility demands captures disproportionate value relative to a similar earner in a high-service-cost metro. Purchasing power metrics confirm this dynamic. The Purchasing Power Index of 55,237.5 implies the median dollar stretches roughly 11.2 percent further than the nominal income suggests, effectively closing about one-third of the $15,394 gap between the local median income and the national median of $65,047. Yet the labor market context tempers this optimism. An unemployment rate of 4.5 percent sits above the national lows seen in recent cycles, and educational attainment remains low—only 6.0 percent of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher—suggesting the local wage ceiling is constrained by a labor pool oriented toward lower-wage sectors. The median age of 40.6 years indicates a stable, mature workforce, but one unlikely to drive rapid wage acceleration without external investment. Over the past decade, the affordability narrative has improved modestly. The RPP declined marginally from 90.433 in 2010 to 89.89 in 2023, a 0.6 percent drop in relative price levels, while median income surged 42.8 percent from $34,774 to $49,653. Median rent rose 39.0 percent over the same span, from $498 to $692, meaning income growth has narrowly outpaced shelter costs. For a prospective resident, the budgeting takeaway is clear: fixed shelter obligations are remarkably manageable, and service-intensive lifestyles—frequent healthcare visits, high climate-control usage, or regular dining out—are heavily subsidized by local price levels. The primary friction point remains the goods basket, where national supply chains limit local price deviation, requiring households to apply standard frugality strategies to groceries and fuel to maximize the county’s inherent cost advantage.

What this means for residents of Hart County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 55237.5, a median household income of $49,653 in Hart County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $27,427,076 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
