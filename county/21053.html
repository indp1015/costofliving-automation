📊 Local Market Analysis

Clinton County residents navigate a cost structure that sits 10.1 percent below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 89.9 in 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals distinct pressure points for household budgets. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel — represent the highest local cost component at 94.5, barely discounting national prices, while services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining plunge to 62.9, the lowest segment and the primary driver of the county’s overall affordability advantage. Rental costs sit at 80.9, offering meaningful shelter savings relative to the national benchmark, but the near-parity pricing on daily necessities means the typical consumer feels the pinch at the checkout counter far more acutely than the headline index suggests. Purchasing power metrics illustrate the tension between low nominal wages and the specific shape of local prices. The median household income of $42,168 trails the national median of $65,047 by 35.2 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of 46,910.7 indicates that the lower cost environment recovers a portion of that nominal gap, effectively boosting real income by roughly 11.3 percent above the raw dollar figure. Rent burden remains remarkably low at 17.9 percent of gross income — a figure derived from a median monthly rent of $629 against the national $928 — freeing cash flow for other categories. However, with goods consuming a disproportionate share of the remaining budget at near-national prices, the effective disposable income for a median earner remains constrained, particularly for households lacking employer-sponsored benefits that might offset the 62.9 service index. Demographic and labor market characteristics amplify these budget dynamics. An unemployment rate of 5.6 percent exceeds typical national lows, signaling slack in the local labor market that likely suppresses wage growth, while an educational attainment rate of just 8.9 percent for bachelor’s degrees or higher limits access to higher-paying remote or professional roles that could arbitrage the low service costs. The median age of 42.5 years suggests a population skewing older than the national average, potentially increasing household demand for the very healthcare services priced at a steep discount, yet potentially reducing earning years ahead. This structural profile creates a scenario where the cost of living "discount" is largely consumed by the necessity of purchasing goods at mainstream prices on below-mainstream incomes. Over the 2010 to 2023 period, the RPP drifted marginally from 90.4 to 89.9, a negligible 0.6 percent decline, while median income surged 50.5 percent from $28,025 to $42,168, significantly outpacing the 19.1 percent rise in median rent from $528 to $629. This divergence indicates that local wage growth has substantially outstripped shelter inflation, improving the rent-to-income ratio over the decade. However, the persistence of goods pricing at 94.5 suggests that the primary inflationary exposure for residents — food and energy — has likely tracked national trends, meaning the real income gains suggested by the rent data may be partially eroded by volatility in the basket items that dominate daily expenditure for the county’s workforce.

What this means for residents of Clinton County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 46910.7, a median household income of $42,168 in Clinton County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $19,781,304 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
