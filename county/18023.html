📊 Local Market Analysis

Clinton County’s cost‑of‑living index sits at 92.1, meaning the typical consumer price level is 7.9 % below the national benchmark of 100. Goods prices are the most elevated component at 95.6, still modestly under the national average but noticeably higher than the county’s service costs, which register at 71.3. Housing, measured by rental costs, is 84.7, indicating rents are 15.3 % cheaper than the national median. The composite picture shows a market where everyday necessities such as groceries, apparel, and fuel are only slightly cheaper than elsewhere, while health care, utilities, and dining out are substantially less expensive, and rent remains well below the national $928 average at $915 per month. The dominant driver of the local price structure is the goods sector. At 95.6, it outweighs both services (71.3) and rental costs (84.7), suggesting that day‑to‑day expenditures on food, clothing, and transportation consume a larger share of household budgets than discretionary services. Despite the relatively higher goods price, daily life feels affordable because service costs—often the most volatile component of personal spending—are markedly low. For a family spending $300 on groceries and $150 on fuel, the savings on utilities and dining (often 30 %–40 % lower than national levels) offset the modest premium on goods, resulting in a net cost advantage for routine consumption. Median household income in Clinton County stands at $63,690, just 2.1 % below the national median of $65,047. After accounting for the median rent of $915, rent consumes 17.2 % of household income, well under the 30 % threshold commonly used to flag housing stress. The Purchasing Power Index (PPI) of 69,155.3 quantifies the county’s ability to buy the same basket of goods and services as the national average; residents effectively retain about 69 % of the purchasing power of a typical American household. Translating the index into disposable income, a median earner with $63,690 annual earnings and $10,980 in annual rent (12 × $915) is left with roughly $52,710 before taxes and other obligations, a figure that aligns closely with the national disposable‑income profile once the lower service costs are factored in. Demographically, the county’s labor market is tight, with unemployment at 3.1 %, well below the national rate. However, only 11.2 % of residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting a workforce concentrated in lower‑skill, lower‑wage occupations. The median age of 36.5 years points to a relatively young population, likely to be in the early to mid‑career stage, which dovetails with the modest income level and limited educational attainment. The combination of low unemployment and limited higher‑skill labor keeps wage growth modest, reinforcing the reliance on affordable services and housing to maintain living standards. Over the past decade, the Regional Price Parity (RPP) slipped from 93.022 to 92.097, a 1.0 % decline, indicating that overall price pressure has eased slightly. Median household income rose from $48,478 to $63,690, a 31.4 % increase, while median rent climbed from $680 to $915, a 34.6 % rise. Income growth outpaced the modest decline in RPP, but rent inflation slightly outstripped income gains, narrowing the rent‑burden margin. The net effect is a modest improvement in real purchasing power, as evidenced by the PPI figure, yet households must remain vigilant about housing costs that erode a larger slice of earnings than a decade ago. Prospective residents should budget around $915 for monthly rent, allocate roughly 30 % of income to goods (groceries, fuel, apparel), and expect service expenses—healthcare, utilities, dining—to be about 28 % of the national average. With a median income of $63,690, a realistic monthly disposable budget after rent sits near $4,400 before taxes, allowing for modest savings if service spending is kept low. Emphasizing cost‑effective grocery shopping and limiting discretionary dining will maximize the county’s inherent affordability advantage, while monitoring rent increases remains essential to preserve the current rent‑burden cushion.

What this means for residents of Clinton County, Indiana

With a Purchasing Power Index of 69155.3, a median household income of $63,690 in Clinton County, Indiana has the equivalent buying power of $44,045,011 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 84.7 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
