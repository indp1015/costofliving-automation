📊 Local Market Analysis

Phillips County presents a cost of living profile significantly below the national baseline, registering an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 89.9 against the national 100, a gap that translates into tangible savings for household budgets. The composite figure masks sharp divergence across categories: Goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—sit at 96.5, merely 3.5 percent below the national average, while Services, covering healthcare, utilities, and dining, plunge to 68.6, a staggering 31.4 percent discount. Rental costs occupy the middle ground at 90.8. This structure indicates that daily essentials like food and gasoline carry near-national price tags, but the heavy lifting on affordability comes from deeply discounted service sectors, likely reflecting lower local labor costs for healthcare providers, utility infrastructure economies, and limited dining-out competition. The consumption breakdown reveals that Goods act as the primary anchor preventing the overall index from falling further, making routine trips to the grocery store or gas station the most "expensive" relative experience for residents. However, the Services index of 68.6 fundamentally reshapes the cost structure; a household allocating a standard share of income to healthcare premiums, electricity, and local services retains significantly more discretionary cash than counterparts in metro areas. With median monthly rent at $621—roughly 33 percent below the national $928 median—shelter consumes only 12.2 percent of the $61,235 median household income, a remarkably low rent burden that insulates renters from the cost pressures prevalent in tighter markets. This combination of cheap shelter and cheap services creates a daily life that is objectively inexpensive, though the near-parity pricing on goods tempers the total savings. Purchasing power metrics confirm the advantage: the Purchasing Power Index of 68,108.5 exceeds the nominal median income by nearly $7,000, meaning the median dollar stretches roughly 11 percent further than in the average U.S. county. Local demographics reinforce this dynamic. An unemployment rate of 3.2 percent signals a tight labor market that supports wage stability, yet the relatively low educational attainment—only 16 percent hold a bachelor’s degree or higher—and a median age of 44.6 years suggest an economy anchored in established, non-degree-intensive sectors where wage growth has historically been modest but steady. The labor market appears sufficient to sustain current income levels without generating the inflationary pressure seen in high-growth hubs. Over the 2010–2023 period, the RPP declined 3.3 percent from 92.991 to 89.908, indicating that local consumer prices actually fell relative to the national trend. Meanwhile, median household income surged 38.6 percent from $44,170 to $61,235, vastly outpacing the 21.8 percent rise in median rent from $510 to $621. This divergence means real purchasing power has expanded considerably; wage growth has not merely kept pace with expenses but has dramatically widened the gap. For a prospective resident, the budgeting implication is clear: fixed costs for shelter and services offer deep discounts, but variable costs for groceries and fuel will feel familiar. Household budgets should allocate near-national amounts for the weekly shop and commute, while enjoying substantial relief on rent, utilities, and medical outlays.

What this means for residents of Phillips County, Kansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 68108.5, a median household income of $61,235 in Phillips County, Kansas has the equivalent buying power of $41,706,240 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 90.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
