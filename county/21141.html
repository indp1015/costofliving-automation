📊 Local Market Analysis

Logan County residents navigate a cost structure that sits 10.1 percentage points below the national average, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity (RPP) for 2023 registering 89.9 against a baseline of 100. This overall discount masks significant internal divergence: the Goods component, covering groceries, apparel, and fuel, runs at 94.5, merely 5.5 points below the national norm, while Services—encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining—collapses to 62.9, a staggering 37.1-point discount that serves as the primary engine suppressing the aggregate index. Rental costs, indexed at 80.9, occupy the middle ground, contributing to a consumption environment where daily necessities like food and gasoline carry near-national price tags, but the recurring service obligations that typically erode household budgets—medical visits, electricity, and restaurant meals—are profoundly cheaper. This asymmetry dictates the lived experience of the median household earning $61,535 annually, a figure 5.4 percent below the national median of $65,047. Despite the income deficit, the Purchasing Power Index of 68,455.9 indicates that local wages stretch considerably further than their nominal value suggests. Housing absorption is remarkably low; the median monthly rent of $719 consumes only 14.0 percent of gross income, a stark contrast to the national median rent of $928 and a threshold that leaves substantial discretionary capacity for the 94.5-indexed goods basket. The labor market context reinforces this dynamic: an unemployment rate of 4.8 percent sits alongside a workforce where only 10.3 percent hold a bachelor’s degree or higher and the median age is 40.6 years, suggesting an economy anchored in sectors that do not command premium metropolitan wages but benefit disproportionately from the region’s deeply discounted service infrastructure. Over the 2010 to 2023 horizon, the RPP has remained remarkably stable, drifting only from 90.433 to 89.89, a marginal 0.6 percent decline in relative price levels. Meanwhile, nominal median income surged 59.5 percent from $38,570 to $61,535, vastly outpacing the 22.3 percent increase in median rent from $588 to $719. This divergence implies a decade of significant real wage growth, where the cost curve for the largest fixed expense—shelter—flattened relative to earnings, and the broader price level essentially flatlined. The data suggests the county has avoided the inflationary pressures that have compressed budgets in higher-cost metros, preserving the purchasing power gains derived from income growth. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the "Goods penalty." While the 14 percent rent-to-income ratio and cheap services (index 62.9) create a high floor of disposable income, the 94.5 index for groceries and fuel means weekly trip costs at the supermarket or gas station will feel nearly identical to those in a city with a 100 RPP. A household relocating from a high-cost area captures immediate shelter and healthcare savings, but the marginal dollar saved on rent is not fully matched by savings at the checkout counter. The demographic profile—older, less credentialed, with moderate unemployment—signals a stable but low-churn labor market, meaning wage negotiation leverage remains limited; budgeting must assume the current income-to-cost ratio is structural rather than cyclical.

What this means for residents of Logan County, Kentucky

With a Purchasing Power Index of 68455.9, a median household income of $61,535 in Logan County, Kentucky has the equivalent buying power of $42,124,338 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 80.9 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
