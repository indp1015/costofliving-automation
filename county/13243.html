📊 Local Market Analysis

Randolph County presents a cost of living profile that sits marginally below the national baseline, with an overall Regional Price Parity (RPP) of 96.5, yet the composition of that index reveals distinct pressure points for household budgets. Goods—encompassing groceries, apparel, and fuel—register at 97.7, nearly par with the national average and representing the highest cost component in the local consumption basket. Conversely, services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining are significantly discounted at 88.3, while rental costs sit at 92.3. This structure creates a bifurcated reality: residents benefit from cheaper services and housing relative to the country, but the essential, high-frequency expenditure on goods offers almost no relief, consuming a disproportionate share of limited local earnings. The consumption breakdown underscores that daily life is defined by income constraints rather than price levels alone. With a median household income of $25,425—roughly 39 percent of the national median of $65,047—the purchasing power index of 26,348.8 confirms that local wages fail to translate the modest cost advantages into meaningful discretionary capacity. Median monthly rent of $554 claims 26.1 percent of gross income, a manageable rent burden on paper, but the residual income after shelter must cover goods priced at 97.7 percent of the national level. A household here effectively navigates a budget where the dollar stretches furthest on services like medical visits or restaurant meals—categories often discretionary for low-income families—while the unavoidable weekly trip for groceries and fuel delivers virtually no geographic discount. Local demographics sharpen the picture of economic fragility. An unemployment rate of 10.3 percent, more than double typical national readings, combined with a bachelor’s degree attainment rate of just 9.2 percent, signals a labor market concentrated in low-wage, likely service-sector or agricultural roles. The median age of 37.6 suggests a working-age population that is not translating labor into wage growth. This structural weakness is evident in the decade-long trend: the RPP drifted upward from 94.6 to 96.5, a 2.0 percent increase in the cost of living, while median income contracted 10.4 percent from $28,377 to $25,425. Even a 6.4 percent decline in median rent over the same period failed to offset the erosion of real earnings, leaving households financially further behind in 2023 than in 2010. For a prospective resident, the budgeting calculus centers on the mismatch between fixed local wages and nationally indexed goods prices. The primary advantage lies in housing costs that run roughly 40 percent below the national median rent of $928, and service costs that are 12 percent cheaper, potentially lowering the floor for survival expenses. However, the high goods RPP means food and transportation budgets will resemble those in far more expensive metros, without the corresponding income. A realistic household ledger must prioritize the 97.7 goods index as the non-negotiable anchor, recognizing that the 88.3 services index offers savings only on expenditures a tight budget may already minimize. The data indicates a locale where the cost of *staying* is low, but the cost of *living*—eating, driving, clothing—remains stubbornly national.

What this means for residents of Randolph County, Georgia

With a Purchasing Power Index of 26348.8, a median household income of $25,425 in Randolph County, Georgia has the equivalent buying power of $6,699,182 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 92.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
