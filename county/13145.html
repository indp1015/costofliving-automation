📊 Local Market Analysis

Harris County, Georgia, registers a cost‑of‑living index of 96.5, meaning consumer prices sit 3.5 percent below the national benchmark of 100. Goods prices are the most elevated component at 97.7, while services are markedly cheaper at 88.3, and rental costs follow at 92.3. The median household income of $89,184 outpaces the national median of $65,047 by roughly 37 percent, providing a clear income advantage that offsets the modestly higher goods prices. The consumption profile is dominated by goods, which sit just under the national level but remain the highest sub‑index in the county. Services—healthcare, utilities, and dining—are the cheapest segment, falling 11.7 percent below the national average. Rental costs, at 92.3, are also below the national level, though the median monthly rent of $941 exceeds the national median rent of $928 by 1.4 percent. Because services consume a smaller share of household budgets than goods, everyday life feels relatively inexpensive; the low service index translates into cheaper utility bills, medical expenses, and restaurant meals, while the modest premium on goods is absorbed by the county’s strong income base. Disposable income calculations illustrate the purchasing power advantage. The Purchasing Power Index of 92,424.4 indicates that a median earner can afford roughly 7.5 percent more than the national median household when price differences are accounted for. After allocating 12.7 percent of income to rent ($941 per month), the median household retains about $78,000 annually for other expenditures, a figure that comfortably exceeds the national median disposable income after housing. This surplus is reinforced by the low service cost, allowing households to allocate a larger share of their budget to discretionary spending or savings. Demographically, the county’s labor market features a 5.8 percent unemployment rate, slightly above the national average, and a relatively low educational attainment—only 19.2 percent hold a bachelor’s degree or higher. The median age of 43.3 years suggests a mature workforce, likely anchored in stable, middle‑income occupations that support the high median earnings. The combination of modest unemployment and a seasoned labor pool sustains the income level, while the lower educational attainment may limit upward wage mobility, keeping the cost structure anchored in affordable services. Over the past decade, the Regional Price Parities (RPP) index rose from 94.574 to 96.494, a 2.0 percent increase, whereas median household income surged from $63,824 to $89,184, a 39.7 percent gain. Rent climbed 8.4 percent, from $868 to $941. The disparity between income growth and price inflation is pronounced; wages have outpaced both goods and rental price increases, expanding real purchasing power for residents. Prospective residents should budget for a rent burden of 12.7 percent of income, well below the national threshold of 30 percent that typically signals affordability stress. With services costing roughly 12 percent less than the national average, utility and healthcare bills will be comparatively modest. However, groceries and apparel remain near‑par with national prices, so budgeting for food and clothing should reflect the 2‑3 percent premium over the national average. A realistic monthly household budget would allocate approximately $300 to utilities, $400 to groceries, $200 to transportation fuel, and $150 to discretionary dining, leaving ample room for savings given the county’s strong disposable income margin.

What this means for residents of Harris County, Georgia

With a Purchasing Power Index of 92424.4, a median household income of $89,184 in Harris County, Georgia has the equivalent buying power of $82,427,777 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 92.3 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
