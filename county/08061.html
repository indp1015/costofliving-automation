📊 Local Market Analysis

Kiowa County presents a cost of living profile that sits marginally above the national baseline at 101.9, yet this aggregate figure masks a severe structural distortion driven almost entirely by service-sector inflation. While goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel track slightly below the national average at 99.2, and rental housing costs offer significant relief at 86.8, the services index—encompassing healthcare, utilities, and dining—surges to 130.5, representing a 30.5 percent premium over the U.S. benchmark. This divergence creates a bifurcated consumer experience: daily necessities and shelter remain accessible, but the unavoidable recurring costs of maintaining a household—medical care, electricity, and water—impose a disproportionate burden on local budgets, effectively neutralizing the savings found in cheaper rent and goods. The purchasing power calculus reveals a household sector struggling to convert nominal income gains into real standard-of-living improvements. The median household income of $56,389 trails the national median of $65,047 by 13.3 percent, and when adjusted by the Regional Price Parity, the Purchasing Power Index falls to $55,361.5, indicating that the typical resident commands roughly $9,700 less in real buying capacity than the national counterpart. Although rent burden sits at a manageable 18.5 percent of income—well below the 30 percent stress threshold—this metric flatters to deceive; the low rental cost index of 86.8 reflects a housing stock that is likely older or limited in supply, while the 130.5 services index suggests that every dollar saved on shelter is likely redirected toward utility bills and healthcare premiums. Historical trends exacerbate this pressure: over the 2010–2023 period, median rents climbed 49.3 percent against a 39.9 percent rise in median income, a divergence that has steadily eroded disposable income despite the county’s overall RPP remaining relatively flat, moving only from 100.59 to 101.856. Local demographics and labor dynamics sharpen the picture of a community operating on thin margins. An unemployment rate of 0.0 percent signals an extremely tight labor market, yet educational attainment remains low, with only 13.8 percent of residents holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting the available employment skews toward lower-wage sectors that lack the leverage to demand wages commensurate with the soaring services index. The median age of 43.9 years, notably above the national median, implies a population with escalating healthcare consumption needs precisely where the cost penalty is most acute. This demographic profile transforms the high services RPP from a statistical anomaly into a daily fiscal constraint; older households on fixed or modest incomes face a structural mismatch between their consumption basket—heavily weighted toward medical services and utilities—and the local price environment. For a prospective resident, the budgeting imperative centers on insulating the household budget from service-sector volatility. The low rental costs—median monthly rent of $869 versus the national $928—provide a stable anchor, but the 30.5 percent premium on services demands aggressive management of utility consumption, careful navigation of healthcare provider networks, and a contingency reserve for medical out-of-pocket maximums. Goods pricing at 99.2 offers no meaningful offset. The critical variable is not housing affordability, which is favorable, but the capacity to absorb recurring, non-discretionary service costs that exceed national norms by a wide margin while earning incomes that lag the national median by double digits.

What this means for residents of Kiowa County, Colorado

Because the Housing Rent index is 86.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
