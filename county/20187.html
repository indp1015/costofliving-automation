📊 Local Market Analysis

Stanton County residents operate within a cost environment that sits 10.1 percentage points below the national average, registering an overall Regional Price Parity of 89.9 in 2023, yet the composition of that discount reveals a distinct bifurcation in daily expenses. Goods — encompassing groceries, apparel, and motor fuel — carry a parity of 96.5, nearly matching the national baseline and standing as the highest cost component in the local basket. By contrast, services such as healthcare, utilities, and dining plunge to 68.6, a staggering 31.4 percent below the U.S. average, while rental costs settle at 90.8. This structure indicates that while keeping a roof overhead and putting food on the table demands near-national outlays, the ancillary services that typically erode disposable income — medical visits, electricity, and restaurant meals — are profoundly inexpensive, creating a daily life that is cheap in its operational rhythm but less forgiving at the checkout counter. The median household income of $70,361 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by 8.2 percent, and when adjusted for the local price level, purchasing power swells to an effective $78,259, a figure that underscores the county’s capacity for wealth retention. Housing absorbs a mere 11.8 percent of gross income at a median rent of $691, roughly half the national median of $928, leaving a wide margin for savings or discretionary spending. The labor market reinforces this stability; a 2.2 percent unemployment rate signals tight demand for labor, while a median age of 36.5 suggests a workforce in its prime earning years. However, with only 16.6 percent of adults holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, the income premium likely stems from trade, agriculture, or energy sectors rather than knowledge-economy roles, a demographic factor that caps upside mobility even as it insulates the community from white-collar volatility. Over the thirteen-year span from 2010 to 2023, the RPP declined from 93.0 to 89.9, a 3.3 percent drop in relative living costs that ran counter to the typical inflationary drift seen in most metros. Simultaneously, median income surged 60.7 percent from $43,780 to $70,361, vastly outpacing the 21.9 percent climb in median rent from $567 to $691. This divergence means the median worker’s real housing burden has effectively halved in relative terms, a rare dynamic where wage growth has not merely kept pace but has structurally decoupled from shelter inflation. The goods parity near 97 suggests that tradable items — fuel, vehicles, packaged food — remain tethered to global supply chains, limiting further downside on the largest daily expense categories. A prospective resident should budget for grocery and fuel bills that mirror suburban America while enjoying service costs that resemble a developing economy; a routine doctor visit or utility bill will feel jarringly cheap by coastal standards. The rent-to-income ratio of 11.8 percent provides a massive buffer, but the goods-heavy consumption basket means inflation spikes in energy or food transmit directly to the household ledger with little local offset. With services so deeply discounted, the marginal dollar goes further here than almost anywhere in the Plains, provided the earner remains attached to the local labor market; remote workers earning coastal wages would find an arbitrage opportunity of historic proportions, though the analysis rests strictly on the median local earner’s experience.

What this means for residents of Stanton County, Kansas

With a Purchasing Power Index of 78258.9, a median household income of $70,361 in Stanton County, Kansas has the equivalent buying power of $55,063,745 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 90.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
