📊 Local Market Analysis

Fremont County’s cost of living sits marginally above the national baseline at 101.9 on the BEA Regional Price Parity index, but that aggregate figure masks a severe structural distortion in local consumer pricing. Services command an index of 130.5, making them roughly 30 percent more expensive than the U.S. average and establishing them as the dominant inflationary force in household budgets. This spike likely reflects elevated healthcare, utility, and dining costs typical of rural mountain economies with limited provider competition. Conversely, rental housing costs register at just 86.8, while goods—groceries, apparel, and fuel—come in slightly below par at 99.2. The net effect is a bifurcated reality: securing shelter is relatively affordable, but the recurring monthly outlays for essential services consume a disproportionate share of income. Purchasing power metrics confirm the squeeze. The median household income of $61,027 trails the national median of $65,047 by 6.2 percent, yet the Purchasing Power Index of $59,915 indicates that local prices erode nearly the entire nominal income advantage renters might perceive from below-average housing costs. Rent burden stands at a manageable 20.4 percent of gross income—well below the 30 percent stress threshold—based on a median monthly rent of $1,036 against the national $928. However, that savings on shelter is likely redirected toward the 130.5 service index. A household here effectively operates on a real income roughly $5,100 below the national median after adjusting for the local cost structure, leaving less discretionary capital for savings or debt reduction than the raw rent-to-income ratio suggests. Demographic and labor market characteristics amplify this pressure. An unemployment rate of 4.4 percent exceeds the national norm, signaling a slack labor market that suppresses wage growth, while a median age of 44.9 years and a bachelor’s attainment rate of just 15 percent point toward a workforce concentrated in lower-wage service, extraction, or public-sector roles. Over the 2010–2023 period, nominal median income surged 51 percent from $40,423, outpacing the 45.5 percent rise in median rent, yet the RPP barely budged, climbing only 1.3 percent from 100.59. This suggests income gains have largely been absorbed by the persistent service-sector premium rather than translating into improved real living standards; the cost curve for non-housing essentials has effectively neutralized wage appreciation. For a prospective resident, the budgeting imperative is clear: optimize housing savings to subsidize the service penalty. The 13.2 percent discount on rent relative to the national average yields roughly $1,100 in annual shelter savings, but the 30.5 percent service premium on categories like healthcare and utilities—which often represent 25 to 30 percent of a typical budget—can easily swallow that margin and more. Households should model scenarios where health insurance premiums, electricity, and vehicle maintenance run significantly higher than national benchmarks. The goods basket offers no relief at 99.2, meaning grocery and fuel strategies yield minimal arbitrage. Financial resilience here depends on securing employment with robust benefits—particularly health coverage—to offset the private service costs that define the county’s economic friction.

What this means for residents of Fremont County, Colorado

With a Purchasing Power Index of 59915.0, a median household income of $61,027 in Fremont County, Colorado has the equivalent buying power of $36,564,327 in an average U.S. county.

Because the Housing Rent index is 86.8 (vs. the national average of 100), housing is the primary driver of the local cost of living. Prospective residents should budget carefully for rent or mortgage payments, even if everyday goods and services feel relatively affordable.
