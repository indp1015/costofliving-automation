📊 Local Market Analysis

Okaloosa County’s cost of living sits modestly above the national norm, with the BEA Regional Price Parities (RPP) index at 103.6 versus a baseline of 100. Goods such as groceries, apparel, and fuel are priced 2.2 % below the national average (98.2), while services—including healthcare, utilities, and dining—run 23.2 % higher (123.2). Rental costs are the most affordable component, measured at 93.7, indicating that housing expenses are 6.3 % cheaper than the countrywide average. The median household income of $79,097 exceeds the national median of $65,047 by roughly 21.6 %, giving residents a clear income advantage that partially offsets the premium on services. The service sector dominates the local cost structure. With a services index of 123.2, everyday expenditures on medical care, electricity, water, and restaurant meals are the primary drivers of higher overall living costs. By contrast, the goods index of 98.2 suggests that routine purchases like food and clothing are relatively inexpensive, and the housing index of 93.7 confirms that rent is notably lower than elsewhere. The median monthly rent of $1,475 translates to a rent‑burden of 22.4 % of household income, well under the 30 % threshold commonly used to define affordability. Consequently, while daily life is not cheap across the board, the lower housing outlay and cheaper goods cushion the impact of elevated service prices, leaving a net disposable income that remains attractive relative to many parts of the United States. Purchasing power in Okaloosa County is quantified by an index of 76,321.9, reflecting the dollar amount a median household can spend after accounting for local price levels. Subtracting the median rent of $1,475 from the monthly income of $6,591 (annual $79,097 ÷ 12) leaves $5,116 for all other expenses, taxes, and savings. After adjusting for the services premium, the effective purchasing power aligns closely with the index figure, indicating that the median resident retains roughly $1,200–$1,300 more per month than a comparable household in a region where the RPP equals 100. The county’s unemployment rate of 3.1 %—well below the national rate—supports a stable labor market, while only 21.2 % of adults hold a bachelor’s degree or higher, suggesting a workforce concentrated in service‑oriented and skilled‑trade occupations that typically command modest wages but benefit from the lower housing cost base. The median age of 37.3 places the population squarely in the prime earning years, reinforcing the capacity to absorb higher service costs. Over the past decade, the RPP rose from 100.4 in 2010 to 103.6 in 2023, a 3.2 % increase, whereas median household income surged from $55,880 to $79,097, a 41.5 % gain. Rental prices climbed from $1,024 to $1,475, a 44.0 % jump, outpacing the modest rise in the overall price index. The disparity between income growth and rent inflation suggests that while wages have outstripped general price pressures, housing costs have expanded at a comparable rate to income, preserving the county’s relative affordability. Nevertheless, the steep service‑price premium has not kept pace with income growth, meaning that discretionary spending on health and leisure remains proportionally higher than in many other locales. Prospective residents should anchor their budgets around three pillars: a modest goods basket, a service‑heavy expense profile, and a rent level that consumes roughly one‑fifth of household earnings. Allocating about 12–15 % of income to groceries and apparel aligns with the sub‑national goods index, while 25–30 % should be earmarked for healthcare, utilities, and dining to match the 123.2 services index. The remaining 55–60 % can cover rent, transportation, and savings. Because rent is the only category where costs exceed the national average, households with tighter budgets may consider shared housing or locating in adjacent, lower‑rent neighborhoods. Overall, the combination of higher-than‑average incomes, low rent burden, and a service‑driven cost structure defines Okaloosa County as a region where discretionary spending power remains robust despite elevated service prices.

What this means for residents of Okaloosa County, Florida
