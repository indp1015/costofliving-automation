Cost of Living in Maine

Below are the counties in Maine, ranked by Purchasing Power Index (higher is better). A score above 100 means residents have more disposable income relative to local costs.

📊 State-Level Economic Review

Maine’s cost profile remains modestly below the national benchmark, with the overall consumer cost index registering 98.0 against a national baseline of 100. This indicates that the average daily cost of living in the state is about two percent cheaper than the country as a whole. The goods component sits at 98.3, essentially in line with the national average, while services are markedly lower at 80.4, suggesting that everyday services such as healthcare, transportation and personal care are more affordable than elsewhere. In stark contrast, rental costs climb to 148.2, positioning housing as the most expensive category and a primary driver of the state’s overall expense structure. The internal variation in purchasing power across Maine’s counties is pronounced. Cumberland County leads with a purchasing power of $94,915.5, whereas Washington County trails at $53,322.6, a gap of roughly $41,593. This disparity underscores a significant divide in residents’ ability to stretch their earnings. Aroostook County and Piscataquis County also rank among the lowest, with purchasing power of $55,381.6 and $56,381.9 respectively, reinforcing a geographic split where southern, more urban counties enjoy substantially higher disposable income than the northern and eastern regions. Among the three consumer categories, rents are the most above‑average component, scoring 148.2 compared with the national baseline of 100. Goods sit just above parity at 98.3, while services remain well below at 80.4. The elevated rental index suggests that housing costs are the primary pressure point for household budgets, offsetting the affordability of other expenses. The divergence between high rents and lower service costs creates a mixed economic environment where some expenditures are favorable and others are burdensome. For residents seeking the greatest value within their household budgets, the counties with the highest purchasing power—Cumberland, York and Sagadahoc—offer the most favorable conditions. These southern counties combine relatively strong income levels with a cost structure where services are below average, partially mitigating the impact of high rents. While housing remains costly, the overall purchasing power in these areas enables households to allocate resources across a broader range of goods and services compared with the lower‑income counties in the north and east. This spatial contrast highlights how local economic dynamics shape the real‑world affordability of daily life across Maine.
