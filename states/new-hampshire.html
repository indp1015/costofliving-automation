Cost of Living in New Hampshire

Below are the counties in New Hampshire, ranked by Purchasing Power Index (higher is better). A score above 100 means residents have more disposable income relative to local costs.

📊 State-Level Economic Review

New Hampshire’s cost profile shows a modest but measurable premium relative to the nation. The state’s overall consumer cost index of 105.4 indicates that daily living expenses are 5.4 % higher than the national baseline of 100. Within this composite, goods costs sit exactly at parity (100.0), while services run 14.5 % above average and rental costs climb to 156.2, the most pronounced deviation among the three categories. The data suggest that housing, rather than everyday goods or services, is the primary pressure point for household budgets in New Hampshire. The internal variation in purchasing power across the ten counties is stark. Rockingham County records the highest purchasing power at 108,097.3, whereas Coos County registers the lowest at 55,448.7. This gap of roughly $52,649 translates to a difference of about 95 % in real disposable income after accounting for local price levels. Intermediate counties such as Hillsborough (95,296.7) and Merrimack (89,136.8) occupy the mid‑range, while Cheshire (76,856.1), Sullivan (72,043.7), and the three lowest‑ranked counties illustrate a progressive decline in economic cushion. The spread underscores how geography within a relatively small state can produce dramatically different financial environments for residents. Among the three consumer categories, rental costs dominate the upward deviation. At 156.2, rents exceed the national average by more than half, dwarfing the 14.5 % premium seen in services. Goods, by contrast, align precisely with the national benchmark. This concentration of high housing expenses helps explain why overall consumer costs remain above the national norm despite goods being neutrally priced. The rental premium likely reflects limited housing supply, high demand near economic hubs, and regulatory factors that are not detailed in the current dataset. For households seeking maximum value, the counties with the strongest purchasing power—Rockingham, Hillsborough, and Merrimack—offer the most favorable balance between income and local price levels. Residents in these areas generally retain a larger share of earnings after covering essential expenses, particularly housing. While the data do not break down rental costs by county, the higher purchasing power in these regions suggests that, all else being equal, families can achieve greater budgetary flexibility there. Conversely, counties with lower purchasing power, especially in the northern tier, face tighter constraints, making cost‑conscious planning more critical.
