Cost of Living in Massachusetts

Below are the counties in Massachusetts, ranked by Purchasing Power Index (higher is better). A score above 100 means residents have more disposable income relative to local costs.

📊 State-Level Economic Review

Massachusetts ranks among the most expensive states for daily living, with an overall consumer cost index of 107.7 relative to the national baseline of 100. This 7.7‑percent premium reflects a persistent upward pressure on household budgets, driven primarily by the steep escalation of rental expenses. While the state’s goods costs sit exactly at the national average of 100.0, services run 30.1 points above parity and rents surge to 166.1, making housing the dominant cost driver in the Commonwealth’s cost profile. Internal variation in purchasing power across the fourteen counties analyzed reveals a stark geographic divide. The highest purchasing power is recorded in Middlesex County at 117,742.3, followed closely by Norfolk County at 117,480.4 and Nantucket County at 111,214.3. In contrast, the lowest figures appear in Franklin County (67,410.3), Berkshire County (67,392.6) and Hampden County (65,507.3). The gap between the best‑performing and worst‑performing counties exceeds 52,000 purchasing power units, indicating that residents in the western tier experience roughly 80 percent less disposable income after accounting for local price levels than their counterparts in the eastern corridor. Among the three consumer categories, rental costs stand out as the most pronounced deviation from the national norm. At 166.1, rents are 66.1 points above the baseline, dwarfing the 30.1‑point excess of services and the neutral performance of goods. This disproportionate rent premium explains why the overall consumer cost index exceeds the national average despite goods being priced at parity. The housing component therefore remains the principal factor inflating the cost of living for Massachusetts households. For residents seeking the greatest value within their household budgets, the counties with the highest purchasing power—Middlesex, Norfolk and Nantucket—offer the most favorable environment. These jurisdictions demonstrate that higher median incomes combine with relatively lower cost pressures to produce a stronger real purchasing capacity. Conversely, the western counties, where purchasing power is markedly lower, suggest a tighter fiscal environment for typical families. The data underscores that geographic location within Massachusetts can materially affect a household’s ability to stretch its earnings, with the eastern corridor generally providing more budgetary flexibility than the western regions.
