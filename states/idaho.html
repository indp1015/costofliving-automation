Cost of Living in Idaho

Below are the counties in Idaho, ranked by Purchasing Power Index (higher is better). A score above 100 means residents have more disposable income relative to local costs.

📊 State-Level Economic Review

Idaho’s overall consumer cost index of 92.2 indicates that the state’s daily living expenses are roughly 7.8 percent below the national benchmark of 100. This discount is driven primarily by rental costs, which sit at 68.1, well under the national average, while goods and services remain closer to parity at 95.9 and 86.9 respectively. The state’s cost profile suggests that households can purchase a comparable basket of goods and services for less than their national counterparts, though the advantage is not uniform across regions. The internal variation in purchasing power is stark. Teton County leads with a purchasing power of 98,378.1, whereas Butte County trails at 46,924.2. The gap of approximately 51,454 points translates into a difference of more than 50 % in real purchasing capacity between the two extremes. Even the middle tier—Ada County at 96,390.8 and Blaine County at 91,580.3—demonstrates that affluence and cost‑of‑living adjustments are highly localized. This dispersion underscores that residents in the same state can experience dramatically different standards of living based solely on county of residence. Among the three consumer categories, goods costs are the most above average. At 95.9, goods are 4.1 points higher than services (86.9) and 27.8 points above rental costs (68.1). While none of the categories exceed the national index of 100, the relative weight of goods suggests that food, clothing, and other tangible items constitute the largest premium component of Idaho’s cost structure. Services, particularly those tied to health, education, and personal care, are comparatively cheaper, and housing remains the most affordable segment of the overall index. For households seeking maximum value, the counties with the lowest purchasing power—Shoshone, Lewis, and Butte—offer the most favorable budget environment. These areas combine lower rental expenses with modest goods and services costs, allowing disposable income to stretch further. Residents in these locales can allocate a larger share of earnings to savings or discretionary spending without sacrificing essential consumption. Conversely, high‑purchasing‑power counties such as Teton and Ada reflect higher income levels that offset relatively higher local price points, but the net effect is a tighter budget constraint for median earners. The analysis illustrates that Idaho’s overall affordability masks significant regional disparities, and strategic location choices can materially influence household financial well‑being.
