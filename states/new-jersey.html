Cost of Living in New Jersey

Below are the counties in New Jersey, ranked by Purchasing Power Index (higher is better). A score above 100 means residents have more disposable income relative to local costs.

📊 State-Level Economic Review

New Jersey’s cost of living remains markedly above the national benchmark, with an overall consumer cost index of 108.9, indicating that the average daily expenses in the state exceed the U.S. average by 8.9 percent. The state’s goods component sits at 105.8, modestly higher than the national baseline, while services climb to 134.1, the most pronounced deviation among the three categories. Rental costs also weigh in above parity at 114.8, reinforcing the picture of a high‑cost environment for households. The disparity between the national baseline and New Jersey’s overall index underscores the financial pressure faced by residents, even when median income reaches $100,891, yielding an average purchasing power of $92,610.4 after accounting for local price levels. The internal variation in purchasing power across New Jersey’s counties is stark. The highest‑earning county, Hunterdon, enjoys a purchasing power of $128,007.8, whereas the lowest, Cumberland, registers $59,205.4. This spread of $68,802.4 translates into a more than twofold difference in what households can afford after local costs are considered. Somerset and Morris counties sit near the upper tier, with purchasing powers of $124,801.5 and $123,855.1 respectively, while Atlantic and Essex counties hover near the lower end at $70,514.3 and $70,416.1. Such geographic disparity highlights how location within the state can dramatically alter a family’s effective income and standard of living, independent of nominal earnings. Among the consumer categories, services are the primary cost driver pushing New Jersey’s index upward. At 134.1, the services index exceeds the national baseline by 34.1 percent, far surpassing the 5.8 percent premium for goods and the 14.8 percent premium for rents. This suggests that health care, education, transportation, and other service-oriented expenses constitute the largest pressure point for household budgets. The outsized services cost may reflect the state’s high labor intensity, regulatory environment, and the concentration of metropolitan areas where service prices are traditionally elevated. For residents seeking the greatest value within their household budget, the northern tier of the state offers the most favorable conditions. Hunterdon, Somerset, and Morris counties consistently rank at the top of purchasing power, indicating that after local costs are factored, households in these areas retain a larger share of income for discretionary spending and savings. The combination of relatively higher median incomes and a cost structure that, while still above average, does not penalize residents as heavily as in the southern counties, makes these regions the most economically advantageous for budget‑conscious families. The contrast between these high‑value locales and the lower‑value southern counties underscores the importance of geographic considerations when evaluating affordability within New Jersey.
