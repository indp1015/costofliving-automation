Cost of Living in Hawaii

Below are the counties in Hawaii, ranked by Purchasing Power Index (higher is better). A score above 100 means residents have more disposable income relative to local costs.

📊 State-Level Economic Review

Hawaii’s cost profile remains markedly elevated relative to the national baseline, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity index showing an overall consumer cost index of 109.7—nearly a 10 percent premium over the U.S. average of 100. This elevation is driven by disproportionate expenses in services and especially rentals, which sit at 128.7 and 191.7 respectively, dwarfing the modest 110.3 reading for goods. The divergence underscores that daily living in Hawaii is not merely a matter of higher wages but a structural cost imbalance that compresses disposable income across the archipelago. Internal variation in purchasing power across the five counties is substantial, reflecting geographic and economic disparities that extend beyond the island’s tourism narrative. The highest purchasing power recorded in Honolulu County reaches 95,042.1, while the lowest appears in Hawaii County at 70,385.5, yielding a spread of roughly 24,656.6. This gap illustrates that residents of the Big Island confront a markedly tighter budget environment than their counterparts on Oahu, despite both regions sharing the same state-level cost index. The overlap of Kauai County’s figures in both the highest and lowest lists suggests data inconsistencies, yet the county’s purchasing power of 85,332.2 remains firmly in the middle tier, indicating a more balanced cost‑to‑income dynamic than the extremes observed elsewhere. Among the three consumer categories, rental costs dominate the premium landscape, registering at 191.7—almost double the national baseline and well above the 110.3 for goods and 128.7 for services. This pronounced rental premium reflects Hawaii’s limited land availability, high demand for housing, and the premium placed on coastal and tourist‑adjacent locations. The disparity in rental costs directly influences household budgeting, as shelter typically constitutes the largest expense for most families, leaving less room for discretionary spending in high‑rent zones. For residents seeking the most value within their household budgets, Honolulu County emerges as the locale with the greatest purchasing power, despite its elevated rental environment. The county’s higher median income and robust economic activity generate a purchasing power of 95,042.1, outpacing Maui, Kauai, and the Big Island. This suggests that, on average, households in Honolulu retain a larger margin after essential expenses, offering relatively more flexibility for savings or non‑essential consumption. However, the decision is nuanced; the high cost of living, particularly in housing, means that value is context‑dependent and varies with individual income levels and lifestyle preferences.
