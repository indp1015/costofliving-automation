Cost of Living in Nevada

Below are the counties in Nevada, ranked by Purchasing Power Index (higher is better). A score above 100 means residents have more disposable income relative to local costs.

📊 State-Level Economic Review

Nevada’s overall consumer cost index of 97.9 places the state roughly two percent below the national benchmark of 100, indicating that, on average, daily expenses are modestly cheaper than the country as a whole. The state’s goods costs sit at 96.8, reinforcing the pattern of lower-than-average spending on tangible items, while services costs rise to 113.3, making services the most expensive category relative to the national average. Rental costs remain the most affordable component at 91.3, suggesting that housing expenses contribute positively to Nevada’s cost‑of‑living advantage. The combination of lower goods and rent prices, offset by higher service prices, creates a nuanced cost profile that differs from the national mix. Purchasing power across Nevada’s counties exhibits a pronounced disparity. Storey County leads with a purchasing power of 98,098.3, whereas Mineral County trails at 51,689.6, a gap of roughly 46,409 units. Douglas and Washoe counties fall in the middle, with values of 89,941.9 and 87,471.0 respectively, while Lincoln and Nye counties also register significantly lower figures at 71,015.0 and 57,198.5. This spread underscores how economic conditions can vary dramatically within a single state, affecting residents’ ability to allocate resources across goods, services, and housing. Among the three consumer categories, services are the principal driver of Nevada’s above‑average cost structure. At 113.3, services exceed the national baseline by 13.3 percent, a margin larger than any deviation observed in goods (96.8) or rents (91.3). The elevated service costs likely reflect higher labor intensity or regional pricing in sectors such as healthcare, education, and personal care, which can strain household budgets despite the state’s overall lower price levels for other necessities. For households seeking maximum value, the geographic distribution of purchasing power offers a clear indicator. Storey County’s purchasing power of 98,098.3 suggests that residents there can stretch their income furthest, benefiting from a combination of relatively low goods and rent costs alongside a robust earnings environment. While services remain costly statewide, the higher purchasing power in Storey County offsets those expenses, making it the most favorable location for budget-conscious families. In contrast, counties with lower purchasing power, such as Mineral and Nye, face tighter budget constraints despite the state’s overall cost advantages. Residents in these areas must allocate a larger share of income to meet service demands, highlighting the importance of local economic conditions in shaping household financial well‑being.
