Cost of Living in Washington

Below are the counties in Washington, ranked by Purchasing Power Index (higher is better). A score above 100 means residents have more disposable income relative to local costs.

📊 State-Level Economic Review

Washington’s cost of living remains above the national benchmark, with an overall consumer cost index of 108.4 (national = 100). This indicates that the average daily expenses for goods, services, and housing in the state are roughly 8.4 % higher than the country as a whole. Within the state, the breakdown shows that services are the most expensive category at 125.5, while goods sit at 106.9 and rental costs are actually below the national average at 84.0. The median household income of $76,363 translates into an average purchasing power of $70,465.4, providing a baseline for assessing how that income stretches across the state’s varied cost structure. The disparity in purchasing power between Washington’s wealthiest and poorest counties is stark. King County leads with a purchasing power of $112,713.9, whereas Whitman County records the lowest at $48,807.8. The gap of approximately $63,906.1 underscores a significant internal variation, reflecting differences in local economies, tax structures, and cost pressures that are not captured by the statewide averages alone. This range means that a resident in King County can command roughly double the purchasing power of a comparable resident in Whitman County, even after accounting for higher service costs in the former. Among the three consumer categories, services are the primary cost driver pushing Washington’s overall index above the national level. At 125.5, the services component exceeds the national baseline by 25.5 points, outpacing goods (106.9) and far surpassing rental costs (84.0). The relatively low rental index suggests that housing expenses are not the main contributor to the state’s elevated cost profile; instead, higher prices for health care, education, and other service-oriented items are the dominant factor. For households seeking the most value within their budget, the data point to counties where the combination of lower rental costs and modest service expenses aligns with available income. Eastern Washington counties such as Whitman, Ferry, and Wahkiakum, despite reporting the lowest purchasing power, benefit from rental costs that are well below the state average, helping to offset the higher service prices elsewhere. Residents in these areas can therefore stretch a given income further, especially when housing is a major expense. Conversely, while King and Snohomish counties offer the highest purchasing power, the elevated service costs may erode some of that advantage, making the trade‑off between income and expense a critical consideration for budget planning.
