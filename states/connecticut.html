Cost of Living in Connecticut

Below are the counties in Connecticut, ranked by Purchasing Power Index (higher is better). A score above 100 means residents have more disposable income relative to local costs.

📊 State-Level Economic Review

Connecticut’s cost profile in 2023 shows an overall consumer cost index of 104.2, meaning the average daily cost of living exceeds the national baseline by 4.2 percent. Goods costs sit at 98.6, slightly below the national average, while services and especially rents drive the state’s premium. Services register at 116.6, 16.6 percent above the national level, and rental costs climb to 153.2, a full 53.2 percent higher than the country as a whole. The median household income of $93,307 yields an average purchasing power of $89,545, indicating that despite higher earnings, residents still face elevated expenses relative to what they can spend. Purchasing power varies markedly across the nine counties examined. The Western Connecticut Planning Region enjoys the highest purchasing power at $119,531.5, whereas the Southeastern Connecticut Planning Region records the lowest at $80,791.0. This spread of $38,740.5 represents roughly a 48 percent disparity between the best‑served and most‑constrained areas. The Naugatuck Valley and South Central regions fall near the bottom, each hovering just above $82,800, while the Capitol region sits in the middle at $87,850.4. The median income of $93,307 sits above the average purchasing power, underscoring that cost pressures erode disposable income in several locales. Among the three consumer categories, rental costs are the most pronounced driver of Connecticut’s above‑average expense profile. While goods remain essentially neutral at 98.6, services are already 16.6 percent above the national index. Rents, however, exceed the national benchmark by more than half, making housing the primary cost pressure point for households. This imbalance explains why the overall consumer cost index rises well above 100 despite goods being relatively affordable. Residents seeking the greatest value for their household budgets are best positioned in the Western Connecticut Planning Region, where purchasing power reaches $119,531.5. This area’s higher income relative to local costs provides the most favorable environment for discretionary spending. In contrast, the Naugatuck Valley, South Central, and Southeastern regions present tighter budgets, with purchasing power below $83,000. The data illustrate that even within a high‑income state, geographic variation in cost structures creates distinct affordability landscapes, guiding households toward regions where earnings stretch furthest against local price levels.
