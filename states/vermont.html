Cost of Living in Vermont

Below are the counties in Vermont, ranked by Purchasing Power Index (higher is better). A score above 100 means residents have more disposable income relative to local costs.

📊 State-Level Economic Review

Vermont’s cost profile in 2023 shows an overall consumer cost index of 97.1, meaning the state’s average daily expenses are roughly 2.9 percent below the national benchmark of 100. While the state’s total cost burden is modestly lower than the country as a whole, the breakdown reveals a striking divergence among categories. Goods costs sit at 97.9, just under the national average, and services are markedly cheaper at 82.3, indicating that everyday items and personal services are more affordable than elsewhere. In contrast, rental costs surge to 122.1, pushing housing expenses nearly 22 percent above the national norm and establishing rents as the primary driver of higher living expenses in Vermont. The internal variation in consumer purchasing power across Vermont’s 14 counties is substantial. The highest purchasing power is recorded in Chittenden County at $97,084.7, followed by Grand Isle County at $93,291.3 and Addison County at $91,081.1. At the opposite end, Caledonia County registers $68,019.0, Rutland County $66,683.8, and Essex County $60,720.4. The spread between the most affluent county and the least affluent exceeds $36,000, illustrating a wide disparity in disposable income that reflects differences in local wage levels, cost structures, and demographic composition. This range underscores that residents in the northern and eastern parts of the state face markedly tighter budget constraints than those in the more populated southwestern corridor. Among the three consumer categories, rental costs are the most above average. With an index of 122.1, housing expenses outpace both goods (97.9) and services (82.3) and stand as the single largest upward deviation from the national baseline. The elevated rent index suggests that housing affordability is the principal challenge for Vermont households, outweighing the relatively modest savings seen in goods and services. The high rental index also helps explain why overall consumer costs remain near the national average despite lower expenditures in other categories. For households seeking maximum value within their budget, the data point to counties where purchasing power is strongest. Chittenden County, with a purchasing power of $97,084.7, offers the greatest capacity to cover daily expenses while maintaining discretionary spending. Although rental costs are elevated statewide, the higher median income in Chittenden County mitigates the impact of those expenses, providing residents with relatively more financial flexibility. In contrast, counties such as Essex, Caledonia, and Rutland, while featuring lower overall costs, are offset by significantly reduced purchasing power, leaving residents with less room for savings or discretionary consumption. Consequently, the most favorable environment for household budgeting is found in the counties where disposable income outpaces local price levels, allowing residents to stretch their earnings further despite the broader state’s high housing costs.
