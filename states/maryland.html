Cost of Living in Maryland

Below are the counties in Maryland, ranked by Purchasing Power Index (higher is better). A score above 100 means residents have more disposable income relative to local costs.

📊 State-Level Economic Review

Maryland’s average consumer costs sit 4.6 percent above the national baseline, registering a Regional Price Parity of 104.6 for 2023, yet the aggregate figure masks a severe distortion in the services sector. While goods track close to the national mean at 103.2 and rents exceed it by a moderate 8.7 percent, services surge to 119.9, marking the category as the primary driver of the state’s elevated cost structure. This 20-percentage-point premium over the national services average suggests that labor-intensive expenses — healthcare, professional fees, and hospitality — impose a disproportionate burden on household budgets relative to tangible goods or shelter alone. The disparity in purchasing power across the state’s 24 counties reveals a bifurcated economic reality. Howard County leads with an adjusted purchasing power of $140,515.5, a figure nearly 2.8 times that of Somerset County, which sits at the bottom with $50,153.9. Calvert and Montgomery counties follow Howard above the $123,000 threshold, while Baltimore City, Allegany, and Somerset form a distinct lower tier clustered near the $55,000 mark. This spread exceeds $90,000, indicating that geographic location within state lines dictates real living standards more aggressively than the statewide median income of $94,152 would suggest. The average purchasing power of $90,009.9 falls below the nominal median income, confirming that the typical Maryland household loses real value to the price level. Residents seeking maximum budgetary efficiency find it in the corridor of high-income, moderate-RPP jurisdictions. Howard County’s combination of top-tier earnings and a price level that does not fully offset those wages yields the highest real return. Calvert and Montgomery counties offer similar dynamics, where six-figure median incomes translate into six-figure real purchasing power. Conversely, Baltimore City and the western and lower Eastern Shore counties demonstrate how modest nominal incomes, when subjected to even average price levels, erode real consumption capacity. The data illustrates that Maryland’s cost of living is not a monolith but a series of distinct micro-economies where the interaction between local wage structures and the 119.9 services index determines actual financial latitude.
