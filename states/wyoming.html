Cost of Living in Wyoming

Below are the counties in Wyoming, ranked by Purchasing Power Index (higher is better). A score above 100 means residents have more disposable income relative to local costs.

📊 State-Level Economic Review

Wyoming’s cost profile remains markedly below the national benchmark, with the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parity (RPP) index for 2023 showing an overall consumer cost of 91.6 relative to the national baseline of 100. This indicates that the average daily cost of living in Wyoming is roughly 8.4 percent lower than the United States as a whole. Within this aggregate, goods costs sit at 97.1, services at 75.7 and rental costs at 74.1, revealing that while goods are the closest to parity, services and housing expenses are substantially cheaper than the national average. The divergence among categories underscores that Wyoming’s affordability advantage is driven primarily by lower housing and service expenditures rather than by price reductions in tangible goods. The internal variation in purchasing power across Wyoming’s 23 counties is stark. The highest purchasing power is recorded in Teton County at $123,080, whereas Niobrara County registers the lowest at $53,507.9. This spread of approximately $69,572 suggests that residents in different regions experience vastly different capacity to stretch their earnings. Even the second‑tier counties—Campbell at $104,043.6 and Weston at $95,624.3—remain well above the bottom tier, which includes Big Horn ($66,134.7), Albany ($65,407.3) and Niobrara. The magnitude of this disparity highlights how geographic location can influence economic well‑being independent of statewide averages. Among the three consumer categories, goods are the most above average. At 97.1, goods costs are only 2.9 points below the national index, whereas services (75.7) and rents (74.1) lag by 24.3 and 25.9 points respectively. This positioning indicates that while housing and services are significantly more affordable in Wyoming, the price of tangible goods remains relatively close to the national level. Consequently, households may find that a larger portion of their budget is absorbed by housing and services, with goods representing a smaller premium over the national baseline. For residents seeking maximum value for their household budget, Teton County emerges as the focal point of purchasing power. Despite its higher absolute costs, the county’s $123,080 purchasing power suggests that incomes there are sufficient to cover expenses with a comfortable margin. In contrast, counties such as Big Horn, Albany and Niobrara, while offering lower living costs, also exhibit reduced purchasing power, indicating tighter budget constraints. Thus, the most favorable environment for budget stretch appears in the high‑income, high‑purchasing‑power locales, where residents can maintain a higher standard of living without proportionally higher expense burdens.
